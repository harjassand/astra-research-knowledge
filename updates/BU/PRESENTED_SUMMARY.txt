# Primitive Genesis: action-compatible retention

The strongest construction developed is a **composition-sensitive retention invariant for positive stochastic representations**. It measures something that a static factorization does not: how much information a representation can preserve while repeatedly implementing transformations.

Its strongest consequence is the following sharp finite-horizon law:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Minimum classical states}
=
\Theta_{D,\delta,\varepsilon}\!\left(T^{D-1}\right)
}
\]

for an explicitly defined weak-measurement process on a \(D\)-dimensional quantum system. The same process has a static classical representation using exactly \(D^2\) states, while the quantum system implements every horizon using the same Hilbert-space dimension \(D\).

**This is not an established 9–10/10 foundational breakthrough.** The written proofs below and in the accompanying packet are substantive, but historical novelty and independent verification remain unestablished. The underlying representation transforms and quantization geometry are familiar mathematics; the candidate contribution is their precise operational connection and the resulting sharp sequential bounds.

## 1. The conceptual inadequacy

Suppose a family of observations has a positive representation

\[
p(y\mid x)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}p_i(x)h_i(y),
\]

where \(p_i(x)\) is a probability distribution and each \(h_i\) is a valid response.

This establishes that \(m\) classical states suffice **to reproduce an observation of \(x\)**. It does not establish that every transformation \(g:x\mapsto gx\) has a stochastic implementation on those same states.

That requires matrices \(P_g\) satisfying a much stronger condition: successive transformations must be implementable by successive positive updates,

\[
p_xP_{g_1}P_{g_2}\cdots P_{g_T},
\]

without access to an uncharged record of the previous inputs.

The distinction is not necessarily missing information about \(x\). A probability vector can encode \(x\) injectively. The obstruction is that the encoded states occupy only part of a simplex, whereas a stochastic matrix must remain positive on the **entire** simplex.

Astra’s N507 source investigates a related distinction between static positive frames and additional dynamic compatibility conditions. I used that as an obstruction comparison, not as a proved premise; its current status remains source-derived and unreviewed. 

The question pursued here was:

> Can the unavoidable loss caused by requiring positive, composable updates be measured directly—and then accumulated into a sharp theorem?

## 2. The primitive

Let a compact group \(G\) act orthogonally on a finite-dimensional real inner-product space \(V\), through a representation \(\pi(g)\).

For an \(m\)-state stochastic update \(P_g\), define the **action-compatible transfer**

\[
\boxed{
(\mathcal A_Pz)_i
=
\int_G
\pi(g)^{-1}
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}P_g(i,j)z_j\right)\,dg,
}
\]

where \(z=(z_1,\dots,z_m)\in V^m\), and \(dg\) is normalized Haar measure.

Averaging \(P_g\) alone discards its correlation with the action. This transfer retains a representation-valued component of that correlation.

Equip \(V^m\) with

\[
\|z\|_{\infty,2}=\max_i\|z_i\|.
\]

The relevant coefficient is

\[
\boxed{
\kappa_m(\pi)
=
\sup_{\|v\|=1}
\sup_{\|w_1\|=\cdots=\|w_m\|=1}
\int_G\max_j\langle w_j,\pi(g)v\rangle\,dg.
}
\]

It is an optimal quantization quantity for the group’s orbits. The proposed mathematical organization is to treat it as a **sequential retention coefficient**, rather than merely a static approximation error.

### The operational interpretation

Let

\[
X_t=\pi(g_t)\cdots\pi(g_1)x
\]

be the true evolving vector, and let \(S_t\) be the simulator’s internal state. Define

\[
b_i(t)=\mathbb E[X_t\mathbf 1_{\{S_t=i\}}],
\qquad
R_t=\sum_i\|b_i(t)\|.
\]

Equivalently,

\[
R_t=
\sum_i\Pr(S_t=i)
\left\|\mathbb E[X_t\mid S_t=i]\right\|.
\]

Thus \(R_t\) is the best average directional correlation obtainable by assigning one unit-vector estimate to each classical state.

### Theorem 1: retention under positive composition

For independent Haar-distributed controls,

\[
\boxed{
R_t\le \kappa_m(\pi)R_{t-1}.
}
\]

Consequently, for a unit initial vector,

\[
R_T\le \kappa_m(\pi)^T.
\]

This holds for arbitrary measurable, time-dependent transition matrices. It does not require reversibility, stationarity, affine preparation maps or diagonalizable transitions.

**Proof.** The Markov update rule gives

\[
b_j(t)
=
\sum_i\int_G
\pi(g)b_i(t-1)P_t(g)(i,j)\,dg.
\]

Fix \(i\), and choose unit vectors \(w_j\) aligned with the individual integrals. Then

\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_j
\left\|
\int_G\pi(g)b_i(t-1)P_t(g)(i,j)\,dg
\right\|
&=
\int_G\sum_jP_t(g)(i,j)
\langle w_j,\pi(g)b_i(t-1)\rangle\,dg\\
&\le
\kappa_m(\pi)\|b_i(t-1)\|.
\end{aligned}
\]

Apply the triangle inequality and sum over \(i\). ∎

The transfer operator is the dual of this conditional-mean evolution. In particular,

\[
\|\mathcal A_P\|\le\kappa_m(\pi).
\]

Independent successive controls produce the actual operator product

\[
\mathcal A_{P_1}\cdots\mathcal A_{P_T}.
\]

This is the essential composition property. State relabeling leaves the norm unchanged, convex mixing acts linearly on the transfer, and adding available states can only increase the retention coefficient.

## 3. A calibration: ordinary directions

For the standard action of \(SO(d)\) on \(\mathbb R^d\), every unit-vector orbit is the sphere. The coefficient becomes

\[
\eta_{m,d}
=
\max_{v_1,\dots,v_m\in S^{d-1}}
\int_{S^{d-1}}\max_j\langle u,v_j\rangle\,d\sigma(u).
\]

Expanding squared distance gives

\[
1-\eta_{m,d}
=
\frac12
\min_{v_1,\dots,v_m}
\mathbb E\min_j\|U-v_j\|^2.
\]

So this coefficient is a familiar spherical quantization object, not a newly invented geometric quantity. Quantization on manifolds has an established literature; the rate arguments used here are proved directly in the packet. [Numdam](https://www.numdam.org/item/COCV_2016__22_3_770_0/)

### An exact circle law

For \(d=2\),

\[
\boxed{
\eta_{m,2}
=
\frac{m}{\pi}\sin\frac{\pi}{m}.
}
\]

To prove this, arrange the code directions cyclically, with consecutive angular gaps \(\Delta_j\), summing to \(2\pi\). Integrating the nearest-direction cosine over each gap gives

\[
\eta
=
\frac1\pi\sum_j\sin(\Delta_j/2)
\le
\frac m\pi\sin(\pi/m),
\]

by concavity of sine on \([0,\pi]\). A regular polygon attains equality.

More generally,

\[
1-\eta_{m,d}
=
\Theta_d\!\left(m^{-2/(d-1)}\right).
\]

For the lower bound, \(m\) sufficiently small spherical caps cannot cover more than half the sphere. For the upper bound, a maximal separated set supplies a covering with matching cardinality.

The sequential coefficient is genuinely sharp here:

\[
\boxed{
\max R_T=\eta_{m,d}^{\,T}.
}
\]

For completeness, choose an optimal code \(v_j\) with nearest-direction cells \(C_j\). Let

\[
c_j=\int_{C_j}u\,d\sigma(u).
\]

Optimality implies \(c_j=a_jv_j\), \(a_j\ge0\), because

\[
\eta
=\sum_jv_j\cdot c_j
\le\sum_j\|c_j\|
\le\eta.
\]

From state \(i\), send the machine to the nearest codeword to \(gv_i\). If its conditional means are aligned with the code, they remain aligned, and their total norm is multiplied by \(\sum_ja_j=\eta\). Starting at one code direction proves equality at every horizon.

The construction therefore identifies an exact loss mechanism, not merely an upper-bound technique.

## 4. The strongest theorem: full quantum unitary control

The spherical argument extends further than a single-qubit example. The relevant geometry is then the geometry of **unitary conjugation orbits**.

Let

\[
n=D^2-1,
\qquad
r_0=\sqrt{\frac{D-1}{D}},
\]

and let \(H_0(D)\) denote the real inner-product space of traceless Hermitian \(D\times D\) matrices, with

\[
\langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}(AB).
\]

Represent a density matrix \(\rho\) by

\[
A_\rho=\frac{\rho-I/D}{r_0}.
\]

All these matrices lie in the unit Hilbert–Schmidt ball; pure-state preparations lie on its boundary.

Consider the probabilities

\[
\boxed{
p_*(+\mid A,U_1,\dots,U_t,B)
=
\frac12
\left[
1+\delta\operatorname{Tr}
\left(BV_tAV_t^*\right)
\right],
}
\]

where \(V_t=U_t\cdots U_1\), \(A=A_\rho\), and \(B\in H_0(D)\) has Hilbert–Schmidt norm at most one.

These are physical quantum probabilities: prepare \(\rho\), apply the unitaries, and measure

\[
E_B=\frac12(I+\delta B/r_0).
\]

For \(0<\delta\le1/n\), this is a valid binary effect.

### Theorem 2: sharp finite-horizon state complexity

Fix

\[
D\ge2,\qquad
0<\delta\le\frac1{D^2-1},
\qquad
0<\varepsilon<
\frac{\delta}{2(D^2-1)}.
\]

Require a classical simulator to approximate every preparation, every displayed terminal query, and every unitary word of length at most \(T\), with probability error at most \(\varepsilon\).

Assume its only persistent record of previous inputs is its \(m\)-valued internal state. Its stochastic transitions may otherwise be arbitrary measurable functions of time and the current gate.

Then

\[
\boxed{
m_*(T;D,\delta,\varepsilon)
=
\Theta_{D,\delta,\varepsilon}(T^{D-1}).
}
\]

Meanwhile, the static family has positive factorization size exactly \(D^2\).

For illustration:

| Quantum dimension | Static classical states | Sequential classical states |
|---|---:|---:|
| \(D=2\), one qubit | \(4\) | \(\Theta(T)\) |
| \(D=3\), a three-level system | \(9\) | \(\Theta(T^2)\) |
| \(D=4\), two qubits | \(16\) | \(\Theta(T^3)\) |

These are fixed-dimension asymptotics at the specified fixed visibility and accuracy. The classical **bit** count is

\[
\boxed{
\lceil\log_2m_*\rceil
=
(D-1)\log_2T+O_{D,\delta,\varepsilon}(1).
}
\]

It is not \(T^{D-1}\) bits.

### Proof, part A: the missing uniformity argument

Define

\[
\kappa_{m,D}
=
\sup_{\substack{A\in H_0(D)\\\|A\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=1}}
\sup_{\|B_j\|_{\mathrm{HS}}=1}
\int_{U(D)}
\max_j\operatorname{Tr}(B_jUAU^*)\,dU.
\]

Theorem 1 gives \(R_T\le\kappa_{m,D}^T\).

A tempting but invalid shortcut would be to quantize only pure states. Conditional means of pure states need not remain pure. The required estimate must hold for **every spectrum** of a normalized conditional mean.

The key bound is

\[
\boxed{
\Pr\!\left(\|UAU^*-B\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\le s\right)
\le C_Ds^{2(D-1)}
}
\]

uniformly over unit-norm traceless Hermitian \(A\), all centers \(B\), and \(0<s\le1\).

Here is its proof.

Write the eigenvalues of \(A\) in decreasing order. Since their sum is zero and the sum of their squares is one, their range is at least \(1/\sqrt D\). Therefore some consecutive gap is at least

\[
g_D=\frac1{\sqrt D(D-1)}.
\]

Let \(P\) be the projection onto the eigenspaces above that gap, of rank \(k\). For \(A'=WAW^*\) and \(P'=WPW^*\),

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|A-A'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2
&=
\sum_{i,j}(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)^2
|\langle e_i,We_j\rangle|^2\\
&\ge
g_D^2\|P-P'\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\end{aligned}
\]

The inequality retains the terms crossing the chosen spectral gap.

Thus a small ball in the matrix orbit forces a small ball in the complex Grassmannian of \(k\)-dimensional subspaces. That space has local real dimension

\[
2k(D-k)\ge2(D-1).
\]

To justify the volume estimate directly, subspaces near a fixed one are graphs of complex matrices \(Z\in\mathbb C^{(D-k)\times k}\), with projection

\[
Q(Z)=
\begin{bmatrix}I\\Z\end{bmatrix}
(I+Z^*Z)^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}I&Z^*\end{bmatrix}.
\]

At zero, its derivative sends \(H\) to

\[
\begin{bmatrix}0&H^*\\H&0\end{bmatrix},
\]

whose norm is \(\sqrt2\|H\|_{\mathrm{HS}}\). The chart is locally bi-Lipschitz, and invariant volume has a smooth positive density there. A radius-\(s\) ball consequently has measure at most a constant times \(s^{2k(D-k)}\).

An arbitrary ball center can be replaced by an orbit point inside the ball, doubling its radius. There are only finitely many possible \(k\), so the constants can be chosen uniformly. This proves the bound.

### Proof, part B: geometry forces per-step loss

For any \(m\) unit code matrices \(B_j\),

\[
1-\max_j\operatorname{Tr}(B_jUAU^*)
=
\frac12\min_j\|UAU^*-B_j\|_{\mathrm{HS}}^2.
\]

Choose

\[
s\asymp_Dm^{-1/[2(D-1)]}.
\]

The uniform small-ball bound and a union bound show that, with probability at least \(1/2\), the orbit point is farther than \(s\) from every code matrix. Therefore

\[
\boxed{
1-\kappa_{m,D}\ge c_Dm^{-1/(D-1)}.
}
\]

The exponent cannot be improved for this one-step coefficient. A normalized pure-state orbit is complex projective space of real dimension \(2(D-1)\); a radius-\(s\) net has \(O_D(s^{-2(D-1)})\) points and squared approximation error at most \(s^2\). Hence

\[
1-\kappa_{m,D}
=
\Theta_D(m^{-1/(D-1)}).
\]

Unlike the spherical case, I do **not** claim that \(\kappa_{m,D}^T\) is attained at every horizon. The matching simulation construction below is a separate argument.

### Proof, part C: an observable witness gives the lower bound

Choose fixed pure-state centers

\[
A=\frac{|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|-I/D}{r_0},
\qquad
B=\frac{|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|-I/D}{r_0},
\]

and define

\[
f=\operatorname{Tr}(BV_TAV_T^*).
\]

Under independent Haar controls,

\[
\mathbb E f=0,
\qquad
\mathbb E f^2=\frac1{D^2-1}.
\]

For an explicit calculation, set \(s=|\langle\phi,V_T\psi\rangle|^2\). A normalized complex Gaussian vector is uniform on the complex unit sphere, so \(s\) has density

\[
(D-1)(1-s)^{D-2},\qquad 0\le s\le1.
\]

Integration gives \(\mathbb Es=1/D\) and \(\mathbb Es^2=2/[D(D+1)]\), yielding the displayed variance.

For the terminal bit \(Y\), use the bounded score

\[
Z=(2Y-1)f.
\]

Its target expectation is \(\delta/(D^2-1)\). A classical simulator has

\[
|\mathbb EZ|\le R_T\le\kappa_{m,D}^T.
\]

Uniform probability error \(\varepsilon\) changes this score expectation by at most \(2\varepsilon\). Consequently,

\[
a:=\frac{\delta}{D^2-1}-2\varepsilon
\le
\kappa_{m,D}^T
\le
\exp\!\left(-c_DT\,m^{-1/(D-1)}\right).
\]

Since \(a>0\),

\[
m\ge
\left(\frac{c_DT}{\log(1/a)}\right)^{D-1}.
\]

This proves the lower bound using observable probabilities alone.

### Proof, part D: a matching positive simulator

Let \(K_D\) be the convex body of normalized density-matrix centers \(A_\rho\).

Choose pure vectors \(\phi_i\) forming a projective net with

\[
1-|\langle\psi,\phi_i\rangle|^2\le r^2
\]

for some \(i\), for every unit vector \(\psi\). There is such a net with

\[
N\le C_Dr^{-2(D-1)}.
\]

Set

\[
A_i=\frac{|\phi_i\rangle\langle\phi_i|-I/D}{r_0},
\qquad
L=\operatorname{conv}\{A_i\},
\qquad
\alpha=1-Dr^2.
\]

Then

\[
\boxed{\alpha K_D\subseteq L\subseteq K_D.}
\]

To prove the first inclusion, take any traceless Hermitian support direction \(B\). Its extreme eigenvalues satisfy

\[
\lambda_{\min}\ge-(D-1)\lambda_{\max}.
\]

Choose a net point close to a top eigenvector. Its expectation obeys

\[
\langle\phi_i,B\phi_i\rangle
\ge
\lambda_{\max}
-r^2(\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min})
\ge
(1-Dr^2)\lambda_{\max}.
\]

Thus every support function of \(L\) dominates that of \(\alpha K_D\), which proves inclusion by separation.

Now scale to the circumscribed polytope

\[
P=L/\alpha,
\qquad w_i=A_i/\alpha.
\]

For every unitary \(U\),

\[
\alpha Uw_iU^*=UA_iU^*\in K_D\subseteq P.
\]

Therefore \(\alpha Uw_iU^*\) has convex coefficients in the \(w_j\). Use these coefficients as a stochastic transition row.

Encode the initial \(A\) by barycentric coordinates in \(P\), and assign terminal responses

\[
h_B(i)=\frac12\bigl(1+\delta\operatorname{Tr}(Bw_i)\bigr).
\]

They lie in \([0,1]\) when \(\alpha\ge1/2\). After \(t\) steps, the expected vertex is exactly

\[
\alpha^tV_tAV_t^*.
\]

The probability error through \(T\) is at most

\[
\frac\delta2(1-\alpha^T)
\le
\frac{\delta DTr^2}{2}.
\]

Choose

\[
r^2=
\min\left\{\frac1{2D},\frac{2\varepsilon}{\delta DT}\right\}.
\]

The error is at most \(\varepsilon\), and

\[
N=O_{D,\delta,\varepsilon}(T^{D-1}).
\]

This proves the matching upper bound and completes Theorem 2. ∎

## 5. Why the static representation needs only \(D^2\) states

Identify \(H_0(D)\) with \(\mathbb R^n\), \(n=D^2-1\), and choose regular-simplex unit vectors \(e_1,\dots,e_{n+1}\). They satisfy

\[
\sum_ie_i=0,
\qquad
\sum_ie_ie_i^\top=\frac{n+1}{n}I.
\]

Define

\[
p_i(A)=\frac{1+e_i\cdot A}{n+1},
\qquad
h_B(i)=\frac{1+\delta n\,e_i\cdot B}{2}.
\]

They are valid probabilities, and multiplication gives

\[
\sum_ip_i(A)h_B(i)
=
\frac{1+\delta A\cdot B}{2}.
\]

So \(D^2=n+1\) classical states suffice.

For necessity, choose an orthonormal basis \(F_1,\dots,F_n\), preparations \(0,cF_1,\dots,cF_n\) with sufficiently small \(c>0\), and queries \(0,F_1,\dots,F_n\). These preparations correspond to positive density matrices. The resulting probability matrix has determinant

\[
\frac{(\delta c)^n}{2^{n+1}}\ne0.
\]

Its rank is \(n+1\), whereas a factorization through \(m\) states has rank at most \(m\). Thus the static minimum is exactly \(D^2\). ∎

The concrete separation is therefore:

> A positive representation can preserve all the information needed for every single weak observation, yet require an increasing number of states to preserve that information under repeated transformations.

## 6. Counterexamples and the boundary of the result

**Signed coordinates remove the obstruction.** For equilateral triangle directions \(e_i\), the planar encoding \(p_i(x)=(1+e_i\cdot x)/3\) supports exact rotations through

\[
P_R(i,j)=\frac{1+2e_j\cdot Re_i}{3}.
\]

These matrices compose correctly and have row sums one, but need not be positive. For \(R=-I\), one row is

\[
(-1/3,\;2/3,\;2/3).
\]

Thus three signed coordinates support lossless composition. The theorem is not an impossibility result for ordinary real-valued computation.

**Correlated external noise is not free memory.** Montina’s 2012 construction reproduces qubit unitary dynamics with four classical states and time-correlated noise. This is an important existing counterexample to any careless claim that “four classical states can never simulate a qubit”; it lies outside the charged Markov-memory contract used here. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.5138)

**Finite alphabets require a separate analysis.** For a general control distribution \(\nu\), the same argument gives the block profile

\[
R_T\le
\inf_{1\le k\le T}
\kappa_m(\nu^{*k})^{\lfloor T/k\rfloor}.
\]

But when \(\nu\) has at most \(m\) atoms, its one-step coefficient is exactly one: the code can contain every possible one-step image. Therefore the Haar bound cannot simply be reused for a fixed finite instruction set.

A finite **group** of controls admits a finite invariant polytope and a bounded-state exact weak-readout realization. A finite alphabet generating an infinite dense group is a different problem.

Finally, the upper construction does not hide its geometric compilation costs behind the state count: net construction, triangulation, barycentric evaluation, probability precision and sampling complexity are not bounded by the theorem.

## 7. Relationship to existing mathematics

The strongest reason not to label this a new foundation is that its components already have substantial predecessors.

Spherical quantization and manifold approximation are established. The transfer operator is a representation-valued transform. Neither is claimed as an invention of this session. [Numdam](https://www.numdam.org/item/COCV_2016__22_3_770_0/)

The connection between quantum dynamics and the dimension of classical Markov descriptions is also established research. Montina’s 2008 work gives a \(2D-2\) continuous-variable dimension bound under its hypotheses; the later analysis discusses the regularity and trajectory assumptions involved. The appearance of \(2(D-1)\) here is not, by itself, a new discovery. [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevA.77.022104?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The candidate contribution is narrower and precise: a **finite-state, finite-horizon, uniform approximation law**, proved through a contraction valid for all conditional means, with a matching positive construction and an exact static-factorization contrast.

A recent preprint by Yibin Wang studies sequential response capacity, phase controls and finite-memory classical comparisons. I retrieved its primary indexed abstract, but full-text retrieval failed, so a detailed comparison with its lemmas remains incomplete. No priority over that work is claimed. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02068)
