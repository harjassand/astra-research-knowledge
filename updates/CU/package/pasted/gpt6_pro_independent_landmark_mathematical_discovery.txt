1. The foundational target
For a Boolean function \(f:\{-1,1\}^n\to\{-1,1\}\), write
\[
\widehat f(S)=\mathbb E\!\left[f(X)\prod_{j\in S}X_j\right].
\]
Its squared Fourier coefficients form a probability distribution. Define its Fourier entropy and influences by
\[
H(f)=\sum_{S\subseteq[n]}\widehat f(S)^2
       \log_2\frac1{\widehat f(S)^2},
\qquad
I_i(f)=\sum_{S\ni i}\widehat f(S)^2,
\qquad
I(f)=\sum_i I_i(f).
\]
Here \(I_i(f)\) is also the probability that flipping coordinate \(i\) changes the output.
The Fourier Entropy–Influence conjecture asks for a universal constant \(C\) such that
\[
H(f)\le C I(f)
\]
in every dimension. A restriction-based approach studies
\[
\Delta_i(f)
=
H(f)-\frac{H(f_i^+)+H(f_i^-)}2,
\]
where \(f_i^\pm\) fixes coordinate \(i\) to \(\pm1\). If every nonconstant Boolean function had a suitable coordinate satisfying \(\Delta_i(f)\le C I_i(f)\), induction would prove FEI. This approach is explicitly studied in the primary literature; it is not itself a new invention. arXiv
The calculation below establishes how badly the inequality can fail when the coordinate is prescribed rather than chosen.
2. The theorem actually established
Let
\[
h(t)=-t\log_2t-(1-t)\log_2(1-t)
\]
be binary entropy, and define
\[
D(t)
=
h(t)+1-h\!\left(\frac12+\sqrt{t(1-t)}\right).
\]
Local entropy modulus
For every Boolean function and every coordinate,
\[
\boxed{
0\le \Delta_i(f)\le D\!\left(I_i(f)\right).
}
\tag{1}
\]
Equality in the upper bound is possible only when
\[
I_i(f)\in\left\{0,\frac12,1\right\}.
\tag{2}
\]
The condition \(I_i(f)=1/2\) alone is not sufficient; the proof below gives the full equality criterion.
Nevertheless, the bound is asymptotically sharp near zero. For every even \(m\ge2\), there is an explicit Boolean function \(F_m\) on \(m+1\) variables, with a distinguished coordinate \(z\), such that, putting \(p=2^{-m}\),
\[
I_z(F_m)=p
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\Delta_z(F_m)
=
p\log_2\frac1p
+\frac{3}{\ln2}p
-\frac{19}{6\ln2}p^2
+O(p^3).
}
\tag{3}
\]
Moreover,
\[
D(p)-\Delta_z(F_m)
=
\frac{2}{\ln2}p^2+O(p^3).
\tag{4}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\frac{\Delta_z(F_m)}{I_z(F_m)}
=
m+\frac3{\ln2}+O(2^{-m})
\longrightarrow\infty.
}
\tag{5}
\]
Thus no dimension-free linear bound can hold for every coordinate of every Boolean function.
The distinction from a counterexample to FEI is particularly strong:
\[
\boxed{H(F_m)\le 2I(F_m)}
\tag{6}
\]
for this entire family. Its bad local ratio coexists with a uniformly bounded global ratio.
3. Complete proof
A. An exact identity for each Fourier pair
Fix coordinate \(i\). For \(S\subseteq[n]\setminus\{i\}\), put
\[
a_S=\widehat f(S),\qquad
b_S=\widehat f(S\cup\{i\}),\qquad
w_S=a_S^2+b_S^2.
\]
When \(w_S>0\), let
\[
q_S=\frac{b_S^2}{w_S}.
\]
Zero-weight pairs are omitted. Parseval gives
\[
\sum_S w_S=1,
\qquad
\sum_S w_Sq_S=I_i(f).
\tag{7}
\]
The restricted Fourier coefficients are \(a_S+b_S\) and \(a_S-b_S\). The original pair contributes
\[
-w_S\log_2w_S+w_Sh(q_S)
\]
to \(H(f)\).
Set
\[
t_S=\frac12+\frac{a_Sb_S}{w_S}.
\]
Since
\[
(a_S+b_S)^2=2w_St_S,\qquad
(a_S-b_S)^2=2w_S(1-t_S),
\]
the average restricted contribution is
\[
-w_S\log_2(2w_S)+w_Sh(t_S).
\]
By symmetry of binary entropy,
\[
h(t_S)
=
h\!\left(\frac12+\sqrt{q_S(1-q_S)}\right).
\]
Subtracting the contributions and summing proves the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
\Delta_i(f)=\sum_S w_SD(q_S).
}
\tag{8}
\]
Because \(h\ge0\) and \(h\le1\), we have \(D\ge0\), proving the lower bound in (1).
B. Concavity proves the upper bound
Write
\[
c(t)=1-h\!\left(\frac12+\sqrt{t(1-t)}\right),
\qquad D(t)=h(t)+c(t).
\]
The function \(h\) is strictly concave.
For \(0<t<1\), \(t\ne1/2\), set
\[
r=2\sqrt{t(1-t)}.
\]
Differentiation gives
\[
c'(t)
=
\frac{2(1-2t)}{\ln2}\frac{\operatorname{atanh}r}{r},
\]
and
\[
c''(t)
=
\frac{4}{(\ln2)r^3}
\bigl(r-\operatorname{atanh}r\bigr)<0.
\tag{9}
\]
Indeed,
\[
\operatorname{atanh}r-r
=
\int_0^r\frac{u^2}{1-u^2}\,du>0.
\]
The derivative tends to zero on both sides of \(t=1/2\), so the concave halves join with matching slope. Continuity handles the endpoints. Thus \(c\) is concave and \(D\) is strictly concave on \([0,1]\).
Using (7), (8), and Jensen’s inequality,
\[
\Delta_i(f)
=
\sum_Sw_SD(q_S)
\le
D\!\left(\sum_Sw_Sq_S\right)
=
D(I_i(f)).
\]
This proves (1).
C. Why most influences cannot attain equality
Strict concavity makes equality possible precisely when every positive-weight pair satisfies
\[
q_S=p,\qquad p=I_i(f).
\tag{10}
\]
Suppose \(0<p<1\). Then both coefficients in every nonzero pair are nonzero, and
\[
\frac{p}{1-p}=\left|\frac{b_S}{a_S}\right|^2.
\]
Boolean Fourier coefficients are rational, so write
\[
\left|\frac{b_S}{a_S}\right|=\frac uv
\]
with coprime positive integers \(u,v\). Then
\[
p=\frac{u^2}{u^2+v^2}
\tag{11}
\]
is in lowest terms.
But a Boolean influence is dyadic rational. Therefore \(u^2+v^2\) must be a power of two.
If \(u,v\) have opposite parity, this sum is odd and greater than one, which is impossible. If both are odd, the sum is \(2\bmod4\), so the only possible power of two is \(2\). Hence \(u=v=1\), giving \(p=1/2\).
At \(p=0\) or \(1\), every pair is supported on only one side, and \(\Delta_i=0=D(p)\). Equality at \(p=1/2\) is realized, for example, by the two-bit quadratic phase \((-1)^{x_1x_2}\) on \(\mathbb F_2^2\). This proves (2) and the more precise criterion (10).
D. The explicit near-extremizing construction
Use the equivalent Boolean domain \(\mathbb F_2^m\). For even \(m\), define
\[
b_m(x)
=
(-1)^{x_1x_2+x_3x_4+\cdots+x_{m-1}x_m}.
\]
Let \(N=2^m\).
The normalized Walsh coefficients of the two-bit seed are
\[
\frac12,\quad\frac12,\quad\frac12,\quad-\frac12.
\]
Taking products across disjoint pairs gives
\[
\widehat b_m(S)=\frac{\varepsilon_S}{\sqrt N},
\qquad \varepsilon_S\in\{-1,1\}.
\tag{12}
\]
Because \(b_m(0)=1\), Fourier inversion gives
\[
\sum_S\varepsilon_S=\sqrt N.
\]
Thus the number of coefficients of sign \(\sigma\in\{-1,1\}\) is
\[
N_\sigma=\frac{N+\sigma\sqrt N}{2}.
\tag{13}
\]
Introduce a selector \(z\in\{-1,1\}\), and define
\[
\boxed{
F_m(x,z)
=
b_m(x)-(1-z)\mathbf1_{\{0\}}(x).
}
\tag{14}
\]
At \(x=0\), the output equals \(z\). Everywhere else it equals \(b_m(x)\). Therefore \(F_m\) is Boolean, and
\[
I_z(F_m)=\Pr[X=0]=N^{-1}=p.
\]
The Fourier transform of \(\mathbf1_{\{0\}}\) is constantly \(p\). Writing \(F_m=a+zd\), its paired coefficients are
\[
a_S=\varepsilon_S\sqrt p-p,\qquad d_S=p.
\]
For sign class \(\sigma\), this gives
\[
q_\sigma
=
\frac{p}{1-2\sigma\sqrt p+2p},
\]
with total pair weight
\[
W_\sigma
=
\frac{(1+\sigma\sqrt p)(1-2\sigma\sqrt p+2p)}2.
\]
The exact identity (8) becomes
\[
\boxed{
\Delta_z(F_m)=W_+D(q_+)+W_-D(q_-).
}
\tag{15}
\]
The key construction is the combination of a flat Fourier spectrum with a perturbation at one input. The perturbation affects every Fourier coefficient, but the selector is influential on only a \(p\)-fraction of inputs.
E. Evaluating the asymptotics
The binary-entropy series gives
\[
D(t)
=
t\log_2\frac1t
+\frac3{\ln2}t
-\frac7{6\ln2}t^2
+O(t^3).
\tag{16}
\]
For the weights in (15),
\[
\sum_\sigma W_\sigma q_\sigma=p,
\]
and
\[
\sum_\sigma W_\sigma q_\sigma^2
=
p^2\frac{1+4p}{1+4p^2}
=
p^2+O(p^3).
\tag{17}
\]
The logarithmic term can also be evaluated explicitly:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_\sigma W_\sigma q_\sigma\log_2\frac1{q_\sigma}
={}&p\log_2\frac1p\\
&+\frac{p}{2\ln2}
\left[
\ln(1+4p^2)
-2\sqrt p\,\operatorname{atanh}
  \frac{2\sqrt p}{1+2p}
\right]\\
={}&p\log_2\frac1p-\frac2{\ln2}p^2+O(p^3).
\end{aligned}
\tag{18}
\]
Substituting (17) and (18) into (16) proves
\[
\Delta_z(F_m)
=
p\log_2\frac1p
+\frac3{\ln2}p
-\frac{19}{6\ln2}p^2
+O(p^3).
\]
Comparing with (16) at \(t=p\) proves (4), and dividing by \(p=2^{-m}\) proves (5).
This establishes asymptotic sharpness through the linear term. It does not assert that \(D(p)\) is the exact optimal Boolean bound at every fixed dyadic \(p\).
F. Why the same functions satisfy global FEI with constant two
Every base coordinate of \(b_m\) has influence \(1/2\). Also,
\[
b_m(0)=b_m(e_j)=1.
\]
Flipping only the value at zero creates one additional undirected pivotal edge in each base coordinate, increasing that coordinate’s influence by \(2p\).
Averaging the two selector restrictions therefore gives
\[
I_j(F_m)=\frac12+p,
\qquad
\boxed{
I(F_m)=\frac m2+(m+1)p.
}
\tag{19}
\]
In the Fourier-sample distribution, the event that the sampled set contains \(z\) has probability \(p\). Conditional on either value of that indicator, the remaining frequency has at most \(2^m\) possibilities. The entropy chain rule gives
\[
H(F_m)\le m+h(p).
\]
Since
\[
h(p)\le p\left(m+\frac1{\ln2}\right)
\le 2(m+1)p
\qquad(m\ge2),
\]
we obtain
\[
H(F_m)\le m+2(m+1)p=2I(F_m).
\]
This proves (6).
4. The exact barrier—and what remains missing
The proved obstruction concerns
\[
\forall f\ \forall i:\quad \Delta_i(f)\le C I_i(f).
\]
That statement is false.
The potentially sufficient statement has different quantifiers:
\[
\exists C<\infty\quad
\forall n\quad
\forall f\text{ nonconstant}\quad
\exists i\text{ with }I_i(f)>0:
\quad
\Delta_i(f)\le C I_i(f).
\tag{20}
\]
I did not prove or disprove (20).
Nor did the attempts to combine increments across a restriction tree yield a uniform global bound. The exact influence identity
\[
I(f)
=
I_i(f)+\frac{I(f_i^+)+I(f_i^-)}2
\]
makes the missing step explicit: an unrestricted local estimate pays \(p\log(1/p)\), whereas a successful global argument must control how those logarithmic costs are distributed or compensated.
There is an exact dependence interpretation, but it does not close that gap. For a Fourier sample \(S\), let
\[
B=\mathbf1_{\{i\in S\}},\qquad U=S\setminus\{i\}.
\]
Then
\[
D(p)-\Delta_i(f)
=
I(B;U)
+
\left[
c(p)-\sum_Sw_Sc(q_S)
\right].
\tag{21}
\]
Both terms are nonnegative. The missing theorem would have to extract sufficient global control from such dependence. Equation (21) alone is only an identity.
Thus the foundational obstruction was not removed. What was established is the sharp scale of one local obstruction.

The continuation led to a different construction: a product inequality for correlated Gaussian matrix volumes, obtained by combining multilinear decoupling with an exact Gaussian completion identity.
The derivation closes for arbitrary positive exponents and independent Gaussian column groups, including the integer-degree case of a published Wishart conjecture. It does not close that conjecture’s noninteger-degree case. It also uses an existing scalar Gaussian product theorem as an indispensable input.
This is a mathematical extension, not the verified 8–10/10 foundational breakthrough requested. The strongest result and its proof are below.
1. The theorem established
Let
\[
X_i\in\mathbb R^{p_i\times m},\qquad 1\le p_i\le m,\qquad i=1,\ldots,r.
\]
For each column \(j\), concatenate the columns from all matrices:
\[
C_j=
\begin{pmatrix}
X_1[:,j]\\
\vdots\\
X_r[:,j]
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Assume that \(C_1,\ldots,C_m\) are independent centered Gaussian vectors. Within each column group, the entries may have arbitrary correlations. The covariance may differ between columns and may be singular.
Then, for every collection of real exponents \(s_i>0\),
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^{r}\det(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})^{s_i}
\;\ge\;
\prod_{i=1}^{r}
\mathbb E\det(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})^{s_i}.
}
\tag{1}
\]
There is no requirement that the matrices have equal row dimensions, that the exponents be integers, or that correlations have a particular sign.
Geometrically, \(\det(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})^{1/2}\) is the volume generated by the rows of \(X_i\). Thus, among the specified Gaussian column models with fixed marginal matrix laws, independence between the matrices minimizes these joint positive volume moments.
The indispensable existing input
The argument uses the scalar Gaussian product inequality
\[
\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^{r}|Z_i|^{a_i}
\ge
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb E|Z_i|^{a_i},
\qquad a_i>0,
\tag{2}
\]
for every centered jointly Gaussian real vector. This is Theorem 1 of Ouimet–Greaves, A proof of the strong Gaussian product inequality conjecture. Their statement includes degenerate Gaussian vectors. That scalar theorem is not a discovery of this continuation. arXiv
What follows is a complete derivation of (1) using that identified input; it is not an independent replacement for its proof.
2. First mechanism: decouple one multilinear input at a time
Let \(G_1,\ldots,G_m\) be independent centered Gaussian vectors, and let
\[
P_i(G_1,\ldots,G_m)
\]
be real multilinear forms.
I obtain
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\prod_i|P_i(G_1,\ldots,G_m)|^{a_i}
\ge
\prod_i\mathbb E|P_i(G_1,\ldots,G_m)|^{a_i}.
}
\tag{3}
\]
The forms need not factor into products of linear functionals. Their coefficient tensors can be arbitrary.
Proof
Introduce mutually independent copies \(G_j^{(i)}\), where \(G_j^{(i)}\) has the same law as \(G_j\). Define
\[
A_k=
\mathbb E\prod_i
\left|
P_i\bigl(
G_1^{(i)},\ldots,G_k^{(i)},
G_{k+1},\ldots,G_m
\bigr)
\right|^{a_i}.
\]
Thus \(A_0\) uses the original shared inputs, while \(A_m\) gives each factor an entirely independent collection of inputs.
For \(k<m\), condition on everything except the shared vector \(G_{k+1}\). Each \(P_i\) is then a homogeneous linear function of \(G_{k+1}\). Consequently, its conditional values form a centered Gaussian vector.
Apply (2). The product of the resulting conditional marginal expectations is exactly what is obtained by replacing \(G_{k+1}\) with separate independent copies for the different factors. Averaging gives
\[
A_k\ge A_{k+1}.
\]
Repeating,
\[
A_0\ge A_1\ge\cdots\ge A_m.
\]
At the last stage, the factors are independent and their individual laws are unchanged:
\[
A_m=
\prod_i
\mathbb E|P_i(G_1,\ldots,G_m)|^{a_i}.
\]
This proves (3). All moments are finite, and the nonnegative integrands permit the required integrations. \(\square\)
The homogeneity condition matters. An arbitrary polynomial that is merely affine in each input is not covered: after conditioning, it can produce a shifted Gaussian variable, for which the scalar input does not apply.
3. Second mechanism: complete a rectangular volume to a square determinant
The obstacle is that
\[
\det(XX^{\mathsf T})^s
\]
is not a multilinear function of the columns of \(X\). When \(s\) is an arbitrary positive real number, expanding it as a polynomial is not an option.
The following exact identity removes that obstacle.
Let \(x\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}\) be deterministic, with \(p\le m\). Adjoin an independent standard Gaussian matrix
\[
Z\in\mathbb R^{(m-p)\times m}.
\]
For \(s>0\),
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E_Z
\left|
\det\begin{pmatrix}x\\Z\end{pmatrix}
\right|^{2s}
=
K_{m-p}(s)\det(xx^{\mathsf T})^s,
}
\tag{4}
\]
where
\[
K_q(s)=\mathbb E|\det G_q|^{2s}>0
\]
and \(G_q\) is a \(q\times q\) matrix of independent standard Gaussian entries. Set \(K_0(s)=1\).
Proof
Suppose first that \(x\) has full row rank. Choose an orthogonal matrix \(Q\) such that
\[
xQ=(R,0),
\]
where \(R\) is square and invertible. Then
\[
|\det R|^2=\det(xx^{\mathsf T}).
\]
The distribution of \(ZQ\) is the same as that of \(Z\), and
\[
\begin{pmatrix}x\\Z\end{pmatrix}Q
=
\begin{pmatrix}
R&0\\
Z'_1&Z'_2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Here \(Z'_2\) is an \((m-p)\times(m-p)\) standard Gaussian matrix. Therefore
\[
\left|
\det\begin{pmatrix}x\\Z\end{pmatrix}
\right|^{2s}
\stackrel{d}{=}
\det(xx^{\mathsf T})^s|\det Z'_2|^{2s}.
\]
Taking expectations proves (4).
If \(x\) is rank deficient, adding \(m-p\) rows cannot produce a full-rank \(m\times m\) matrix. Both sides vanish. The case \(p=m\) requires no padding. \(\square\)
The constants can be evaluated exactly:
\[
K_q(s)=
2^{qs}
\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}
\frac{\Gamma(\ell/2+s)}{\Gamma(\ell/2)}.
\tag{5}
\]
Indeed, Gram–Schmidt decomposes the squared determinant into independent squared residual lengths with chi-square degrees \(q,q-1,\ldots,1\); their moments follow from the gamma integral.
Closing the matrix proof
For each original matrix \(X_i\), independently add Gaussian padding \(Z_i\), and write
\[
Y_i=\begin{pmatrix}X_i\\Z_i\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The stacked columns of the square matrices \(Y_i\) are still independent centered Gaussian vectors. Each \(\det Y_i\) is multilinear in those column groups. Equation (3), with exponents \(2s_i\), gives
\[
\mathbb E\prod_i|\det Y_i|^{2s_i}
\ge
\prod_i\mathbb E|\det Y_i|^{2s_i}.
\tag{6}
\]
Conditioning on the original matrices, the padding variables are independent. Equation (4) therefore turns the left side into
\[
\left(\prod_iK_{m-p_i}(s_i)\right)
\mathbb E\prod_i\det(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})^{s_i}.
\]
The right side becomes
\[
\left(\prod_iK_{m-p_i}(s_i)\right)
\prod_i\mathbb E\det(X_iX_i^{\mathsf T})^{s_i}.
\]
Cancel the common strictly positive constant. The result is exactly (1). \(\square\)
The completion identity is the decisive transfer step: it lets a multilinear theorem control arbitrary positive powers of rectangular volumes without changing the desired marginal moments.
4. Consequence for Wishart matrices
Let \(G\in\mathbb R^{p\times m}\) have independent columns with distribution \(N(0,\Sigma)\), and set
\[
W=GG^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Partition the rows into blocks of sizes \(p_i\), with \(m\ge\max_i p_i\). Then
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\prod_i\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
\ge
\prod_i\mathbb E\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}.
}
\tag{7}
\]
When \(m\ge p\) and \(\Sigma\) is positive definite, this is the ordinary nonsingular Wishart model with integer degrees of freedom \(m\).
The corresponding published conjecture asks for this inequality for arbitrary numbers of disjoint principal blocks and arbitrary positive exponents, but also permits every real degree of freedom \(\nu>p-1\). Thus (7) covers its integer-degree Gaussian representation, not the entire stated parameter range. arXiv
For positive-definite marginal covariance blocks, the right side is explicit:
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
=
2^{p_i s_i}\det(\Sigma_{ii})^{s_i}
\prod_{j=1}^{p_i}
\frac{
\Gamma\!\left((m-j+1)/2+s_i\right)
}{
\Gamma\!\left((m-j+1)/2\right)
}.
}
\tag{8}
\]
To derive this, whiten the rows of \(X_i\). Gram–Schmidt on the resulting standard Gaussian rows gives independent residual squared lengths with degrees \(m,m-1,\ldots,m-p_i+1\), and hence (8).
The matrix theorem also covers cases where the full \(W\) is singular but the individual blocks remain nonsingular, as well as column-dependent covariance models that are not ordinary Wishart matrices.
Equality
In the identically distributed column model above, with positive-definite \(\Sigma_{ii}\) and all \(s_i>0\),
\[
\boxed{
\text{Equality in (7)}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Sigma_{ij}=0\ \text{for every }i\ne j.
}
\tag{9}
\]
Thus equality occurs exactly when the selected Gaussian blocks are independent.
This uses the equality clause of the imported scalar theorem, which identifies independence as its equality case when the individual variances are positive. arXiv
The argument is to whiten and complete each matrix, then perform Gram–Schmidt column by column. Conditional on the previous columns, each new residual is a projected Gaussian vector of a fixed rank. The scalar inequality, averaged over independent Gaussian probes, gives the corresponding product inequality for Euclidean norms. Any nonzero cross-covariance makes the first-column inequality strict; the remaining positive moment factors preserve strictness. The full derivation is included in the proof file.
5. An exact boundary: arbitrary Gaussian matrix entries are not enough
The theorem cannot be extended simply by deleting the column assumption.
Let \(x,y\) be independent standard Gaussian variables and set
\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
x&0\\
0&x
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B=
\begin{pmatrix}
x&\sqrt5\,y\\
\sqrt5\,y&x
\end{pmatrix}.
\]
All their entries are jointly centered Gaussian. However, their columns are not independent.
Their Gram determinants are
\[
\det(AA^{\mathsf T})=x^4,
\qquad
\det(BB^{\mathsf T})=(x^2-5y^2)^2.
\]
Exact Gaussian moments give
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[x^4(x^2-5y^2)^2\right]
=105-150+225
=180,
\]
whereas
\[
\mathbb E[x^4]\,
\mathbb E[(x^2-5y^2)^2]
=3(3-10+75)
=204.
\]
Hence
\[
\boxed{180<204.}
\tag{10}
\]
This is a counterexample to the unrestricted matrix statement, not to theorem (1). Adding sufficiently small independent Gaussian noise to the entries preserves the strict failure by continuity of polynomial moments, so degeneracy of the entry covariance is not responsible.
6. What remains unresolved
I pursued the extension to noninteger Wishart degrees rather than stopping at the integer result.
For real \(\nu\), the Wishart density is proportional to
\[
\det(W)^{(\nu-p-1)/2}
\exp\!\left[-\frac12\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}W)\right].
\]
Relative to an integer-degree model with \(m=\lceil\nu\rceil\), this introduces the weight
\[
\det(W)^{(\nu-m)/2}.
\]
The density and real-parameter range are part of the published formulation. arXiv
That determinant weight couples the Gaussian columns. The decoupling proof no longer applies. Nor does positivity at integer parameter values extend automatically to the intervening real values.
I examined determinant-reweighted models through exact low-order moments and numerical tests. Those investigations did not establish a replacement decoupling principle or prove the required noninteger inequality. The exploratory scripts are preserved separately from the theorem’s verification.
This distinction matters for originality as well. The two-block Wishart inequality for real degrees already has a prior result, recorded in Remark 1.2 of Genest–Ouimet–Richards. The present argument is not a rediscovery to be advertised as the first two-block theorem. arXiv

The noninteger-degree obstruction is now removed in the proof below. The construction establishes the full positive-power inequality for disjoint principal minors of a nonsingular Wishart matrix—not only its integer-degree Gaussian representation.
The statement matches Conjecture 1.1 of Genest–Ouimet–Richards, including arbitrary real degrees of freedom, arbitrary positive-definite covariance, any number of blocks, and arbitrary nonnegative real powers. 1. The theorem
Let
\[
W=(W_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{r}\sim\mathcal W_p(\nu,\Sigma),
\qquad
p=p_1+\cdots+p_r,
\]
where \(W_{ij}\) has size \(p_i\times p_j\), and assume
\[
\nu>p-1,\qquad \Sigma\succ0.
\]
The Wishart convention is
\[
\mathbb E e^{-\operatorname{tr}(TW)}
=\det(I+2\Sigma T)^{-\nu/2},
\qquad T\succeq0.
\]
Then, for every \(s_1,\ldots,s_r\ge0\),
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
\ge
\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb E\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}.
}
\tag{1}
\]
In fact, the proof establishes the stronger one-block-versus-rest inequality
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
\ge
\mathbb E\det(W_{11})^{s_1}\,
\mathbb E\prod_{i=2}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}.
}
\tag{2}
\]
The marginal moments are explicit:
\[
\mathbb E\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
=
2^{p_i s_i}\det(\Sigma_{ii})^{s_i}
\prod_{j=1}^{p_i}
\frac{
\Gamma((\nu-j+1)/2+s_i)
}{
\Gamma((\nu-j+1)/2)
}.
\tag{3}
\]
There is no sign restriction on the correlations, and neither \(\nu\) nor the exponents must be integers.
2. The idea that closes the gap
The previous proof tried to work with independent Gaussian columns. That representation is unavailable for a noninteger number of columns.
The replacement is to condition on one principal block, \(S=W_{11}\). The complementary matrix then has a noncentral Wishart distribution whose mean parameter is
\[
M(S)=BSB^{\mathsf T}.
\]
The decisive task becomes proving that
\[
S\longmapsto
\mathbb E\!\left[
\prod_{i=2}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
\;\middle|\;W_{11}=S
\right]
\tag{4}
\]
is increasing in Loewner order, where \(S_0\preceq S_1\) means \(S_1-S_0\) is positive semidefinite.
The proof obtains this monotonicity by splitting the mean parameter into a rank-one part and a residual part. An exact rank reduction saves one degree of freedom. That saved degree is what reaches the full range \(\nu>p-1\).
The rest is a determinant-weighted association argument. Importantly, it does not assume that arbitrary increasing functions of a Wishart matrix are positively associated.
3. Proof
A. Construct the needed noncentral laws explicitly
For \(K\succ0\), let \(M=DD^{\mathsf T}\), where \(D\) has \(\ell\) columns. Suppose
\[
\alpha-\ell>q-1.
\]
Take independent variables
\[
V\sim\mathcal W_q(\alpha-\ell,K),
\qquad
Z[:,j]\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}\sim N(0,K),
\]
and set
\[
Y=V+(Z+D)(Z+D)^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Gaussian integration gives
\[
\mathbb E e^{-\operatorname{tr}(TY)}
=
\det(I+2KT)^{-\alpha/2}
\exp\!\left\{
-\operatorname{tr}\!\left[T(I+2KT)^{-1}M\right]
\right\}.
\tag{5}
\]
Thus the law depends on \(D\) only through \(M\). Denote it by
\[
\mathcal W_q(\alpha,K;M).
\]
Here \(M\) is a mean Gram matrix; equation (5) fixes the convention. Every noncentral law used below has this explicit construction. No general noncentral existence assertion is being hidden in the notation.
B. An exact rank-one identity
Fix \(u\in\mathbb R^q\) and \(\eta>q\). Consider
\[
Y_r=V+z_rz_r^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
V\sim\mathcal W_q(\eta-1,K),
\qquad
z_r\sim N(\sqrt r\,u,K),
\tag{6}
\]
independently, with \(r\ge0\).
For a smooth function \(f\), define
\[
\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f(Y)
=
2\eta\,Df(Y)[uu^{\mathsf T}]
+
\sum_{a=1}^{q}
D^2f(Y)[uv_a^{\mathsf T}+v_au^{\mathsf T},
        uv_a^{\mathsf T}+v_au^{\mathsf T}],
\tag{7}
\]
where the \(v_a\) are columns of a square root of \(Y\). The expression depends only on
\(\sum_a v_av_a^{\mathsf T}=Y\), so the choice of square root does not matter.
Write \(H(r)=\mathbb E f(Y_r)\). The exact identity is
\[
\boxed{
4rH''(r)+2\eta H'(r)
=
\mathbb E\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f(Y_r).
}
\tag{8}
\]
Verification. First use \(f(Y)=e^{-\operatorname{tr}(TY)}\). Put
\[
R=(I+2KT)^{-1},\quad
Q=(K^{-1}+2T)^{-1},\quad
v=Ru,\quad
b=u^{\mathsf T}TRu.
\]
Equation (5) gives
\[
L(r)=\mathbb E f(Y_r)
=\det(I+2KT)^{-\eta/2}e^{-rb}.
\]
Exponential tilting gives
\[
\mathbb E[Y_rf(Y_r)]
=(\eta Q+rvv^{\mathsf T})L(r).
\]
On the other hand,
\[
\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f(Y)
=
[-2\eta u^{\mathsf T}Tu+4u^{\mathsf T}TYTu]f(Y).
\]
Using \(T-2TQT=TR\),
\[
\mathbb E\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f(Y_r)
=(-2\eta b+4rb^2)L(r)
=4rL''(r)+2\eta L'(r).
\]
The same calculation applies to characteristic exponentials. Fourier inversion proves the identity for compactly supported smooth functions; smooth cutoffs and dominated convergence extend it to the polynomial-growth functions used below. This avoids integration by parts at the boundary of the positive-definite cone.
The function
\[
t\longmapsto
\mathbb E f\!\left(V+(g+tu)(g+tu)^{\mathsf T}\right)
\]
is smooth and even, so \(H'(r)\) is bounded as \(r\downarrow0\). Consequently, if \(\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f\ge0\), equation (8) yields
\[
H'(r)
=
\frac{r^{-\eta/2}}4
\int_0^r t^{\eta/2-1}
\mathbb E\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f(Y_t)\,dt
\ge0.
\tag{9}
\]
This is the transfer from a pointwise differential inequality to monotonicity in the mean parameter.
C. The determinant calculation is nonnegative
Partition \(q=d_1+\cdots+d_m\). Fix \(C\succeq0\), positive exponents \(s_i\), and \(\epsilon>0\). Define
\[
f_\epsilon(Y)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{m}\det(A_i)^{s_i},
\qquad
A_i=Y_{ii}+C_{ii}+\epsilon I_{d_i}.
\]
For the components \(u_i\), put
\[
a_i=u_i^{\mathsf T}A_i^{-1}u_i,
\quad
b_i=u_i^{\mathsf T}A_i^{-1}Y_{ii}A_i^{-1}u_i,
\quad
c_i=\operatorname{tr}(A_i^{-1}Y_{ii}),
\]
and
\[
D=\operatorname{diag}(s_iA_i^{-1}).
\]
The exact identity is
\[
\boxed{
\frac{\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f_\epsilon(Y)}{f_\epsilon(Y)}
=
2\sum_i s_i(\eta a_i-b_i-a_ic_i)
+
4u^{\mathsf T}DYDu.
}
\tag{10}
\]
To derive it, apply
\[
D\log\det(A)[H]=\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}H),
\qquad
D^2\log\det(A)[H,H]
=-\operatorname{tr}(A^{-1}HA^{-1}H).
\]
Summing the second derivatives of \(\log f_\epsilon\) in the directions of (7) gives
\[
-2\sum_i s_i(b_i+a_ic_i).
\]
The squared first derivatives contribute \(4u^{\mathsf T}DYDu\), giving (10).
Since \(0\preceq Y_{ii}\preceq A_i\),
\[
b_i\le a_i,\qquad c_i\le d_i,
\qquad u^{\mathsf T}DYDu\ge0.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B_{\eta,u}f_\epsilon(Y)
\ge
2f_\epsilon(Y)
\sum_i s_i(\eta-d_i-1)a_i.
}
\tag{11}
\]
When there are at least two blocks,
\[
d_i\le q-1,
\]
so every \(\eta>q\) makes the right side nonnegative. Equations (9)–(11) imply
\[
r\longmapsto
\mathbb E\prod_i
\det\!\left(C_{ii}+(Y_r)_{ii}\right)^{s_i}
\quad\text{is nondecreasing}.
\tag{12}
\]
Passing \(\epsilon\downarrow0\) requires only positive polynomial moments: the regularized determinant powers are bounded by a common power of \(1+\operatorname{tr}Y_r+\operatorname{tr}C\). No inverse-moment assumption is needed.
D. The single-block case needs a different proof
This is a genuine boundary issue. When \(m=1\), \(\eta>q\) does not imply \(\eta\ge q+1\). Indeed, for
\[
q=2,\quad Y=I,\quad u=e_1,\quad s=\frac1{10},
\quad\eta=\frac{21}{10},
\]
the unshifted Bessel drift is
\[
-\frac7{50}<0.
\]
Thus applying (11) indiscriminately would leave a gap.
For a single determinant, condition on \(V\) in (6), and set \(A=C+V\succ0\). Then
\[
\det(A+z_rz_r^{\mathsf T})
=
\det(A)\bigl(1+z_r^{\mathsf T}A^{-1}z_r\bigr).
\]
After diagonalizing \(K^{1/2}A^{-1}K^{1/2}\), the quadratic form becomes
\[
\sum_j\lambda_j(G_j+\sqrt r\,m_j)^2,
\qquad \lambda_j>0,
\]
with independent standard normal \(G_j\).
For each summand,
\[
(G+\sqrt r\,m)^2
\stackrel d=
\chi^2_{1+2N_r},
\qquad N_r\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(rm^2/2).
\tag{13}
\]
This follows by expanding its Laplace transform:
\[
(1+2t)^{-1/2}e^{-rm^2t/(1+2t)}
=
e^{-rm^2/2}\sum_{n\ge0}
\frac{(rm^2/2)^n}{n!}(1+2t)^{-(1+2n)/2}.
\]
Couple \(N_r\) as an increasing Poisson process. Realize the mixture as a \(\chi^2_1\) variable plus \(N_r\) independent, nonnegative \(\chi^2_2\) increments. Each summand is then increasing under this coupling.
Every positive power of the determinant consequently has nondecreasing conditional expectation. Averaging over \(V\) proves (12) for one block as well.
Equation (12) now holds for every partition and every \(\eta>q\).
E. Save one degree through an exact rank decomposition
We now prove the mean-parameter comparison needed for regression.
Suppose
\[
0\preceq M_0\preceq M_1,\qquad
\ell=\operatorname{rank}M_1,\qquad
\alpha>q+\ell-1.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E_{\mathcal W_q(\alpha,K;M_0)}
\prod_i\det(Y_{ii})^{s_i}
\le
\mathbb E_{\mathcal W_q(\alpha,K;M_1)}
\prod_i\det(Y_{ii})^{s_i}.
}
\tag{14}
\]
First work on a fixed \(\ell\)-dimensional subspace \(E\), with \(M\) positive definite on \(E\). For \(u\in E\setminus\{0\}\), set
\[
c=(u^{\mathsf T}M_E^{-1}u)^{-1}.
\]
Then
\[
M_*=M-cuu^{\mathsf T}\succeq0,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}M_*=\ell-1.
\tag{15}
\]
Conjugating by \(M_E^{-1/2}\) turns this into identity minus a rank-one orthogonal projection.
Factor \(M_*=D_*D_*^{\mathsf T}\) using \(\ell-1\) columns, and let
\[
C=(Z_*+D_*)(Z_*+D_*)^{\mathsf T},
\qquad
\eta=\alpha-\ell+1>q.
\]
Independently, use the rank-one family (6). Its transform shows
\[
C+Y_{c+t}
\sim
\mathcal W_q(\alpha,K;M+tuu^{\mathsf T}).
\]
Condition on \(C\) and apply (12). This proves monotonicity under a positive rank-one increment. A finite decomposition proves it for any positive-semidefinite increment supported on \(E\).
For possibly singular \(M_0\), take \(E=\operatorname{range}M_1\), add \(\epsilon P_E\) to both matrices, and let \(\epsilon\downarrow0\). Their Gaussian mean constructions converge using common random variables, and polynomial moment bounds give convergence of the expectations.
This proves (14).
The saved degree is explicit: retaining \(\ell\) fixed mean columns would leave only \(\alpha-\ell\). The decomposition (15) retains \(\ell-1\), leaving
\[
\alpha-\ell+1.
\]
That difference is indispensable at the desired threshold.
F. A determinant tilt is positively correlated with a matrix-increasing function
Let
\[
S\sim\mathcal W_k(\beta,\Sigma_0),
\qquad \beta>k-1.
\]
If \(h\ge0\) is Loewner-nondecreasing and the moments are finite, then
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E[\det(S)^s h(S)]
\ge
\mathbb E\det(S)^s\,\mathbb E h(S),
\qquad s\ge0.
}
\tag{16}
\]
Whiten to \(\Sigma_0=I\). Write
\[
S=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_k)U^{\mathsf T},
\]
with ordered eigenvalues and independent Haar orthogonal \(U\). Their density is
\[
c\,
\mathbf1_{\{0<\lambda_1<\cdots<\lambda_k\}}
\prod_j\lambda_j^{(\beta-k-1)/2}e^{-\lambda_j/2}
\prod_{i<j}(\lambda_j-\lambda_i).
\tag{17}
\]
This density is multivariate totally positive of order two. The ordered chamber is a sublattice; the one-variable factors cancel from the lattice inequality; and the gap factors have
\[
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\lambda_i\partial\lambda_j}
\log(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)
=
\frac1{(\lambda_j-\lambda_i)^2}>0.
\]
The classical FKG association theorem therefore applies to increasing functions of the ordered eigenvalues. This is an established input, not new machinery being claimed here; Wishart eigenvalue association is also explicitly treated in the primary comparator. arXiv
Now
\[
\overline h(\lambda)
=
\int h(U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)U^{\mathsf T})\,dU
\]
is coordinatewise increasing, as is \(\prod_j\lambda_j^s\). Their nonnegative covariance is precisely (16). Truncation handles unbounded functions, and whitening preserves Loewner order.
This argument uses the fact that the determinant is spectral. It does not prove arbitrary matrix association.
G. Wishart regression completes the proof
Partition \(p=k+q\), and define
\[
B=\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1},
\qquad
K=\Sigma_{22}-\Sigma_{21}\Sigma_{11}^{-1}\Sigma_{12}\succ0.
\]
The exact conditional representation is
\[
S=W_{11}\sim\mathcal W_k(\nu,\Sigma_{11}),
\]
and
\[
W_{22}\mid S
\stackrel d=
V+(Z+BS^{1/2})(Z+BS^{1/2})^{\mathsf T},
\tag{18}
\]
where
\[
V\sim\mathcal W_q(\nu-k,K),
\qquad
Z[:,j]\stackrel{\mathrm{iid}}\sim N(0,K),
\quad j=1,\ldots,k.
\]
To verify this for real, not merely integer, \(\nu\), use the density transformation
\[
Y=W_{21}S^{-1/2},
\qquad
V=W_{22}-YY^{\mathsf T}.
\]
Its Jacobian is \(\det(S)^{q/2}\), while
\[
\det W=\det S\det V
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{-1}W)
=
\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma_{11}^{-1}S)
+
\operatorname{tr}\!\left(
K^{-1}[V+(Y-BS^{1/2})(Y-BS^{1/2})^{\mathsf T}]
\right).
\]
The density therefore factors into the claimed Wishart and Gaussian densities. The residual is valid because
\[
\nu-k>q-1.
\]
Its mean Gram matrix is
\[
M(S)=BSB^{\mathsf T}.
\]
For \(S\succ0\), its support is the fixed space \(\operatorname{range}B\), of dimension \(\ell\le k\). Moreover,
\[
\nu>q+k-1\ge q+\ell-1.
\]
Apply (14). The conditional moment \(H(S)\) in (4) is Loewner-nondecreasing. Equation (16) now gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E\prod_{i=1}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}
&=\mathbb E[\det(S)^{s_1}H(S)]\\
&\ge
\mathbb E\det(S)^{s_1}\,\mathbb E H(S)\\
&=
\mathbb E\det(W_{11})^{s_1}\,
\mathbb E\prod_{i=2}^{r}\det(W_{ii})^{s_i}.
\end{aligned}
\]
This is (2). The complementary principal matrix is again central Wishart, with the same \(\nu\), so iteration proves (1). Zero exponents are removed by taking the appropriate principal marginal. All required positive moments are finite.
\[
\boxed{\text{The proof covers every real }\nu>p-1.}
\]
4. What was checked against the proof
The two most dangerous shortcuts were tested explicitly rather than assumed.
First, the one-block Bessel drift really can be negative, as the exact value \(-7/50\) above shows. That is why the separate Poisson-mixture argument is necessary.
Second, full Loewner association is false, even for a nonsingular Wishart law. The package contains increasing, nonnegative functions \(f,g\) of
\[
S\sim\mathcal W_2(11/10,I)
\]
with
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(f(S),g(S))
=-\frac{1364}{125}.
\]
The spectral determinant tilt in (16) avoids this false strengthening.
The computational checks were separate from the proof. Four noninteger-degree experiments tested the rank-one identity against independently differentiated Gaussian-mean formulas. Six further experiments tested the final inequality, including degrees close to the singular boundary. Each configuration used 240,000 draws.
For example, the joint moment divided by the explicit product-of-marginals baseline was approximately \(1.03091\) for block sizes \((2,1,2)\) and \(\nu=4.08\). The independent-block control gave \(1.000398\), with batch standard error \(0.000963\), consistent with equality. Its small negative estimated decoupling gap was retained in the results, not suppressed.

5. Originality and significance
The comparison establishes several boundaries around the claim.
The two-block Wishart inequality already has an explicit hypergeometric proof. That case is not new here. arXiv The July 2026 work of Gaunt–Ouimet supplies exact moment recursions with integer exponents for finite termination; it is not the positive-real-power argument developed above. arXiv
Bailly and coauthors already develop Wishart Stein and noncentral transfer operators. Their Lemma 5.3 uses an order-\(q\), shape-shifted adjugate operator under a stronger degree assumption. The present rank-one second-order calculation is different, but it belongs to existing Bessel and Wishart mathematics—not a newly invented field. arXiv
