## Result: easy optimisation can conceal uncomputable convergence rates

I derived an explicit construction that embeds arbitrary computation into **ordinary gradient descent on one fixed, strongly convex loss whose condition number can be made arbitrarily close to one**.

The stronger consequence is not merely that a shrinking coordinate can encode a computation:

> **Approximating the actual asymptotic loss-decay exponent to fixed additive accuracy is undecidable—even when the limit exists, takes one of two fixed integer values, and the objective has a known minimizer and a globally contracting gradient-descent map.**

There is also a finite-time consequence: **predicting logarithmic loss progress to constant accuracy is EXPTIME-hard when the iteration horizon is supplied in binary.** A corresponding fixed-threshold decision problem is EXPTIME-complete.

These are impossibility results for **rate prediction**, not hardness of minimising the objective. The minimizer is simply zero. The construction and reductions are supplied below and in the proof note; independent proof review and historical novelty remain unresolved.

## 1. The precise theorem

Fix any rational \(0<\varepsilon<1\). The construction produces one fixed objective
\[
f(z)=\frac{\mu}{2}\|z\|^{2}
+\sum_{j=1}^{N}a_j\max\{0,b_j^\top z\}^{2},
\]
where \(\mu>0\), \(a_j\ge0\), and all coefficients are rational.

This is positive quadratic regularisation plus a finite nonnegative sum of squared ReLU terms—not an arbitrary computational oracle disguised as a loss.

The objective is globally continuously differentiable with Lipschitz gradient. It is \((1-\varepsilon)\)-strongly convex and \((1+\varepsilon)\)-smooth, giving the condition-number bound
\[
\kappa(f)\le \frac{1+\varepsilon}{1-\varepsilon}.
\]
Thus the bound approaches one as \(\varepsilon\) approaches zero.

Its unique minimizer and minimum are
\[
z_*=0,\qquad f(z_*)=0.
\]

Ordinary full-batch gradient descent, with the fixed learning rate \(1\), has update
\[
G(z)=z-\nabla f(z)
\]
and satisfies
\[
\|G(x)-G(y)\|\le\varepsilon\|x-y\|.
\]
Every trajectory therefore converges rapidly to the known minimizer.

Nevertheless, there is a polynomial-time encoding \(E\) of finite programs and inputs into rational starting points such that
\[
\Lambda(E(w))
:=
\lim_{t\to\infty}
-\frac1t\log_2
\frac{f(G^tE(w))}{f(E(w))}
\]
exists and equals
\[
\boxed{
\Lambda(E(w))
=
\begin{cases}
2k-2,&\text{the encoded computation accepts},\\[2mm]
2k,&\text{it never accepts},
\end{cases}}
\]
for one fixed integer \(k\).

The objective, dimension, coefficients, learning rate and two possible answers are all fixed. **Only the rational initial point varies.**

An algorithm approximating \(\Lambda\) within \(1/3\) would distinguish the two cases. A program that accepts precisely when another program halts then turns that algorithm into a halting decider, contradicting the classical undecidability result. [University of Virginia Computer Science](https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/Turing_Paper_1936.pdf)

The central separation is therefore
\[
\boxed{
\begin{gathered}
\text{Known minimizer, near-unit conditioning,}\\
\text{and globally contractive gradient descent}
\end{gathered}
\quad\not\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{computable instance-specific asymptotic rate}.}
\]

Here “rate” means the asymptotic average logarithmic decay above. It does not mean that the one-step ratio \(f(z_{t+1})/f(z_t)\) necessarily has a limit.

## 2. The mechanism: an exact gradient-exchange construction

The main mathematical obstacle is that an arbitrary computational update is not generally a gradient. Its derivatives need not satisfy the symmetry constraints imposed by a scalar potential.

The construction resolves that obstacle explicitly.

### A scalar potential that computes in one direction and copies in the other

Write \(\sigma(a)=\max\{0,a\}\), and define
\[
p(a,b)=\frac14\left[
\sigma(a+b)^2-\sigma(a-b)^2
-\sigma(b)^2+\sigma(-b)^2
\right].
\]

Direct differentiation gives the exact identities
\[
\nabla p(a,0)=(0,\sigma(a)),
\qquad
\nabla p(0,b)=(b/2,0).
\]

Also,
\[
p(a,0)=p(0,b)=0.
\]

Thus the same scalar function supplies a nonlinear forward transfer and a linear reverse transfer. There is no stop-gradient operation or independently prescribed “gradient” vector field.

### Completing the reverse transfer without changing the computation

Start with any rational recurrent ReLU system
\[
u_{t+1}=R(u_t):=\sigma(Wu_t),
\qquad u_0\ge0,
\]
where \(W\in\mathbb Q^{m\times m}\).

Define
\[
D=
\begin{bmatrix}
W\\-I
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
C=
\begin{bmatrix}
I&W-2I
\end{bmatrix}.
\]
Then
\[
CD=2I.
\]

The extended map
\[
A(u)=C\sigma(Du)
=\sigma(Wu)+(W-2I)\sigma(-u)
\]
agrees with the desired computation whenever \(u\ge0\), because the extra term vanishes there.

But the extension satisfies
\[
A(u)-A(-u)=2u.
\]

That is the useful operation: **modify the map outside the valid computational region to supply the reciprocal structure required by an actual gradient, while leaving the intended computation unchanged.**

Let \(d_j^\top\) denote row \(j\) of \(D\), and set
\[
H(u,v)=
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{2m}
C_{ij}\,p(d_j^\top u,v_i).
\]
The scalar identities now give
\[
\boxed{
\nabla H(u,0)=(0,A(u)),
\qquad
\nabla H(0,v)=(v,0).}
\]

The first transfer performs the computation. The second copies its output back into the original register.

### Making the objective strongly convex

An explicit global bound is
\[
\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla H)\le K,
\]
where one may take
\[
K=
\max\left\{
1,\,
\sum_{i,j}|C_{ij}|
\bigl(\|d_j\|^2+2\bigr)
\right\}.
\]

Choose a dyadic rational
\[
\rho=2^{-k},
\qquad
\rho K\le\varepsilon,
\]
and define
\[
f(z)=\frac12\|z\|^2-\rho H(z).
\]

The Lipschitz bound immediately supplies the strong-convexity and smoothness bounds:
\[
f\text{ is }(1-\varepsilon)\text{-strongly convex},
\qquad
\operatorname{Lip}(\nabla f)\le1+\varepsilon.
\]

Ordinary gradient descent performs the exact cancellation
\[
z-\nabla f(z)=\rho\nabla H(z).
\]

Consequently, starting from \(z_0=(u_0,0)\),
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
z_{2t}&=\bigl(\rho^{2t}R^t(u_0),\,0\bigr),\\
z_{2t+1}&=\bigl(0,\,\rho^{2t+1}R^{t+1}(u_0)\bigr).
\end{aligned}}
\]

**Two genuine gradient-descent updates simulate one recurrent-network update**, with a fully specified geometric rescaling.

Moreover, every constructed iterate lies on one of the two coordinate subspaces, where \(H\) vanishes. Hence
\[
\boxed{f(z_t)=\frac12\|z_t\|^2}
\]
throughout the simulated trajectory.

The loss values on the trajectory are exactly quadratic, while its transverse gradients perform the computation.

### The signed terms can be removed

The intermediate expression contains negative squared ReLU terms. They are not necessary in the final loss.

For \(a>0\),
\[
-a\sigma(\ell)^2=a\sigma(-\ell)^2-a\ell^2.
\]

Apply this identity to every negative term. The negative contributions become a quadratic correction. The explicit bound above ensures that the remaining quadratic matrix is still positive definite.

A rational \(LDL^\top\) decomposition then expresses that quadratic as positive rational multiples of squares of rational linear forms. Finally,
\[
\ell^2=\sigma(\ell)^2+\sigma(-\ell)^2.
\]

This yields the nonnegative squared-ReLU representation stated in the theorem. The whole matrix-to-objective construction has polynomial description and rational bit complexity.

## 3. Pushing beyond a shrinking-coordinate encoding

A construction that merely asks whether an exponentially small coordinate is positive would leave the operational meaning of the output questionable. I therefore made the computation change **the actual exponential decay of the loss**.

The bridge has three parts.

### Encode finite programs without hiding exponential input costs

A Turing-machine tape can be represented by two finite stacks. For an alphabet of size \(a\), assign odd digits \(d_j=2j-1\) and choose \(B=4a\). Encode a stack as
\[
x=\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}d_{w_r}B^{-r}.
\]

A stack of \(\ell\) symbols requires \(O(\ell)\) bits—not an exponentially long initial number.

The possible top symbols occupy separated rational intervals. Push and pop are affine on their respective branches:
\[
\operatorname{push}_d(x)=\frac{d+x}{B},
\qquad
\operatorname{pop}_d(x)=Bx-d.
\]

Continuous piecewise-linear plateau functions select the appropriate symbol interval. These functions have finite ReLU representations. Adding a scale coordinate homogenises the affine operations without computing a division by that scale.

The proof note gives the branch-selection formulas and both-stack transition rules explicitly.

### Convert acceptance into persistent geometric growth

Construct a fixed finite-depth homogeneous ReLU network \(F\) implementing one transition of a fixed universal interpreter.

Before acceptance, its scale coordinate remains one, and its encoded configurations stay in a fixed bounded set.

Upon acceptance, it resets to a canonical accepting vector \(q_*\) and thereafter obeys
\[
F(sq_*)=\Gamma s q_*.
\]

Let the feedforward depth be \(\ell\). Choose
\[
\Gamma=4^\ell.
\]

There is no circular choice here: the circuit architecture is fixed first, with \(\Gamma\) treated as a coefficient parameter. Changing its numerical value does not change the depth.

A block-cyclic pipeline converts the successive layers into one fixed recurrent matrix \(W\). With a suitable finite encoding \(P\),
\[
R^\ell P(q)=P(Fq),
\qquad R(u)=\sigma(Wu).
\]

Therefore
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|R^tP(q_0)\|^{1/t}
=
\begin{cases}
4,&\text{acceptance occurs},\\
1,&\text{acceptance never occurs}.
\end{cases}
\]

### Transfer the growth distinction into loss decay

The gradient-exchange construction inserts one recurrent update every two gradient steps. Consequently,
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|z_t\|^{1/t}
=
\begin{cases}
2\rho,&\text{acceptance occurs},\\
\rho,&\text{acceptance never occurs}.
\end{cases}
\]

Both cases still contract rapidly toward zero.

Since \(f(z_t)=\|z_t\|^2/2\) and \(\rho=2^{-k}\), the two loss exponents are exactly
\[
2k-2
\quad\text{and}\quad
2k.
\]

This produces a **fixed gap in the requested numerical output**, rather than requiring an increasingly accurate sign measurement of a vanishing coordinate.

It does not eliminate the growing precision needed to follow the computation internally. That cost is accounted for below.

## 4. A finite-horizon impossibility, not only an infinite-time one

Define
\[
\Lambda_T(z_0)
=
-\frac1T\log_2
\frac{f(G^Tz_0)}{f(z_0)}.
\]

For the same fixed objective, consider the decision problem
\[
\boxed{
f(G^TE(w))
\ge
2^{-(2k-1)T}f(E(w)),
}
\]
where \(w\) and the binary representation of \(T\) are the inputs.

The supplied reduction shows that this problem is **EXPTIME-complete**. Approximating \(\Lambda_T(E(w))\) within \(1/4\) is EXPTIME-hard.

The argument preserves a constant numerical gap.

At an aligned horizon \(T=2\ell n\), a computation that never accepts satisfies
\[
\Lambda_T=2k+O(1/T).
\]

If acceptance occurs at machine time \(h\), then
\[
\Lambda_T
=
2k-2(1-h/n)+O(1/T).
\]

Take any exponential-time decision procedure with an exponential running-time bound \(H\), and choose \(n\ge4(H+1)\). Its horizon still has only polynomially many binary digits.

After fixed additional padding, rejecting inputs give
\[
\Lambda_T\ge2k-\frac18,
\]
while accepting inputs give
\[
\Lambda_T\le2k-\frac{11}{8}.
\]

An approximation with error \(1/4\) distinguishes them.

Membership in EXPTIME follows by exact simulation: at step \(t\), each coordinate requires only \(O(|w|+t)\) bits for this fixed instance. Simulating through \(T\) therefore takes
\[
T\,\operatorname{poly}(|w|+T)
\]
bit operations, which is exponential in the actual input length \(|w|+\log T\).

The exclusion of a polynomial-time predictor is unconditional. It uses \(\mathrm P\ne\mathrm{EXPTIME}\), for which the note includes a short diagonal proof—not an assumption such as \(P\ne NP\).

Importantly, the decision formulation requires **only rational arithmetic**. No logarithm oracle is needed.

## 5. Resource accounting and the decisive boundaries

The result depends on the computational interface being stated accurately.

| Resource or operation | What the construction requires |
|---|---|
| Input acquisition | An ordinary finite program and input, encoded into \(O(n)\) rational bits |
| Objective preprocessing | One fixed finite compilation, independent of the subsequently supplied program |
| Update rule | Ordinary full-batch \(z-\nabla f(z)\), with no changing objective or gradient override |
| Bit memory at step \(t\) | \(O(n+t)\) per coordinate for the fixed instance |
| Exact simulation through \(T\) | \(T\,\operatorname{poly}(n+T)\) bit operations |
| Prediction output | A fixed-threshold decision or fixed additive accuracy for logarithmic loss decay |
| Verification supplied | General mathematical derivation plus executable exact checks of the compiler and a small example |

Several limitations are essential, not incidental.

### Absolute-error state prediction is easy

Global contraction gives
\[
\|G^Tz_0\|\le\varepsilon^T\|z_0\|.
\]

For any requested absolute tolerance \(\delta\), sufficiently late iterates can therefore be replaced by zero. Earlier iterates require only a number of simulation steps polynomial in the input size and \(\log(1/\delta)\).

Thus:

> **The theorem does not establish hardness of predicting the parameters or loss to fixed absolute error. It establishes hardness of predicting logarithmic decay.**

The distinction is part of the result. The same objective permits easy absolute-error prediction and hard rate prediction.

### Finding the minimizer is trivial

The exact answer is zero. The theorem supplies no new optimisation lower bound for obtaining a low-loss point.

What fails is inferring the **true input-specific convergence rate** from an objective whose minimizer, conditioning and global convergence bound are all already known.

### Fixed-dimensional state is not fixed memory

The number of coordinates is fixed, but their exact rational descriptions grow with computation time.

There is no claim that constant-precision floating-point hardware can simulate indefinitely, nor that an infinite computation is being performed with finite bit resources.

### The regularity and learning rate are specific

The objective is \(C^{1,1}\): continuously differentiable with globally Lipschitz gradient. It is not asserted to be \(C^2\), analytic or polynomial.

The unit learning rate is stable because
\[
1<\frac{2}{1+\varepsilon}.
\]
It need not satisfy the more conservative restriction \(1\le1/L\). The result does not automatically extend to every smaller learning rate, continuous gradient flow, or persistent numerical noise.

These restrictions prevent an unjustified escalation from the proved construction to all well-conditioned optimisation.

## 7. Prior work and the significance that is justified

Computational complexity of gradient-descent trajectories is not itself a new subject. Chatziafratis, Roughgarden and Wang established that **online gradient descent can encode arbitrary polynomial-space computations**, including simple learning settings. A claim that this investigation discovered computational universality of gradient descent in general would therefore be wrong. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01280)

Welper’s examined neural-training universality construction explicitly uses **stop-gradient operations** and a **conditional-gradient update on a simplex**. Those differ materially from differentiating one actual scalar objective and applying ordinary full-batch Euclidean gradient descent, as done here. They do not, by themselves, establish priority for the present theorem. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2007.13664v1)

The specific combination derived here is:

**an explicit gradient-exchange construction; a single rational strongly convex squared-ReLU loss; conditioning arbitrarily close to one; globally contractive ordinary gradient descent; fixed-gap undecidability of its actual loss-decay exponent; and an unconditional finite-horizon prediction barrier.**

The reusable mathematical mechanism is the extension outside the valid computational region that restores the reciprocal structure required by a scalar potential. The consequential result is the separation between **easy optimisation** and **hard prediction of its actual convergence rate**.

This goes beyond a representation counterexample: it supplies explicit reductions for numerical prediction tasks with fixed output accuracy. It does not supply a faster general-purpose solver, establish robustness at fixed physical precision, or demonstrate a practical advantage for learning systems.

**The construction and reductions are the research result reached here. Historical novelty, independent correctness and the mission’s transformative significance have not yet been established; I am not presenting this as completion of the broader mission.**
