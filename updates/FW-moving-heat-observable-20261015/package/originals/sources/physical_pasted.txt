Research outcome: transporting crystal defects without accumulating additional net plastic strain
The strongest candidate I found is a proposed operation for moving an existing dislocation loop through a crystal while restoring its size and net signed slip after each cycle.
The intervention would combine mechanical loading with optical control of the relative mobility of the loop’s two ends. The intended output is retained relocation of a defect, not faster deformation, altered image contrast, or a larger plastic strain.
I will call this closed-span dislocation transport.
This investigation produced a conditional mechanism, an exact transport criterion, a sharp limitation, and a same-platform experimental test. It did not establish the indispensable material response, historical novelty, or a technological advantage. The proposal merits one narrowly targeted experimental investigation—not promotion to a demonstrated breakthrough.
The central finding is:
Changing dislocation speed is insufficient. A closed expansion–contraction cycle can translate a loop only if the relative responses of its two ends change between the outward and return strokes. If one end remains pinned, the proposed operation fails.

The selected opportunity is different: use an experimentally observed difference in how dislocations respond to light to control where an existing defect ends up.
The potential technological target is selective, post-growth relocation of threading defects away from a device’s active region, under a constrained strain and damage budget. The attraction is not that defects have never been moved or removed. It is the possibility of acquiring an additional control over their trajectories without continually increasing the film’s net plastic deformation.
2. The experimental anchor—and the missing response
Montenegro and colleagues’ 2026 study investigates optoelectronic control of dislocation glide in ZnS/GaP. Its systematic optical experiment uses 20 nm ZnS films, annealing at 350°C, and 365 nm illumination at 0.07–0.67 mW/cm². Imaging occurs after cooling. The measurements resolve light-sensitive glide associated with Zn-rich dislocations. arXiv
A crucial geometrical detail appears in the accompanying analysis: an interface misfit segment belongs to a surface half-loop with two threading arms sharing a Burgers vector—one charged mixed-character arm and one nominally neutral screw arm. The study measures misfit-segment length changes, not independently controlled transport of both endpoints. Pinning is substantial. arXiv
That suggests a specific question:
Can illumination change the mobility ratio of the two arms of the same loop, while both remain reversibly movable?
This is not answered by comparing Zn-rich and S-rich dislocation populations. Nor does “electrically neutral” establish that a screw arm is unaffected by illumination.
The unacquired response is therefore precise:
\[
\boxed{
\text{Reproducible, light-dependent relative endpoint mobility,
with accessible outward and inward glide on the same loop.}
}
\]
No favourable mobility ratio, reverse-glide stress, switching time or lifetime has been assigned to this material in the calculations below.
3. The proposed physical operation
Consider a loop with an interface segment and two arms connecting it to the surface.
During one stroke, mechanical loading expands the loop. Suppose one arm moves farther than the other. Switch the optical condition, changing their relative mobility, and then contract the loop back to its original span.
The contraction need not retrace the expansion. The loop can return to its original size at a displaced location.
The proposed sequence is:
1. Expand under optical condition D, to a measured span.
2. Change optical condition, while holding the shape or explicitly accounting for switching motion.
3. Contract under condition L, restoring the original span and shape.
4. Return the specimen to its prescribed service state and measure the retained position.
Mechanical loading supplies the forces. Optical excitation is intended to alter how those forces are distributed into endpoint motion through carrier-dependent glide response. The retained output would be translation of the same identified defect.
This is a geometric locomotion principle, not a claim of new generic mathematics. Related shape-and-response cycles have longstanding precedents, including variable-geometry microswimmers. The possible novelty here is the specific physical realization and useful endpoint in a crystal, which the search did not establish as either previously demonstrated or historically new. arXiv
Why two ends of one loop matter
An initial alternative was to switch motion between two orthogonal dislocation families. That is not a strong enough distinction: conventional multiaxial stress must be allowed to compete, and family-specific speed alone does not supply a useful retained transformation.
The same-loop proposal instead asks whether a cycle can change position without retaining additional loop expansion.
Even then, it must compete with stress gradients, stress-dependent mobility, thermal cycles and other accessible controls. The calculation below does not prove that every conventional mechanical protocol is incapable of the same endpoint.
4. Conditional derivation: when translation occurs
Let the two endpoint coordinates be \(x_C<x_N\), with
\[
\ell=x_N-x_C,\qquad
c=\frac{x_N+x_C}{2}.
\]
Here \(\ell\) is the span and \(c\) the centre. The subscripts label the charged arm and the other arm; their left–right ordering is a convention.
Assume a planar loop with reproducible shape, an approximately translation-invariant environment, and equal-and-opposite generalized endpoint forces. Let \(F\) be positive when it drives expansion. Under a minimal overdamped model,
\[
\dot x_C=-m_C F,\qquad
\dot x_N=m_N F,
\]
where the nonnegative endpoint mobilities may depend on optical condition.
Then
\[
\dot\ell=(m_C+m_N)F,
\]
and
\[
\frac{dc}{d\ell}
=
\frac{m_N-m_C}{2(m_N+m_C)}
=:A.
\]
For expansion by \(a>0\) in condition D and contraction by the same amount in condition L,
\[
\boxed{\Delta c=a(A_D-A_L).}
\]
Equivalently,
\[
\boxed{
\Delta c=
a\,
\frac{
m_N^D m_C^L-m_C^D m_N^L
}{
(m_N^D+m_C^D)(m_N^L+m_C^L)
}.
}
\]
This gives three immediate conclusions.
A common speed change does nothing. Multiplying both endpoint mobilities by the same factor changes the time required but not the closed-cycle displacement.
A permanently pinned endpoint prevents this operation. The other endpoint must return to its original position to close the span.
Interchanging the optical conditions assigned to expansion and contraction reverses the predicted displacement, provided the same reversible response branches remain available.
These are conditional results of the stated model, not measured properties of ZnS.
The experiment can measure the relevant quantity directly
For reproducible span-dependent responses,
\[
\Delta c=
\int_{\ell_0}^{\ell_1}
\left[A_D(\ell)-A_L(\ell)\right]\,d\ell.
\]
This is useful because \(A=dc/d\ell\) can be obtained from endpoint trajectories. The first test does not require reconstructing every atomistic activation barrier.
However, the trajectory measurement must include the actual force dependence, optical history and shape changes. If the response depends strongly on position, pinning history, bowing or cross-slip, a two-coordinate model is insufficient. Those effects cannot be removed by fitting an “effective mobility” after observing the desired result.
A sharp limitation: strong switching alone is not enough
Suppose, conditionally, that the second arm has unchanged mobility \(m>0\), while the charged arm switches between \(p>q>0\). Then
\[
\frac{|\Delta c|}{a}
=
\frac{m(p-q)}{(m+p)(m+q)}.
\]
Differentiation shows that this is largest at
\[
m=\sqrt{pq}.
\]
Writing \(R=p/q\),
\[
\boxed{
\frac{|\Delta c|}{a}
\leq
\frac{\sqrt R-1}{\sqrt R+1}.
}
\]
Thus, even a large charged-arm mobility contrast is ineffective when the other arm is much slower or much faster.
For illustration, a hypothetical contrast \(R=10\) permits at most approximately \(0.519\) span-excursions of displacement per cycle in this model. This is not a measured ZnS contrast or a material-performance prediction.
I checked the formula against independent endpoint integration, the fixed-condition and common-scaling null cases, phase reversal, and span-dependent integration. These checks validate the implementation of the conditional model—not its physical assumptions.
5. The decisive failure must be tested before downstream design
The first experiment should not be a defect-clearing machine. It should determine whether the required response exists at all.
Both endpoints must be movable
The proposed operation fails if the nominally neutral arm cannot move over the accessible stress and temperature range. A dramatic light response at the charged arm would not rescue it.
The proper measurement is therefore the pair of endpoint trajectories, including arrested cases, rather than the longest or fastest-moving interface segments alone.
Contraction must be physically accessible
The return stroke is not supplied by switching the light off.
A useful loading window must permit contraction through a measured change in mechanical driving force, possibly allowing line tension to dominate. It must do so before fracture, new dislocation nucleation, irreversible pinning or a change of slip system.
A nominal critical thickness or an equilibrium strain estimate does not establish that window. Neither does mobility measured in a different, more relaxed specimen.
Failure to obtain reversible contraction on the same platform is an early rejection criterion, not a reason to proceed to a more elaborate architecture.
Optical switching must reset a response, not accumulate damage
The optical states must be reproducible after repeated switching. Persistent charge trapping, changing core structure, photochemical damage or evolving pinning could make intensity an inadequate description of the state.
That does not make transport impossible, but it changes the control problem. Any retained hidden state has to be measured or included in the protocol.
6. A realistic discriminator
A proposed apparatus would combine an existing loop-imaging platform with calibrated reversible substrate loading and controlled illumination. The initial experiment should remain close to the published material and temperature regime rather than import room-temperature mobility from another system.
First acquire the response. Identify the two endpoints and the same loop under suitable diffraction conditions. Measure expansion and contraction in each optical condition, with calibrated specimen strain and temperature. Establish switching and settling behaviour before choosing the transport sequence.
Then test one closed cycle. Expand from \(\ell_0\) to \(\ell_1\), switch condition, contract to \(\ell_0\), restore the external state, and measure centre displacement against substrate fiducials. Independently check whether the full shape and relevant strain observables also close.
The distinctive prediction is not just displacement. It is:
A displacement predicted from separately acquired endpoint responses, whose direction reverses when the optical assignment to the outward and return strokes is exchanged.

The controls must include an optimized stress-only closed cycle, fixed-light cycles, matched-temperature controls, and equal-dose illumination with a different phase sequence. Beam-dose and registration controls are also essential: imaging can perturb the electronic state, and a common image displacement can imitate motion of both endpoints.
Actuation should therefore occur with the imaging beam off where possible, with all interruptions and thermal cycling included in the comparison. Apparent loss of diffraction contrast must not be counted as defect annihilation.
A mechanism-level pass would require reproducible retained displacement, closure of the specified shape/strain cycle, separation from matched controls, and agreement with the independently measured response integral. Report the full accessible loop population, including immobile and damaged loops.
No arbitrary specimen count guarantees this. A noise pilot should determine the preregistered sample size and detection threshold.
What would reject the candidate?
The strongest falsifiers are straightforward: no useful change in relative endpoint response; no non-destructive contraction window; or disappearance of the claimed displacement after proper phase, thermal and registration controls.
A positive result that is fully reproduced by the best stress-only protocol would still be scientifically informative, but it would remove the proposed optical operation’s claimed advantage.
7. Retained translation is not automatically defect removal
The distinction between a physical operation and a technology is especially important here.
Closing the span does not restore every local strain
For a fixed planar shape family, the signed slipped area is proportional to span. Restoring span and full shape can therefore restore its spatial integral while relocating the local plastic-distortion field.
The local field is not unchanged. A device could experience a different stress distribution even when the net integrated slip closes.
This is why endpoint separation alone is an inadequate acceptance measurement.
Translation does not annihilate a dislocation
The immediate output is relocation of the same line structure.
Clearing an active region requires a destination: a perimeter, a guard region, or a separately validated reaction or sink. That introduces another physical requirement.
Removing a strain-relieving interface segment changes how mismatch is accommodated. The proposal cannot promise simultaneous removal of every misfit dislocation and preservation of exactly the same strain relaxation without another accommodation mechanism.
For a guard-region architecture, capacity, backstress, later renucleation and motion back into the active region must all be measured. Moving a defect outside one microscope image is not sufficient.
Closing net plastic strain does not make the process energetically free
The reduced model dissipates power at the rate
\[
P_{\rm glide}=(m_C+m_N)F^2\geq0.
\]
Both expansion and contraction can dissipate energy even though their signed strain increments cancel.
The full resource boundary includes optical wall-plug energy, mechanical actuation, heating and cooling, metrology, specimen preparation, failed attempts and replacement. Mechanical energy recovery can be credited only when the apparatus actually recovers it.
The appropriate engineering metric is not “photons per moving dislocation.” It is something like
\[
\frac{\text{complete process cost}}
{\text{accepted device area meeting structural and functional specifications}}.
\]
Useful throughput remains unknown
For measured retained displacement \(d\) and complete cycle duration \(t_{\rm cyc}\),
\[
v_{\rm useful}=\frac{d}{t_{\rm cyc}}.
\]
A constant-response idealization needs approximately \(L/|d|\) successful cycles to move a loop a distance \(L\). Real throughput must additionally include settling, imaging, arrest, interactions and failures.
Uniform illumination and loading also do not automatically route every loop toward the desired destination. Differently oriented loops can respond differently, and clearing a heterogeneous population may demand additional control or selection.
These are scalability questions that a single-loop demonstration would not answer.
8. The incumbent comparison is already strong
The relevant competitors are not untreated defective films.
Actual defect reduction is established. Bioud and colleagues demonstrated dislocation-selective electrochemical processing followed by annealing in Ge/Si. They reported a reduction from roughly \(10^8\) to an etch-pit-based estimate near \(10^4\ \mathrm{cm^{-2}}\), together with improved optical emission from subsequently grown GaAs. Their paper discusses the characterization limits; this is not a universally transferable density guarantee. Nature
Growth engineering can also overcome apparent defect-density limits. Reverse grading in SiGe/Ge/Si has demonstrated enhanced dislocation reduction, with the lower density retained after subsequent Ge growth. APS Journals
Optical dislocation engineering is not new. A 2025 PMN-PT study demonstrated writing, erasing and rewriting dislocation structures. Thermal effects were indispensable, and an amorphous central region remained distinct from the erasable dislocation region. This is a close comparator, not evidence for the proposed ZnS mechanism. Nature
Accordingly, the candidate needs a consequential distinction: for example, selective post-growth relocation with less retained deformation or damage, lower complete processing burden, or access to a device geometry that existing routes cannot accommodate.
A successful loop experiment would not yet demonstrate any of those advantages.
There is no defensible single “best semiconductor-defect technology” independent of material and device. A later device comparison must allow the strongest practical route for that output—including substrate and integration choices—not artificially require the incumbent to use the new operation’s architecture.
9. Concrete compounding potential
The shortest credible development path is post-growth correction of selected active regions, not universal crystal repair.
The first useful task would specify a region, an initial threading-defect population, an allowed change in strain and roughness, and a retained electrical or optical performance requirement. The operation would then have to clear or relocate defects without generating an equally damaging population elsewhere in the device.
If successful, a subsequent task could test whether the improved template reduces defect propagation into an overgrown layer. That would turn a local intervention into a potentially compounding manufacturing benefit. It would require a fresh overgrowth experiment; the single-loop result would not establish it.
Only after those steps would transfer to other semiconductor families become justified. The carrier response, endpoint mobility, safe loading window and defect interactions must be reacquired in each material. ZnS parameters cannot be assigned to GaN, SiC or GaAs.
The broader technological possibility is therefore conditional: an additional way to control defect trajectories after growth could expand the manufacturable combinations of materials and device layouts. But a motion observed only in a small, specially selected population would remain a materials-science result, not a civilisation-scale manufacturing capability.

Decision
Retain closed-span dislocation transport as a narrowly defined experimental lead.
The work completed here is a conditional transport derivation, its exact failure conditions, a mobility-matching bound, numerical implementation checks, and a discriminating experiment tied to an existing material platform.
The next scientifically valuable information is same-loop, two-endpoint response under reversible loading and optical switching. Another uncalibrated mobility calculation would not materially advance the opportunity.
If that response exists, the proposed experiment could establish an additional operation for controlling where crystal defects reside. If either endpoint is immobile, contraction is destructive, or matched mechanical controls reproduce the effect, the candidate should be rejected or substantially redirected.
The mission is not yet achieved. The preserved opportunity is specific: controlled defect transport separated from continued net strain accumulation—not a claim that light already repairs crystals.
The search established the experimental foothold and substantial competing prior art, but did not resolve the missing response or historical priority. No physical experiment, material-calibrated simulation or industrial resource advantage was demonstrated.
