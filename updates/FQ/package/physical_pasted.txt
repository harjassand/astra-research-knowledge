Research outcome: phase-directed lattice-polarity rewriting
The candidate operation I developed is to use the relative phase of two coherent optical fields to favor one crystal polarity over its opposite, drive an existing boundary between them, and retain the selected structure after the fields are removed.
The proposed distinction is important: the light would supply a polarity-selective mechanical bias, rather than merely heating defects or increasing their mobility.
There is a credible physical mechanism for that bias. There is also experimental evidence that the relevant boundaries can disappear in a candidate material, gallium arsenide. What I have not established is an operating regime in which the optical force, boundary motion, absorption, material stability and retained useful outcome coexist. The quantitative screen exposes substantial obstacles, particularly to a low-duty-cycle femtosecond implementation.
This is therefore a specific invention hypothesis with a derived actuator law and a demanding experimental discriminator—not a demonstrated new manufacturing capability or a civilization-scale breakthrough.
1. The complete operation—and why it is different from “light moves defects”
Consider a compound semiconductor containing two regions with opposite lattice polarity. The desired operation is:
\[
\text{mixed-polarity crystal}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{phase-selected optical bias}}\;
\text{chosen polarity occupies a specified region}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{cooling and field removal}}\;
\text{retained useful structure}.
\]
The output must include the final atomic structure and its function. A changed optical signal, temporary displacement, or reduced visible boundary count is insufficient.
Gallium arsenide is a useful first assay because there is a demonstrated structural transformation to work with. Barrett and colleagues studied post-growth annealing of GaAs on silicon between 550 and 700 °C. At temperatures of at least 650 °C, their reported antiphase-boundary density decreased from \(0.10\) to approximately \(0.010\,\mu\mathrm m^{-1}\) in less than ten minutes. They reported an activation energy of \(3.8\) eV for dissolution. Those figures come from the accessible publisher abstract; I did not obtain the subscription full text. Springer
That establishes neither optical control nor a directional boundary mobility. It does, however, avoid inventing a material in which the indispensable atomic rearrangement has never been observed.
The technological problem must also be stated narrowly. Producing GaAs without antiphase boundaries is not itself an unavailable capability. Alcotte and colleagues demonstrated antiphase-boundary-free GaAs layers as thin as 150 nm on nominal 300 mm silicon substrates, with substantially improved optical and electrical properties relative to their boundary-containing samples. AIP Publishing
The proposed opportunity is therefore post-growth, directional selection of lattice polarity where ordinary annealing does not reliably produce the required spatial outcome, not the generic promise of “better semiconductor crystals.”
What I retained from Astra’s earlier work

2. The physical mechanism: an optical field that distinguishes opposite polarities
Opposite inversion-related crystal polarities have the same ideal bulk linear optical response, while their second-order susceptibility changes sign. This follows from the different transformation rules of second- and third-rank tensors. GaAs has the nonzero bulk second-order response permitted by its zincblende symmetry; its tensor structure has been analyzed against second-harmonic-generation measurements. arXiv
Let \(s=\pm1\) label the two polarities. To expose the mechanism, first use a deliberately restricted model:
\[
P_{\mathrm{NL}}
=
\epsilon_0 s\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}E^2.
\]
Here \(\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}\) includes the polarization/tensor contraction. Assume a transparent, approximately instantaneous response and a prescribed external field. The corresponding nonlinear material interaction potential is
\[
u_{\mathrm{NL}}(E)
=
-\int_0^E P_{\mathrm{NL}}(E')\,dE'
=
-\frac{\epsilon_0s\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}}{3}E^3.
\]
Apply phase-locked fields at \(\omega\) and \(2\omega\):
\[
E(t)=E_1\cos\omega t+E_2\cos(2\omega t+\phi),
\]
where \(E_1,E_2\) are peak internal field amplitudes. Direct cycle averaging gives
\[
\overline{E^3}
=
\frac34E_1^2E_2\cos\phi.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\overline{u_{\mathrm{NL},s}}
=
-\frac{\epsilon_0s\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}}4
E_1^2E_2\cos\phi.
\]
The energy-density difference between the polarities acts as a pressure on their boundary:
\[
\boxed{
p_\phi
=
\frac{\epsilon_0\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}}2
E_1^2E_2\cos\phi
}
\]
with its sign defined relative to the chosen direction of boundary advance.
Changing \(\phi\) by \(\pi\) reverses the preferred polarity without changing either individual beam intensity.
This is the useful feature. In the model, the control does not merely multiply all transition rates by a common factor. It changes the relative energetic preference for different structural outcomes.
What is—and is not—new here
The underlying two-color hyperpolarizability interaction is established physics. Phase-locked fundamental and second-harmonic fields have already been used to orient molecules; theoretical work explicitly analyzes the phase-dependent orienting potential. I am not claiming invention of that interaction. Frontiers
The narrower proposed operation is:
Use the reactive three-wave interaction to drive retained relocation or removal of a nonferroelectric crystal’s inversion boundary.

The sources reviewed do not establish that complete operation. They also do not constitute exhaustive novelty clearance. A publication claim would require a more complete search of nonlinear-optical forces, optical poling, domain engineering and relevant patents.
Geometry can eliminate the effect entirely
The scalar notation must not conceal a zero tensor contraction. In conventional cubic axes, the relevant GaAs bulk coupling involves the three distinct Cartesian components. Thus, a simplistic normal-incidence arrangement on a (001) surface with both fields entirely in-plane does not supply the assumed bulk cubic interaction.
A nonzero geometry must be designed from the actual tensor, internal field directions and optical boundary conditions. For example, the idealized combination of propagation along \([1\bar10]\) and polarization along \([111]\) permits a nonzero contraction; realizing those fields in a particular film or waveguide remains an optical-design obligation. This conclusion follows from the published tensor structure, not from assigning a favorable scalar susceptibility. arXiv
3. Quantitative screening: the attractive symmetry does not guarantee useful force
I evaluated the pressure scale using the following normalizations, not measured hot-GaAs operating parameters:
\[
\chi_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(2)}=100\ \mathrm{pm/V},
\qquad
n_\omega=n_{2\omega}=3.3,
\qquad E_1=E_2=E.
\]
For each color,
\[
I=\frac12n\epsilon_0cE^2.
\]
The resulting values are:
Peak internal field, each color	Phase-selective on-state pressure	Internal intensity, each color
\(10^7\ \mathrm{V/m}\)	\(0.443\ \mathrm{Pa}\)	\(0.0438\ \mathrm{GW/cm^2}\)
\(10^8\ \mathrm{V/m}\)	\(443\ \mathrm{Pa}\)	\(4.38\ \mathrm{GW/cm^2}\)
\(3\times10^8\ \mathrm{V/m}\)	\(11.95\ \mathrm{kPa}\)	\(39.4\ \mathrm{GW/cm^2}\)


These are calculated scales under the stated approximation. The intensities are internal traveling-wave-equivalent intensities; they are not automatically equal to the external power required by a resonator.
The important finding is nevertheless clear: substantial internal fields produce relatively modest mechanical pressures.
The short-pulse implementation has a severe duty-cycle problem
Consider rectangular 100 fs pulses at 1 MHz. Their duty fraction is
\[
D=10^{-7}.
\]
For an isothermal boundary with a linear overdamped response, the relevant average optical pressure at the middle field level is
\[
\overline p
=
Dp_{\mathrm{peak}}
=
4.43\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa}.
\]
The average internal intensity is still approximately \(438\ \mathrm{W/cm^2}\) per color.
As a declared first target—not an achieved performance—suppose a boundary must traverse \(1\,\mu\mathrm m\) in 600 s. With \(v=Mp\), and optimistically neglecting opposing forces and pinning, the required mobility is
\[
M_{\mathrm{req}}
=
\frac{10^{-6}}{600\,p}.
\]
For continuous application of \(443\) Pa,
\[
M_{\mathrm{req}}
=
3.76\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{m^4/(J\,s)}.
\]
For the specified pulsed exposure,
\[
M_{\mathrm{req,pulsed}}
=
3.76\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^4/(J\,s)}.
\]
No measured boundary mobility has been supplied that validates either target. The seven-order-of-magnitude pulse penalty is an exact consequence of this particular linear, fixed-temperature model.
I would reject the argument that a large femtosecond peak field, by itself, makes this a promising boundary-rewriting actuator. Nonlinear activated motion could change the conclusion, but it must be acquired rather than presumed.
For example, in a simplified single-path Arrhenius model, doubling the time-averaged rate through a duty-\(D\) barrier reduction requires
\[
D\left[
\exp\left(\frac{p\Delta V^\ddagger}{k_BT}\right)-1
\right]\ge 1.
\]
At \(650\,^\circ\mathrm C\), \(p=443\) Pa and \(D=10^{-7}\), this requires
\[
\Delta V^\ddagger
\gtrsim
4.64\times10^{-22}\ \mathrm{m^3}.
\]
That is an unmeasured collective activation volume, not evidence that such a cooperative transition exists. It specifies how demanding the proposed kinetic rescue would be.
“Below the bandgap” does not mean “negligible loss”
Hurlbut and colleagues measured two- and three-photon absorption in GaAs across the mid-infrared using 100 fs pulses. Later measurements and modeling also explicitly include free-carrier absorption generated by multiphoton excitation. PubMed
One useful warning comes from the tabulated GaAs three-photon coefficient at \(2.3\,\mu\mathrm m\):
\[
\alpha_3=0.35\ \mathrm{cm^3/GW^2},
\]
credited to Hurlbut’s measurements in the later comparison. At \(I=4.38\ \mathrm{GW/cm^2}\), this term alone corresponds to an effective on-state attenuation coefficient
\[
\alpha_3I^2\simeq6.71\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}.
\]
Thus, even an optically thin layer can absorb substantial power at the required intensity. This is not a hot-material loss prediction; it is evidence against treating a sub-bandgap beam as lossless. arXiv
The temperature mismatch is central. The demonstrated boundary dissolution uses hot GaAs. Published analysis shows that the two-photon and free-carrier absorption depend on temperature, wavelength and carrier state. A room-temperature optical coefficient cannot simply be paired with high-temperature structural mobility. AIP Publishing
Moving to longer wavelengths may change the balance. It does not establish a solution without measuring the resulting nonlinear coupling, carrier absorption and both-beam losses in the same regime.
4. The coupled physical model—not an independently optimized machine
The scalar pressure formula is an explanatory limit. A frequency-dispersive implementation should instead be formulated through a calibrated interaction Hamiltonian.
Let \(X\) describe boundary position, and let \(a,b\) be optical modes at \(\omega,2\omega\). A candidate conservative coupling is
\[
H_{\mathrm{int}}
=
\hbar\left[
g(X)b^\dagger a^2+
g^*(X)b(a^\dagger)^2
\right].
\]
The boundary changes the spatial overlap of the sign-changing nonlinear response with the optical fields. For coherent amplitudes \(\alpha,\beta\),
\[
F_X
=
-2\hbar\operatorname{Re}
\left[g'(X)\beta^*\alpha^2\right].
\]
This is the force expression to calibrate or derive microscopically. Substituting an arbitrary tabulated second-harmonic coefficient into the earlier scalar formula is not a substitute for that calculation.
The optical amplitudes themselves respond to the interaction:
\[
\dot\alpha
=
(i\Delta_1-\kappa_1/2)\alpha
-i2g^*\alpha^*\beta
+\sqrt{\kappa_{1e}}\,s_1,
\]
\[
\dot\beta
=
(i\Delta_2-\kappa_2/2)\beta
-ig\alpha^2
+\sqrt{\kappa_{2e}}\,s_2.
\]
Here \(\Delta_j\) are detunings, \(\kappa_j\) total loss rates, and \(s_j\) external drives. These quantities can depend on temperature, carriers and the evolving structure.
The material evolution must simultaneously include a boundary law and heat/carrier balance. Schematically,
\[
\dot X
=
\mathcal V
\left(
p_\phi+p_{\mathrm{capillary}}+p_{\mathrm{stress}},
T,n,\text{pinning and history}
\right),
\]
\[
C_V\dot T
=
\nabla\!\cdot(k\nabla T)
+
q_{\mathrm{abs}}(\alpha,\beta,T,n)
+
q_{\mathrm{diss}},
\]
\[
\dot n
=
G_{\mathrm{abs}}(\alpha,\beta,T,n)-R(n,T).
\]
These equations expose the missing joint acquisition rather than filling it with favorable numbers.
Several consequences follow.
A cavity is not an absorption exemption. It can reduce external drive power for a given internal field, but the material still absorbs at that field. Its hot loaded quality factor, both-color resonance conditions and moving-boundary detuning must be included.
Reactive interaction is not free work. Even at a phase where stationary frequency conversion is suppressed, the field phases and stored interaction energy respond to boundary motion. Mechanical work and losses must be supplied through the optical drive and other energy sources.
The phase bias need not be spatially uniform. Propagation introduces a relative phase involving \(k_{2\omega}-2k_\omega\). The force can vary or reverse across the sample. A thin region or a properly measured mode-overlap design is required; uniform bulk pressure cannot be assumed.
A useful conditional optimization result
At fixed temperature, carrier state, wavelength and beam ratio, suppose the measured local behavior can be represented by
\[
p=A I^{3/2},
\qquad
q_{\mathrm{abs}}=aI+bI^2+cI^3,
\]
with nonnegative loss coefficients.
Then
\[
\frac{p}{q_{\mathrm{abs}}}
=
\frac{A\sqrt I}{a+bI+cI^2}.
\]
For \(a>0\), an interior optimum satisfies
\[
\boxed{a-bI-3cI^2=0.}
\]
This is not a full process optimum: mobility, temperature, carriers and damage may change with intensity. It does show why “increase the field” is not an unlimited remedy once nonlinear absorption matters.
The optimization must ultimately concern retained useful transformation per total input—not pressure alone.
5. The strongest competitor is not necessarily optical
There are two already serious practical baselines: optimized dark annealing and growth that prevents the boundaries. Both have published GaAs demonstrations. Springer
There is also a less obvious physical comparator.
GaAs is piezoelectric. Opposite polarities reverse the piezoelectric tensor, so a combined electric field and mechanical stress can also distinguish them. Interferometric measurements reported a GaAs \(d_{14}\) coefficient of approximately \(-2.7\) pm/V. figshare.mq.edu.au
For an appropriately chosen tensor contraction, the mixed electromechanical interaction has the form
\[
g_s^{\mathrm{em}}
=
-sd_{\mathrm{eff}}E\sigma,
\]
giving a polarity pressure difference
\[
p_{\mathrm{em}}
=
2d_{\mathrm{eff}}E\sigma.
\]
Using \(|d_{\mathrm{eff}}|=2.7\) pm/V as an illustrative normalization,
\[
E=10^6\ \mathrm{V/m},
\qquad
\sigma=10^8\ \mathrm{Pa}
\]
gives
\[
p_{\mathrm{em}}\approx540\ \mathrm{Pa}.
\]
That is comparable to the middle optical-pressure example.
This is not a validated alternative process either. The reported coefficient is not a hot-annealing measurement; electrical leakage, screening, stress-induced defects and the compatible field geometry must be assessed. Nevertheless, it invalidates the assumption that coherent light is the only available polarity-conjugate actuator.
The optical route should advance only if it offers something this alternative cannot provide at acceptable cost—for example, genuinely useful contactless spatial control—not merely because its phase dependence is interesting.
6. The decisive experiment: retained, phase-selected structure
The experiment should be designed to distinguish two claims:
1. A phase-dependent intervention changes boundary behavior.
2. The proposed reactive optical pressure produces a retained useful transformation.
The first is not sufficient evidence for the second.
Establish the operating window before attempting a device
Use a specimen with an independently mapped boundary and a physically available exit or annihilation path. Measure the two-color nonlinear response, absorbed power, local temperature, carrier response and boundary kinetics under the same conditions.
A boundary anchored by its topology or interface registry cannot be treated as a freely translating wall. Nor can an overall decrease in boundary density be converted into a universal directional mobility.
For the illustrative \(1\,\mu\mathrm m\)-in-600-s target, the measured response must support the required displacement while remaining within the sample’s thermal, optical and mechanical limits. If it does not, stop that implementation.
Randomize phase and controls on comparable initial structures
Use matched regions or specimens rather than assuming that reversing the phase on an irreversibly altered boundary reproduces the original initial condition.
Compare phase settings separated by \(\pi\), a quadrature setting, loss of mutual coherence, each color separately and optimized dark annealing. Include the stress–electric-field comparator when experimentally compatible.
The reactive-pressure model predicts a specific phase dependence, field-amplitude scaling and tensor dependence. Its coupling should be determined independently of the final boundary-displacement fit.
Equal incident intensities do not establish equal heating or equal carrier excitation. A phase-dependent absorption pathway could mimic phase-selected motion. Temperature, absorption, carriers and strain must be measured sufficiently well to exclude that explanation—or the observed mechanism must be relabeled rather than claimed as confirmation of conservative optical pressure.
Demand the complete endpoint after field removal
The success criterion should be a predeclared region acquiring the selected polarity after illumination and annealing cease, with retention over a relevant time and temperature interval.
Structural assessment must check for residual boundaries, unwanted faults, stoichiometric changes, cracks and displaced damage in a surrounding guard region. Optical polarity mapping can guide the assay, but an altered nonlinear optical signal alone does not establish atomic repair.
The same region must then pass an application-relevant functional test. That might concern electrical transport, recombination or a deliberately specified optical response. The test must not assume that one favorable structural count guarantees all useful properties.
Finally, report total electrical input to lasers, heating, cooling and stabilization, together with processing time, affected volume and yield. Compare equivalent final quality—not unequal intermediate signals.
A successful experiment would therefore establish phase-selected, retained manufacturing control, not merely another pump–probe effect.
7. Does this qualify as an Astra flagship direction?
Not yet.
The positive result is a concrete route from a measured symmetry-sensitive interaction to an outcome-selective force. Its direction is not an arbitrary fitted “repair selectivity.” The candidate also has a real structural precedent in its first proposed material.
The negative results are equally consequential. The simple short-pulse implementation has a severe duty-cycle penalty. Strong internal fields need not provide strong boundary pressure. The hot structural regime and the optical-loss regime have not been qualified together. Existing growth and annealing methods already set a demanding baseline, and an electromechanical actuator could supply a comparable symmetry-selective bias.
A successful GaAs experiment would establish a narrow physical capability. It would not establish a general atom assembler, arbitrary defect repair, an energy-generation technology or a path to civilization-scale manufacturing by itself.
The broader hypothesis worth retaining is more precise:
A structural distinction invisible to ordinary linear driving may become controllable through an externally imposed field product that couples to a sign-changing material response.

For that to become a major manufacturing principle, the material must simultaneously possess a sufficiently strong response, an accessible structural pathway, tolerable losses and a useful retained endpoint. Transfer to another material requires reacquiring those conditions; shared symmetry is not enough.
My present decision is to retain phase-directed polarity rewriting as a falsifiable mechanism, but not promote the GaAs optical implementation as a breakthrough or build a larger apparatus concept around it. The decisive missing result is a same-regime measurement showing that the available phase-selective bias can complete a useful retained transformation faster, more selectively or at lower total cost than the strongest compatible alternative.
The literature establishes relevant ingredients and serious competitors. It does not establish their coexistence in that successful operation.
