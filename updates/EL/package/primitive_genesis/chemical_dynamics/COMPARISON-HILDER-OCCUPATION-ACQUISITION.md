# Hilder coarse-graining results versus the occupation certificate

**Audit status:** completed against the pinned arXiv versions below. This is a comparison of two specified primary sources with the candidate in [`../astra_capital/RESEARCH_NOTE.md`](../astra_capital/RESEARCH_NOTE.md), not an exhaustive priority search and not a novelty claim.

## Finding

Neither paper supplies (i) a finite-experiment, confidence-controlled acquisition of the capital note's occupation residual (D), or (ii) a whole-visible-path total-variation bound for an arbitrary frozen, history-dependent predictor. The closest established mathematical result is a time-marginal coarse-graining estimate proved by converting conditional hidden-law error to entropy dissipation. The 2026 paper extends that functional-inequality route and provides explicit model-based lower bounds for its constants.

This is substantial adjacent prior art. It already establishes quantitative control of the error between an exact projected **one-time distribution** and a reduced effective **one-time distribution** for finite-state continuous-time Markov chains. The 2026 result makes the required functional-inequality constants more explicit and applies them to non-reversible coarse-graining. Neither argument measures the candidate's direct on-policy hazard residual or compares laws on the space of visible trajectories.

## Exact overlap with the capital note

The common high-level setting is a finite hidden CTMC with a visible coarse-graining map. Hilder–Sharma define the exact projected marginal generator by averaging the full generator against the current hidden conditional law μ_t(·|y). This generator is time-dependent and requires the full law; they explicitly note that the projected process itself is generally non-Markov. Their effective chain instead averages against the stationary conditional ρ(·|y). See Section 1.1 and Section 2.2 of [arXiv:2201.10256v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10256v2), HTML lines 61–91.

The nearest proof mechanism is Hilder–Sharma Lemma 3.7 and Theorem 3.1. Lemma 3.7 differentiates the relative entropy between the exact coarse marginal μ̂_t and effective marginal η_t. Its remainder is driven by the difference between the time-dependent coarse generator \(\hat L_t\) and the stationary-conditional effective generator N; the proof bounds that term by a weighted conditional distance, \(\sum_y \|\mu_t(\cdot|y)-\rho(\cdot|y)\|_{TV}\,\hat\mu_t(y)\). Theorem 3.1 then uses Cauchy–Schwarz in time, Pinsker, the assumed within-fiber log-Sobolev inequality, and the full-chain entropy dissipation identity. The resulting bound controls \(\mathscr H(\hat\mu_t|\eta_t)\) by initial coarse entropy plus a constant times the square root of \(\mathscr H(\mu_0|\rho)-\mathscr H(\mu_t|\rho)\). For fixed finite horizon it also gives a constant independent of N and the coarse-graining map; a stronger 1/α version requires positive initial coarse marginals. These are statements about distributions at each time t, not path laws. See [Theorem 3.1 and Remark 3.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10256v2) (HTML lines 259–281), [Lemma 3.7](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10256v2) (lines 332–357), [Lemma 3.8](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10256v2) (lines 359–418), and the full [Theorem 3.1 proof](https://arxiv.org/html/2201.10256v2) (lines 420–450).

The exact analogue is therefore **not** the capital theorem's rate-residual inequality. In the capital note, for the frozen visible-history predictor, \(D=(1/T)\mathbb E\int_0^T\|Q^{(J_t)}E(X_t,:)-\hat v(t,H_t)\|_2^2dt\). The proof constructs a comparison process that matches the predictor's visible intensities and couples it with the true process up to the first discrepancy. The discrepancy intensity is at most the destination-wise ℓ1 rate error, which is bounded by \(\sqrt{k-1}\) times the instantaneous residual norm; Cauchy–Schwarz in occupation time yields \(TV(P,\hat P)\leq T\sqrt{(k-1)D}\). This controls the joint visible path, including jump times, under the tested feedback policy. It does not rely on stationarity, an invariant reference distribution, conditional mixing, or an LSI constant. See Theorem A and its proof in the capital note, especially Sections 2–3.

The 2023 result is closest to the *entropy/conditional-law* component of the candidate's motivation, not to its claimed path-space conclusion. The candidate's D directly measures the visible-intensity mismatch under the actual controller's hidden occupation law; the Hilder–Sharma proof controls hidden conditional-law departure from a supplied stationary conditional reference and integrates that through entropy dissipation. There is no identity in that paper that acquires the candidate's D from experiments.

## What the 2026 extension adds—and does not add

Hilder, van Meurs, and Sharma replace equilibrium-based PI/LSI tools with generalized inequalities relative to an arbitrary strictly positive reference measure on a finite space. The abstract states the main additions: continuity of the constants, explicit positive lower bounds, and non-reversible coarse-graining applications. In Section 5.1 they compare two time-dependent distributions solving the same forward Kolmogorov equation: generalized variance and relative entropy dissipate at rates set by gPI/gLSI constants, producing exponential **marginal** comparison bounds. See [arXiv:2602.17579v1](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17579v1), abstract/Sections 3–5 (HTML lines 41–50, 197–204, 652–681).

The most relevant quantitative step is Theorem 4.7. For an irreducible finite generator M and positive reference measure ζ, it gives

\[
\alpha_{gPI}(\zeta)\geq \frac{\zeta_* M_*}{|\mathcal Z|},\qquad
\alpha_{gLSI}(\zeta)\geq
\frac{\zeta_* M_* / |\mathcal Z|}{1+\tfrac12\log(1/\zeta_*)},
\]

where ζ_* is the minimum reference mass and M_* the smallest positive transition rate. Its proof routes a function's minimum to maximum along a path in the finite transition graph, applies Cauchy–Schwarz, then bounds gLSI using the ζ-symmetrized generator and a standard LSI estimate. The bound is conservative but explicit once the finite generator, its state count, and the reference masses/rates are supplied. The irreducibility hypothesis matters when applying Theorem 4.7 to a within-cluster restriction: that restricted generator must itself satisfy the theorem's hypothesis (or its gLSI constant must be bounded by another argument). See [Theorem 4.7 and proof](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17579v1) (HTML lines 586–620); the symmetrized-generator lemma is Section 4.3, Lemma 4.6 (lines 533–562).

For coarse-graining, Proposition 5.3 applies the Hilder–Sharma estimate with these generalized constants. It bounds relative entropy between the exact projected marginal and the effective marginal on a fixed interval, under a positive-initial-marginal condition, with the within-cluster gLSI constants in the denominator. The paper gives a spectral-gap expression for the symmetrized within-cluster generators and notes the more explicit lower bound from Theorem 4.7. This is the strongest directly comparable result among these two sources; it improves the **constant/model-level marginal estimate**, not its path-law or acquisition interface. See [Section 5.2.1, Proposition 5.3 and surrounding derivation](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.17579v1) (HTML lines 721–756). Theorem 5.6 treats an explicit scale-separated regime and likewise bounds coarse-grained marginals, not visible path distributions (Section 5.2.2, HTML lines 825–868).

The new constants do not close the candidate's acquisition gate. They use inputs such as a finite state-space size, positive reference masses, positive transition-rate information, or a spectral gap of a symmetrized generator. The candidate's assay instead seeks a high-confidence upper bound on a sampled controller-occupation functional without enumerating hidden states or supplying hidden support/mass bounds. Section 6's suggestion that the gLSI quality score may serve as an objective for learning coarse-graining maps is a proposed direction, not a finite-sample estimator or a confidence theorem (HTML lines 1031–1040).

## Acquisition comparison

The capital note's acquisition is a separate, explicit operation: replay the frozen controller from a repeatable preparation; independently stop at a uniform occupation time U; preserve the actual hidden state and visible history; randomize a post-stop probe; use product-compatible gain settings to observe a positive Gram form; use sequential short-time responses to acquire generator terms; freeze the predictor at the stop to measure its cross term; and combine moments by \(D=G-2B_{pred}+C_{pred}\). Confidence bounds on the raw moments and a PSD upper certificate yield \(D_{cert}\geq D\) with stated finite-sample concentration costs. Product composition, pre-stop noninterference, resetting, rate bounds, timing accuracy, and either within-label neutrality or microscopic event sensing remain hard physical gates. See capital note Sections 2–6.

Neither Hilder paper contains these random-stop, branch/replay, product-gain, moment-identity, or finite-confidence steps. The 2026 paper's deterministic lower bound for a functional-inequality constant is useful when a finite model is supplied; it is not an experimental estimate of D. Conversely, the capital note's proposed assay does not estimate the papers' conditional stationary reference law or gLSI constants. The acquisition targets and evidence interfaces differ.

## Bounded conclusion

The existing step to credit is quantitative **marginal coarse-graining via entropy dissipation**, with the 2026 paper supplying generalized functional inequalities and explicit finite-model constants. Neither requested source gives active finite-experiment acquisition of the occupation residual or a whole-path frozen-history predictor bound. The candidate path coupling and assay therefore remain distinct relative to this pair of sources; historical originality and any broader priority claim remain unassessed.
