That failure exposed the question pursued below:
When a continuous latent representation must obey exact discrete constraints, what algebraic structure is forced into the representation—and can that structure be extracted?
Let
\[ p_1,\ldots,p_n\in\mathbb R[z_1,\ldots,z_m] \]
be nonzero polynomials, and define binary outputs
\[ F_i(z)=\mathbf 1\{p_i(z)<0\}. \]
Assume the latent variable \(Z\) has a density strictly positive almost everywhere on \(\mathbb R^m\). Standard Gaussian input is one example. Polynomial zero sets then have probability zero.
Factor the output polynomials over \(\mathbb R\). Collect the distinct irreducible factors
\[ q_1,\ldots,q_s \]
that take both positive and negative values.
For each factor, record the outputs in which it occurs to odd multiplicity:
\[ v_j= \bigl(\operatorname{ord}_{q_j}(p_i)\bmod 2\bigr)_{i=1}^n \in\mathbb F_2^n. \]
Call \(v_j\) its factor mask. Let
\[ V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_2}\{v_1,\ldots,v_s\}. \]
For any output \(c_0\) attained away from the polynomial zero sets,
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{aff}_{\mathbb F_2} \bigl(\operatorname{supp}F(Z)\bigr) = c_0+V. } \]
Equivalently, a parity
\[ a\cdot F(Z)\pmod 2 \]
is almost surely constant if and only if
\[ a\cdot v_j=0 \qquad\text{for every }j. \]
Thus all exact parity invariants are the kernel of a finite binary matrix.
Even powers do not change signs. An irreducible factor that is not sign-changing has a fixed sign away from its zeros; absorb these fixed signs into an offset \(b\in\mathbb F_2^n\).
Unique factorization gives
\[ F(z) = b+\sum_{j=1}^s v_j\,\mathbf 1\{q_j(z)<0\} \quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2^n. \]
Therefore the support lies in \(c_0+V\).
The reverse inclusion requires a geometric fact: each sign-changing irreducible factor can be crossed without crossing any other distinct factor.
Here is why. Choose two points where \(q_j\) has opposite signs, and use their connecting direction as one coordinate \(t\), with remaining coordinates \(y\). Opposite endpoint signs persist on nearby parallel lines.
Over \(\mathbb R(y)\), irreducibility in characteristic zero makes \(q_j\) relatively prime to its \(t\)-derivative. Their resultant is therefore nonzero. Likewise, the resultant of \(q_j\) with each other distinct irreducible factor is nonzero. Choose a nearby line avoiding the zeros of these finitely many resultant polynomials and the relevant leading coefficients.
On that line, the opposite endpoint signs force a real root of \(q_j\). The root is simple and is not a zero of any other factor. Crossing it changes the output by exactly \(v_j\).
Both sides contain open sets, so both output values have positive probability. Consequently every \(v_j\) belongs to the span of differences of actual support points. This proves the reverse inclusion. \(\square\)
The operation can be written
\[ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}p = \bigl(\operatorname{ord}_{q}p\bmod2\bigr)_ {q\text{ sign-changing}}, \]
with
\[ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}(pr) = \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}p+ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}r. \]
It converts an exact-support question about continuous polynomial maps into binary linear algebra.
But it does not recover arbitrary support constraints.
For example, take
\[ p_1=x(x+y),\qquad p_2=y(x+y),\qquad p_3=xy. \]
The output has even parity because
\[ p_1p_2p_3=x^2y^2(x+y)^2. \]
The factor masks span the full four-point even-parity code, but only
\[ 000,\quad101,\quad011 \]
occur. The fourth point, \(110\), is impossible.
The theorem extracts the affine hull, not the full support or its probabilities. This counterexample prevents a false escalation into a general compatibility theorem.
Let
\[ C\le\mathbb F_2^n \]
be a binary linear code of dimension \(k\), and consider the uniform distribution on an affine translate \(c+C\).
For a basis
\[ B=(b_1,\ldots,b_k) \]
of \(C\), define its coordinate loads
\[ \ell_i(B)=\sum_{j=1}^k(b_j)_i. \]
The uniform law on \(c+C\) admits a Gaussian polynomial-threshold generator with coordinate degrees at most
\[ (d_1,\ldots,d_n) \]
if and only if \(C\) has a basis \(B\) satisfying
\[ \boxed{\ell_i(B)\le d_i\quad\text{for every }i.} \]
Whenever such a generator exists, there is a parity-monomial generator with no larger degree in any coordinate.
Suppose the original generator exists. Its support is exactly \(c+C\), so Theorem 1 says that its sign-changing factor masks span \(C\).
Select \(k\) independent masks \(b_1,\ldots,b_k\). Whenever \((b_j)_i=1\), the corresponding irreducible factor appears to odd positive multiplicity in \(p_i\). It contributes at least one to its degree. Hence
\[ \ell_i(B)\le\deg p_i\le d_i. \]
Conversely, suppose such a basis exists. Take independent standard Gaussians \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_k\), and set
\[ \boxed{ \widetilde p_i(Y) = (-1)^{c_i} \prod_{j:(b_j)_i=1}Y_j. } \]
An empty product is one.
The signs of the \(Y_j\) are independent fair bits. Therefore the output is
\[ c+\sum_{j=1}^k U_jb_j, \]
with independent uniform \(U_j\in\mathbb F_2\). This is uniform on \(c+C\), and
\[ \deg\widetilde p_i=\ell_i(B)\le d_i. \]
\(\square\)
The necessity proof extracts a basis from the actual factors of any successful representation. The sufficiency proof then gives an explicit replacement.
Arbitrary nonlinear real polynomials and arbitrary Gaussian latent dimension cannot improve the coordinate-degree budget beyond the best parity-monomial encoder.
The exact optimization identities are
\[ \min_F\sum_i\deg p_i = \min_{B\text{ basis of }C} \sum_{b\in B}\operatorname{wt}(b), \]
and
\[ \min_F\max_i\deg p_i = \min_{B\text{ basis of }C}\max_i\ell_i(B). \]
This preserves degree budgets, not necessarily the original latent dimension or circuit architecture. The replacement uses \(k\le n\) independent signs.
Suppose every nonzero codeword has Hamming weight at least \(d\). Every basis has \(k\) nonzero vectors, so
\[ \boxed{ \sum_i\deg p_i\ge dk, \qquad \max_i\deg p_i\ge\frac{dk}{n}. } \]
For a family with
\[ k\ge Rn,\qquad d\ge\delta n, \]
this forces
\[ \max_i\deg p_i\ge\delta Rn. \]
Thus exact generation requires linear degree in at least one coordinate.
Suppose a mixture of \(L\) polynomial-threshold generators produces the exact uniform law on \(c+C\). Let \(T_j\) be the total coordinate degree in component \(j\).
Every positive-weight component must itself be supported inside \(c+C\). If its affine-support dimension is \(r_j\), factor-mask extraction gives
\[ dr_j\le T_j. \]
Its support therefore has at most \(2^{T_j/d}\) points. Covering all \(2^k\) codewords requires
\[ \sum_{j=1}^L2^{T_j/d}\ge2^k. \]
If every coordinate degree is at most \(D\), then
\[ \boxed{ \log_2L+\frac D\delta\ge k, \qquad \delta=\frac dn. } \]
For affine Gaussian thresholds, \(D=1\), constant-rate and constant-relative-distance codes require exponentially many mixture components.
These distributions remain easy to sample when a generator matrix is supplied: draw \(k\) fair bits and perform binary matrix multiplication, using \(O(nk)\) operations.
Polynomial degree is not computational complexity. A high-degree parity monomial can be evaluated efficiently. None of these statements proves \(P\ne NP\), general sampling hardness, or an unrestricted neural-network lower bound.
The exact theorem could have been fragile: perhaps tiny leakage outside the code would destroy every lower bound.
A different argument shows that this is not so.
Let \(S\subseteq\{0,1\}^n\) have pairwise Hamming distance at least \(\delta n\). Let \(P\) be supported on \(S\), with
\[ \max_xP(x)\le2^{-h}. \]
For a uniform \(k\)-dimensional code, \(h=k\).
Let \(Q\) be a mixture of \(L\) Gaussian polynomial-threshold generators, each with coordinate degrees at most \(D\ge1\). Components may have different latent dimensions, means, covariances, and coefficients.
For \(0<\eta<1\), define
\[ K(D,\delta,\eta) = \left(\frac2\eta\right)^{ \cot^2\left(\frac{\pi\delta\eta^2}{4D}\right) }. \]
\[ \boxed{ d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q) \ge 1-\eta-LK(D,\delta,\eta)\,2^{-h}. } \]
No component is assumed to lie inside \(S\). The theorem allows arbitrary leakage and arbitrary component biases.
Two established analytic inequalities supply the inputs.
For correlated standard Gaussians \(G,G_\rho\), with
\[ \rho=\cos\theta, \]
Kane’s degree-\(D\) polynomial-threshold noise bound gives
\[ \Pr[F_i(G)\ne F_i(G_\rho)] \le\frac{D\theta}{\pi}. \]
This inequality is imported, not a new result of this investigation. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.2709)
Gaussian hypercontractivity gives, for every measurable set \(A\),
\[ \Pr[G\in A,\ G_\rho\in A] \le \Pr[G\in A]^{2/(1+\rho)}. \]
This follows from the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck \(L^{1+\rho}\)-to-\(L^2\) inequality. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1306.2410)
Now consider one mixture component, with atom probabilities \(q_x\). Put
\[ \theta=\frac{\pi\delta\eta^2}{2D}, \qquad a=\frac{1-\rho}{1+\rho} =\tan^2(\theta/2), \]
and
\[ \tau=\frac1K =\left(\frac\eta2\right)^{1/a}. \]
Call an atom in \(S\) heavy when \(q_x>\tau\). There are at most \(K\) heavy atoms. Let \(T\) be the remaining target atoms, and set
\[ q=Q_j(T). \]
The probability that both correlated outputs lie in \(T\) is at least \(q^2\). Indeed, the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck correlation operator is positive semidefinite:
\[ \langle1_A,P_\rho1_A\rangle = \|P_{\sqrt\rho}1_A\|_2^2 \ge(\mathbb E1_A)^2. \]
Split this joint event into equal and unequal outputs.
If the outputs are unequal, they differ in at least \(\delta n\) coordinates. Summing the noise bound over coordinates gives
\[ \Pr[\text{both in }T\text{ and unequal}] \le\frac{D\theta}{\pi\delta} =\frac{\eta^2}{2}. \]
For equal outputs, hypercontractivity applied to each atom’s inverse image gives
\[ \Pr[F(G)=F(G_\rho)=x]\le q_x^{1+a}. \]
Consequently
\[ \sum_{x\in T}q_x^{1+a} \le q\tau^a =\frac{\eta q}{2}. \]
Combining the two parts,
\[ q^2\le\frac{\eta q}{2}+\frac{\eta^2}{2}. \]
The positive root is \(\eta\), so
\[ Q_j(T)\le\eta. \]
Take the union \(H\) of the heavy sets across all components. It has at most \(LK\) points, while
\[ Q(S\setminus H)\le\eta, \qquad P(S\setminus H)\ge1-LK2^{-h}. \]
The difference on this event proves the theorem. \(\square\)
Since \(\tan x\ge x\),
\[ \log_2K \le \frac{16D^2\log_2(2/\eta)} {\pi^2\delta^2\eta^4}. \]
For fixed \(D,\delta\), targets with \(h\ge Rn\), and subexponentially many components,
\[ L=2^{o(n)}, \]
the total-variation distance tends to one.
For a simple finite consequence, take \(\eta=\tfrac12\). If
\[ d_{\mathrm{TV}}(P,Q)\le\frac14, \]
then necessarily
\[ \boxed{ \log_2L+ \frac{512}{\pi^2}\frac{D^2}{\delta^2} \ge h-2. } \]
For uniform good codes and polynomially many components, this forces
\[ D=\Omega(\sqrt n). \]
The constants can be large; the displayed bound may be numerically vacuous at small block lengths. Its content is the resource scaling and its independence from latent dimension.
The exact theorem gives a linear-degree obstruction:
\[ D=\Omega(n). \]
The approximation theorem gives only
\[ D=\Omega(\sqrt n) \]
for polynomial mixtures and fixed total-variation error.
Closing that gap is the precise unresolved extension of this investigation. I have neither proved that the linear bound survives approximation nor constructed a sublinear-degree approximate generator.
A tempting extension of the factorization argument is false.
Take
\[ p_1(x)=x,\qquad p_2(x)=x-\varepsilon. \]
For every \(\varepsilon>0\), they have no common irreducible factor. Nevertheless their sign bits disagree only on
\[ 0<x<\varepsilon, \]
whose Gaussian probability tends to zero.
Therefore:
Small parity error does not force literal shared polynomial factors.
Replacing exact factors with “approximately shared factors” without defining and controlling that notion would only hide the missing theorem.
The next object should describe probability-weighted sign boundaries, rather than exact divisibility.
In the exact proof, crossing a shared irreducible hypersurface flips a codeword-valued mask. Under approximation, nearby distinct hypersurfaces may replace one shared hypersurface. A useful replacement must measure this near-cancellation geometrically and charge it against polynomial degree.
The concrete question is whether near-code generation forces enough approximately codeword-labelled boundary structure to recover a linear degree cost—or whether approximate boundary matching permits a genuinely cheaper construction.
