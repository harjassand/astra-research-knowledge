2. The global modulation theorem
Let
\[
F:\mathbb R^{m\times n}\to\mathbb R
\]
be \(C^2\) and rank-one convex.
Let \(V:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R^m\) be a periodic curve satisfying
\[
\boxed{
V''(\theta)=-a(\theta)V(\theta),
\qquad a(\theta)\ge0.
}
\tag{1}
\]
The principal example is
\[
V(\theta)=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta),
\qquad a=1.
\]
Ellipses obtained by fixed linear transformations also satisfy this condition.
For a constant wavevector \(k\), consider the actual vector potential
\[
\boxed{
\varphi(x)=r(x)V(k\cdot x),
}
\tag{2}
\]
where \(r\) is an arbitrary real scalar amplitude.
In the periodic problem, choose \(k\) compatible with the period of \(V\). The amplitude may depend on every spatial coordinate. It need not be small, nonnegative, slowly varying or restricted to a finite Fourier bandwidth.
Theorem A — Positive modulation identity
For every constant matrix \(B\),
\[
\boxed{
\int_\Omega [F(B+D\varphi)-F(B)]\ge0.
}
\tag{3}
\]
This holds for smooth periodic amplitudes, and for smooth zero-boundary amplitudes on bounded domains. For quartic \(F\), it extends to the corresponding \(W^{1,4}\) amplitudes.
More importantly, the proof constructs the nonnegative terms.
Put
\[
\theta=k\cdot x,\qquad W=V',
\]
\[
P=rW\otimes k,\qquad Q=V\otimes\nabla r.
\]
Then \(D\varphi=P+Q\), and both \(P\) and \(Q\) are rank one pointwise.
Define the rank-one Bregman remainder
\[
\mathcal R_F(C,Q)
=
F(C+Q)-F(C)-DF(C)[Q].
\]
Rank-one convexity gives \(\mathcal R_F(C,Q)\ge0\).
The exact global identity is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\int_\Omega [F(B+D\varphi)-F(B)]
={}&\int_\Omega \mathcal R_F(B+P,Q)\\
&+\int_\Omega a(\theta)r^2
\int_0^1 t\,D^2F(B+tP)
[V\otimes k,V\otimes k]\,dt.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{4}
\]
Both terms on the right are nonnegative.
Proof: a primitive that retains the amplitude–direction correlation
Define a spatial flux through
\[
\mathcal A_i(\theta,r)
=
\int_0^r
DF(B+sW(\theta)\otimes k)
[V(\theta)\otimes e_i]\,ds.
\tag{5}
\]
Differentiate its pullback to the spatial domain. The amplitude derivative gives \(DF(B+P)[Q]\). The phase derivative uses both \(V'=W\) and \(W'=-aV\), yielding
\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{div}\mathcal A
={}&DF(B+P)[Q]+F(B+P)-F(B)\\
&-a(\theta)\int_0^r
s\,D^2F(B+sW\otimes k)
[V\otimes k,V\otimes k]\,ds.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]
The divergence integrates to zero. In the zero-boundary case, the primitive itself vanishes at \(r=0\). Moreover,
\[
\int_0^r s\,g(s)\,ds
=
r^2\int_0^1 t\,g(rt)\,dt,
\]
so nonnegativity holds for negative amplitudes as well.
Substitution into the Bregman identity proves (4).
No determinant-moment substitution, contact classification, heat flow or inverse-Hessian estimate appears in this proof. It handles the affine background before introducing any minimization.
The curve condition also has geometric content. A smooth, positively curved, closed planar curve containing the origin can be parametrized at constant areal speed. Then
\[
\det(V,V')=\text{constant}>0
\]
implies \(V''\parallel V\), and positive curvature gives \(V''=-aV\) with \(a>0\). Thus the construction is not restricted to a circular target loop.
For bounded-gradient instances, approximation of continuous rank-one-convex integrands extends Jensen beyond \(C^2\) tests. In the usual terminology, these gradient distributions are laminates. That property follows from the proved identity; it is not assumed merely because the distributions arise from gradients.
3. A uniform fourth-order margin for the complete invariant cone
Now let \(F\) be any rank-one-convex homogeneous quartic invariant under left \(SO(2)\). Define its rank-one lower bound
\[
\mu_F=
\min_{\substack{|R|=1\\ \operatorname{rank}R=1}}F(R)\ge0.
\]
For the circular carrier,
\[
\varphi(x)=r(x)
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(k\cdot x)\\
\sin(k\cdot x)
\end{pmatrix},
\]
the positive identity gives the quantitative theorem
\[
\boxed{
\inf_{B\in\mathbb R^{2\times2}}
\int_\Omega[F(B+D\varphi)-F(B)]
\ge
\frac{\mu_F}{12}\int_\Omega|D\varphi|^4.
}
\tag{7}
\]
All nine coefficients are permitted. The restriction is on the potential’s structure, not on a new low-dimensional subfamily of integrands.
The constant is independent of the amplitude, wavevector and affine background.
Why the coefficient is explicit
For a rank-one direction \(Q\), the polynomial
\[
f(t)=F(C+tQ)=At^4+Lt^3+C_2t^2+Dt+E
\]
is convex, with \(A=F(Q)\ge0\). Nonnegativity of \(f''\) gives
\[
C_2\ge\frac{3L^2}{8A}
\]
when \(A>0\). Completing the square proves
\[
\boxed{\mathcal R_F(C,Q)\ge\frac13F(Q).}
\tag{8}
\]
The degenerate \(A=0\) case follows directly from the nonnegative linear second derivative.
For the second term of (4), write
\[
g(s)=D^2F(B+sW\otimes k)[V\otimes k,V\otimes k].
\]
It is a nonnegative quadratic polynomial. Since \(V,W\) are orthonormal and \(F\) is output-rotation invariant, its leading coefficient is
\[
D^2F(W\otimes k)[V\otimes k,V\otimes k]
=4F(W\otimes k).
\]
A second scalar square completion gives
\[
\int_0^r s\,g(s)\,ds
\ge \frac19F(W\otimes k)r^4.
\tag{9}
\]
Consequently,
\[
\int[F(B+D\varphi)-F(B)]
\ge
\mu_F\int\left(
\frac{|\nabla r|^4}{3}
+\frac{r^4|k|^4}{9}
\right).
\]
Because
\[
|D\varphi|^2=|\nabla r|^2+r^2|k|^2,
\qquad
\frac{x^2}{3}+\frac{y^2}{9}\ge\frac{(x+y)^2}{12},
\]
equation (7) follows. No sharpness claim is made for \(1/12\).
This is genuinely outside a rank-one neighborhood
Take
\[
r(x,y)=1+\frac12\cos x,
\qquad
\varphi=r(x,y)(\cos y,\sin y).
\]
Its covariance, in row-major matrix coordinates, is exactly
\[
\boxed{
C=\operatorname{diag}\left(
\frac1{16},\frac9{16},\frac1{16},\frac9{16}
\right).
}
\]
No rank-one line captures more than \(9/20\) of its second moment. The smallest transverse-to-line \(L^2\) ratio is
\[
\sqrt{\frac{11}{9}}>1.
\]
This field is not covered by a small-relative-transverse-mass argument.
There are also examples with nonzero cubic coupling. For
\[
r(x,y)=
1+\frac13\cos x+\frac14\cos y+\frac15\cos(x+y),
\tag{10}
\]
the potential \(r(x,y)(\cos y,\sin y)\) has positive-definite covariance and averaged Hessian for the retained integrand
\[
G=3(u+v-2q)^2+\frac{35}{24}u^2+q^2-\frac52qu.
\tag{11}
\]
Its cubic vector is nonzero. Its six frequency classes form one connected component containing both primitive triad and quartet resonances.
The exact verifier reconstructs those facts and the completely minimized value:
\[
\Delta_G(D\varphi)
=
\frac{3646241010554033}{507189555936000}>0.
\]
For this \(G\), \(\mu_G=3\), so the uniform theorem gives the lower bound \(\tfrac14\int|D\varphi|^4\).
The theorem also survives critical concentration of its zero-boundary templates. Under
\[
\varphi_\rho(x)=\rho^{1/2}\varphi(x/\rho),
\]
the new amplitude is \(\rho^{1/2}r(x/\rho)\), the carrier becomes \(k/\rho\), and the fourth gradient integral is unchanged in two dimensions. Thus concentration does not defeat (7). This does not express every concentrated profile in the template.
4. An exact assembly rule after all affine directions are eliminated
The modulation theorem controls arbitrary internal resonances of one structured potential. To combine different potentials, their cross-correlations must be addressed explicitly.
Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_N\) be centered matrix fields whose joint moments through total degree four agree with those of independent fields having the same individual laws. Define
\[
C_i=\mathbb E[X_iX_i^T],\quad
H_i=\mathbb ED^2F(X_i),\quad
b_i=\mathbb EDF(X_i),\quad
c_i=\mathbb EF(X_i).
\]
Quartic polarization gives
\[
H_{\Sigma}=\sum_iH_i,\qquad b_{\Sigma}=\sum_ib_i,
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
c_{\Sigma}
=
\sum_i c_i+
\frac12\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{tr}(H_iC_j).
}
\tag{12}
\]
For compatible gradients and rank-one-convex quartics, \(H_i\succeq0\) in any matrix dimension. Indeed,
\[
A:H_iA=\mathbb ED^2F(A)[X_i,X_i],
\]
and Parseval expands the right side into nonnegative acoustic forms at rank-one Fourier coefficients.
Suppose the individual affine minima are finite and attained—as they are for the globally positive blocks constructed above. Write
\[
B_i=-H_i^\dagger b_i,\qquad
B_*=-H_\Sigma^\dagger b_\Sigma.
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\Delta_F\!\left(\sum_iX_i\right)
={}&\sum_i\Delta_F(X_i)
+\frac12\sum_{i<j}\operatorname{tr}(H_iC_j)\\
&+\frac12\sum_i(B_i-B_*)^TH_i(B_i-B_*).
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{13}
\]
Every additional term is nonnegative.
The last term is the exact cost of making all blocks use one affine background instead of their separately optimal backgrounds. It is derived by completing the individual affine quadratics—not by assuming their optimizers coincide.
This is an assembly rule for spatially overlapping gradients, distinct from disjoint-support gluing. The moment expansion and quadratic minimization are elementary; their value here is preserving the complete optimized quartic interaction.
Deterministic gradients can satisfy the required moment condition exactly
For finite Fourier potentials, call a signed zero-sum tuple primitive if no proper nonempty subtuple sums to zero. Multiplicities are allowed.
Partition the frequencies into packets and require:
Every primitive zero-sum tuple of length at most four lies entirely inside one packet.

Then the joint moments through degree four factor across packets.
To prove this, expand any monomial of degree at most four into Fourier characters. Every surviving zero-sum tuple decomposes into primitive zero-sum subtuples. By the hypothesis, each subtuple belongs to one packet, so each packet’s total frequency sum vanishes separately. These are exactly the terms that survive independent averaging.
Internal packet resonances are unrestricted. Thus packets from Theorem A can contain arbitrarily complicated amplitudes and can be assembled using (13), provided their short cross-packet resonances are excluded.
An exact construction is available. If packet \(i\) has maximum frequency norm \(K_i\), replace
\[
X_i(x)\quad\text{by}\quad X_i(N_ix)
=
D\!\left[\frac{\varphi_i(N_ix)}{N_i}\right],
\]
with integers satisfying
\[
N_j>4\sum_{i<j}N_iK_i.
\tag{14}
\]
In any relation of length at most four, a nonzero net frequency from the largest-index packet is too large to be cancelled by lower packets. Induction proves the required separation.
This preserves each packet’s internal distribution and compatibility exactly. It constructs a new arrangement; it does not pretend to preserve the energy of an arbitrary pre-existing arrangement.
A canonical reduction of counterexamples
For any finite Fourier potential, connect frequency classes through primitive triad and quartet relations. The connected components satisfy the separation condition above.
Therefore:
\[
\boxed{
\text{A negative finite-Fourier profile must contain a
negative primitive-resonance-connected component.}
}
\tag{15}
\]
This follows even without assuming all individual minima are attained: if every component had nonnegative gap for every background, their sum and the nonnegative covariance interactions would also be nonnegative.
The components may still be large. Equation (15) does not prove positivity within a connected component. It is a compatibility-preserving reduction, not a universal decomposition into already positive pieces. Fourier-wave and laminate approaches have extensive precedent; the current construction must be assessed against that literature rather than labelled new merely because it uses packets. arXiv
5. A compatible obstruction to unrestricted assembly
The cross-resonance condition is substantive. Even the polyconvex quartic
\[
F(A)=(\det A)^2
\]
has strictly negative interaction increments.
Let
\[
r(x)=1+a\cos x,\qquad s(x)=1-a\cos x,
\]
\[
\varphi_1=r(x)(\cos y,\sin y),\qquad
\varphi_2=s(x)(\cos2y,\sin2y).
\]
Each potential belongs to the globally positive modulation class.
Their combined Jacobian is
\[
\det D(\varphi_1+\varphi_2)
=
rr'+2ss'+(2r's+rs')\cos y.
\]
Exact integration gives
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb E(\det D(\varphi_1+\varphi_2))^2
-\mathbb E(\det D\varphi_1)^2
-\mathbb E(\det D\varphi_2)^2\\
&\hspace{5em}=-\frac74a^2+\frac{17}{16}a^4.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{16}
\]
At \(a=\tfrac12\),
\[
c_1=\frac{17}{128},\qquad
c_2=\frac{17}{32},\qquad
c_{\Sigma}=\frac{75}{256},
\]
so
\[
\boxed{c_{\Sigma}-c_1-c_2=-\frac{95}{256}.}
\tag{17}
\]
The individual cubic vectors vanish, hence their optimized gaps equal \(c_1,c_2\). The sum’s optimized gap is at most its zero-background value \(c_\Sigma\), strictly less than the sum of the individual optimized gaps.
This is not a quasiconvexity counterexample. Determinant-square Jensen proves the total gap nonnegative. It is an exact compatible counterexample to unrestricted additive assembly of positive margins.
It establishes why “prove every piece positive and add” is insufficient: genuine cross-packet resonances can lower the energy.
6. What changes when the phase is allowed to vary freely
The successful identity fixes an affine phase. For a general smooth phase \(\theta(x)\), put
\[
k(x)=\nabla\theta(x),\qquad
\varphi=rV(\theta).
\]
The same calculation gives an additional term
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_F
=
\sum_i\int_0^r s\,
D^2F(B+sW\otimes k)
[W\otimes\partial_i k,\ V\otimes e_i]\,ds.
}
\tag{18}
\]
The complete identity is now
\[
\boxed{
\text{Jensen gap}
=
\text{nonnegative Bregman term}
+\text{nonnegative acoustic term}
-\int\mathcal E_F.
}
\tag{19}
\]
The mixed Hessian in (18) pairs two different rank-one directions. Rank-one convexity does not assign it a sign.
For \(B=0\) in the invariant quartic cone, this term is particularly explicit. Write the orientation-sensitive part as
\[
q(Lu+Mv+Nw),
\]
and define
\[
\Xi(k)=
(Lk_1^2+Mk_2^2+Nk_1k_2)(k_2,-k_1).
\]
Then
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E_F=\frac{r^4}{4}
\operatorname{div}\Xi(\nabla\theta).
}
\tag{20}
\]
The amplitude weights the phase curvature; their correlation cannot be discarded.
The sign failure occurs even for the convex invariant quartic
\[
F_c(A)=(u+v-2q)^2
=|x_{\mathbb C}+\mathrm i y_{\mathbb C}|^4.
\]
Choose
\[
\theta(x,y)=x+\sin y,\qquad
r(y)^4=1-\frac12\sin2y.
\]
This defines a smooth periodic potential, and exact normalized integration gives
\[
\boxed{\mathbb E\mathcal E_{F_c}=\frac14>0.}
\tag{21}
\]
Changing the sign of the sine in \(r^4\) changes the sign of the mean defect.
The actual Jensen inequality remains true because \(F_c\) is convex. Equation (21) shows that a global extension cannot simply declare the new term harmless; it must control it against the positive terms in (19).
Some nonaffine phases can still be handled differently. The note proves
\[
\inf_B\mathbb E[F(B+D\varphi)-F(B)]
\ge\frac{\mu_F}{24}\mathbb E|D\varphi|^4
\]
for every complex one-coordinate envelope
\[
\varphi(x,y)=z(x)e^{\mathrm iny},\qquad n\ne0.
\]
That proof uses amplitude-weighted convexity in one matrix column and full averaging over the output rotation. It permits arbitrary complex envelopes, zeros and winding after approximation. It does not cover arbitrary two-dimensional phases or general multi-carrier superpositions.

What substantial obstacle was removed
There is now a positive construction for an infinite-dimensional, globally multidirectional, internally resonant class, with a uniform quartic margin and arbitrary affine backgrounds. Its proof does not pass through the contact machinery.
There is also an exact way to assemble many such classes while preserving complete affine optimization—provided their short cross-packet resonances are controlled.
What remains
An arbitrary gradient is not known to admit either an affine-phase modulation representation or a resonance-separated decomposition into positive blocks. The exact examples show that the missing cross-correlations can lower energy, even when the individual components and the integrand itself are well behaved.
The substantive continuation is therefore to control the signed phase-curvature or connected cross-packet interaction together with its positive remainder, or to construct a compatible profile for which the total becomes negative. Merely renaming the signed term as an invariant, or straightening the phase while ignoring the resulting variable coefficients, would not advance the proof.
Further local-contact classification or single-carrier optimization is no longer a productive target. A focused investigation of the explicit signed interaction is justified by these identities. Without a mechanism comparing it to the positive terms, repeated unguided runs should be stopped and this branch preserved rather than treated as converging on the complete theorem.
The advance is global but scoped: compatibility can produce an exact positive flux identity far from rank-one neighborhoods, and quartic optimization can be preserved under a precise assembly rule. The unresolved question is how to control the cross-phase correlations that neither operation removes.
