Given incomplete but calibrated information about the environment, determine exactly which energy uncertainty remains—and whether it is already too small to affect a design decision.
The resulting operation is not another best-fit bath. It constructs extremal compatible baths, and in an important subclass certifies their maximum possible effect without solving an interacting system.
Let \(d\) denote \(m\) impurity fermion modes, with an arbitrary finite, number-conserving interacting Hamiltonian \(H_A\). Couple them to a quadratic bath:
\[ H=H_A+c^\dagger h c+d^\dagger Vc+c^\dagger V^\dagger d. \]
All bath energies are measured relative to the same specified chemical potential. Define the impurity excess ground energy
\[ \mathcal E(H_A;h,V) = \lambda_{\min}(H)-\operatorname{Tr}\min(h,0). \]
The subtraction removes the uncoupled bath’s ground energy. For two designs with the same bare bath, this reference cancels.
This is a zero-temperature grand-canonical comparison. Although the Hamiltonian conserves particle number, the minimum is not restricted to a specified particle-number sector.
Partition the bath spectrum into signed bands. Within one band, write
\[ h_b=\sigma A,\qquad \sigma\in\{-1,+1\},\qquad aI\preceq A\preceq bI,\quad 0\le a<b. \]
The required information is
\[ \boxed{W=VV^\dagger,\qquad T=VAV^\dagger.} \]
Here \(W\) is the coupling-weight matrix of that band, and \(T\) its first absolute-energy moment. Both are only \(m\times m\), regardless of how many bath modes occupy the band.
They satisfy
\[ W\succeq0,\qquad aW\preceq T\preceq bW. \]
No bath eigenvectors, bath state, marginal, gap, or optimal channel is supplied.
Factor \(W=LL^\dagger\), where \(L\) has full column rank \(r\le m\), and define
\[ B=L^+T(L^+)^\dagger. \]
The mean bath has one-particle Hamiltonian \(\sigma B\) and coupling \(L\). It uses \(r\) modes.
For the endpoint bath, construct the positive residues
\[ W_a=\frac{bW-T}{b-a}, \qquad W_b=\frac{T-aW}{b-a}, \]
and place them at energies \(\sigma a,\sigma b\). Factoring these residues constructs its couplings. It uses at most \(2r\) modes.
Both baths reproduce the supplied moments exactly.
For every allowed interacting impurity \(H_A\),
\[ \boxed{ \mathcal E_{\mathrm{endpoint}} \le \mathcal E_{\mathrm{true}} \le \mathcal E_{\mathrm{mean}}. } \]
Moreover, the endpoint and mean baths themselves satisfy the information supplied. Therefore,
\[ \boxed{ \inf_{\text{compatible baths}}\mathcal E = \mathcal E_{\mathrm{endpoint}}, \qquad \sup_{\text{compatible baths}}\mathcal E = \mathcal E_{\mathrm{mean}}. } \]
This requires no particle–hole symmetry.
The extrema refer to the stated moment-information class. Additional requirements such as a fixed lattice geometry or bath dimension can narrow that class. Also, subtracting two individually sharp energy intervals is valid but is not asserted to give the jointly sharp interval for a shared-bath design difference.
The upper bound follows from a specific variational restriction. Compress each bath band onto the subspace directly coupled to the impurity. Freeze the discarded positive-energy modes empty and negative-energy modes filled. After correctly subtracting the filled-bath reference energy, the restricted Hamiltonian is exactly the mean-bath Hamiltonian.
The lower bound uses a constructive dilation. For any \(aI\preceq A\preceq bI\), define
\[ \widetilde A= \begin{pmatrix} A & \sqrt{(A-aI)(bI-A)}\\ \sqrt{(A-aI)(bI-A)} & (a+b)I-A \end{pmatrix}. \]
Its spectrum lies entirely at \(a\) and \(b\). Couple the impurity only to the original block. Restricting the added modes to their empty or filled reference state recovers the original Hamiltonian, so the enlarged system can only lower the normalized ground energy.
The coupling residues of its two eigenspaces are precisely \(W_a,W_b\). Decoupled modes can then be removed.
Thus two measured or computed band moments identify actual extremal environments, not merely a plausible approximation.
The second theorem imposes a stronger environmental symmetry but provides a much cheaper certificate.
Assume the matrix-valued coupling spectrum is symmetric about the chemical potential. The impurity Hamiltonian itself need not be symmetric.
Writing \(s=\epsilon^2\), the hybridization on the imaginary axis has the form
\[ \Delta(i\omega)=-i\Gamma(\omega), \qquad \Gamma(\omega)=\omega\int\frac{\nu(ds)}{\omega^2+s}, \]
where \(\nu\) is a positive matrix-valued measure.
For each absolute-energy band \([a,b]\), acquire
\[ W=\int\nu(ds), \qquad S=\int s\,\nu(ds). \]
Here the second input is a squared-energy moment \(S\), not the moment \(T\) in Theorem 1.
Set \(W=LL^\dagger\) and \(B=L^+S(L^+)^\dagger\). Then
\[ \Gamma_{\mathrm{low},b}(\omega) = \omega L(\omega^2I+B)^{-1}L^\dagger. \]
An upper bound is constructed from endpoint residues
\[ W_a=\frac{b^2W-S}{b^2-a^2}, \qquad W_b=\frac{S-a^2W}{b^2-a^2}, \]
giving
\[ \Gamma_{\mathrm{high},b}(\omega) = \omega\left( \frac{W_a}{\omega^2+a^2} + \frac{W_b}{\omega^2+b^2} \right). \]
Summing over bands yields
\[ \Gamma_{\mathrm{low}}(\omega) \preceq \Gamma_{\mathrm{true}}(\omega) \preceq \Gamma_{\mathrm{high}}(\omega) \quad\text{for every }\omega>0. \]
Both bounds are physical finite baths reproducing the supplied moments.
For every allowed \(H_A\),
\[ \mathcal E(\Gamma_{\mathrm{high}}) \le \mathcal E(\Gamma_{\mathrm{true}}) \le \mathcal E(\Gamma_{\mathrm{low}}), \]
and
\[ \boxed{ 0\le \mathcal E(\Gamma_{\mathrm{low}}) - \mathcal E(\Gamma_{\mathrm{high}}) \le R_0, } \]
where
\[ \boxed{ R_0= \frac1\pi\int_0^\infty \log \frac{\det[\omega I+\Gamma_{\mathrm{high}}(\omega)]}      {\det[\omega I+\Gamma_{\mathrm{low}}(\omega)]} \,d\omega. } \]
The decisive property is
\[ \boxed{\text{The bound is attained by the free resonant impurity }H_A=0.} \]
Therefore, the largest uncertainty permitted by these bath envelopes, uniformly over all impurity interactions, can be calculated using two noninteracting one-particle problems.
There is a finite-temperature version for the impurity grand potential:
\[ R_\beta= \frac{2}{\beta} \sum_{n\ge0} \log \frac{\det[\omega_nI+\Gamma_{\mathrm{high}}(\omega_n)]}      {\det[\omega_nI+\Gamma_{\mathrm{low}}(\omega_n)]}, \qquad \omega_n=\frac{(2n+1)\pi}{\beta}. \]
The exact impurity Green function satisfies a causal inverse representation. At \(i\omega\), that gives
\[ 0\preceq-\operatorname{Im}G(i\omega) \preceq[\omega I+\Gamma(\omega)]^{-1}. \]
The proof in the note derives this through a self-adjoint Liouvillian and a Schur complement; it does not assume an available exact self-energy. Impurity self-energy sparsity itself is established prior mathematics, not a new primitive claimed here. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.04796)
Interpolate between the two physical baths. The derivative of the impurity grand potential is an exact contraction of the hybridization change with \(-\operatorname{Im}G\). The displayed matrix inequality bounds that derivative. Integrating it produces the logarithmic determinant.
For \(H_A=0\), the inequality becomes equality.
This removes an important circularity: the correlated calculation is not needed to establish whether the environmental description is sufficiently accurate for that calculation.

Suppose the finite reference is the higher-energy bound, and the environmental uncertainty satisfies
\[ E_{\mathrm{ref},j}-R_j \le E_{\mathrm{true},j} \le E_{\mathrm{ref},j}. \]
Then, for a design pair,
\[ \boxed{ \Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}-R_1 \le \Delta_{\mathrm{true}} \le \Delta_{\mathrm{ref}}+R_0. } \]
With the same bound \(R\) for both designs, this is a \(\pm R\) interval, not \(\pm2R\).
For certified finite-reference solver intervals \([\ell_j,u_j]\), the fully propagated comparison is
\[ \boxed{ \Delta_{\mathrm{true}} \in [\ell_1-u_0-R_1,\;  u_1-\ell_0+R_0]. } \]
A decision is certified when this interval lies entirely on one side of zero—or another specified design threshold.
This gives a constructive stopping rule: acquire more environmental information only while its certified uncertainty could still change the decision. A variational upper estimate alone does not supply the two-sided reference-solver certificate needed here.
The inputs are coupling-weighted spectral moments, including off-diagonal matrix information for multiple impurity channels. An ordinary scalar density of states is generally insufficient.

Locality or a known band structure can reduce that cost in particular systems, but the theorem does not assume a free spectral oracle.

The largest unresolved issue is not bath discretization. It is whether an interacting substrate can legitimately be represented by the quadratic bath whose spectrum was measured or computed.
I constructed an exact fermionic counterexample showing that even complete one-particle spectral agreement cannot establish that.
Consider two bath modes with
\[ H_B(U_B)=n_2+n_3+U_Bn_2n_3, \]
and choose either
\[ U_B=-\frac95 \quad\text{or}\quad U_B=2. \]
Both have the vacuum as their unique ground state. Their entire uncoupled zero-temperature one-particle Green functions are identical:
\[ \boxed{G_B(z)=\frac{I_2}{z-1}.} \]
The interaction is invisible to this observation because it acts only when both bath modes are occupied.
Now couple either bath to
\[ H_A=-n_0-n_1+n_0n_1 \]
with
\[ H_{\mathrm{coupling}} = \frac45 (d_0^\dagger c_2+d_1^\dagger c_3+\mathrm{h.c.}). \]
Exact rational certificates give
\[ \boxed{ E\!\left(U_B=-\frac95\right) < -\frac{19}{10} < E(U_B=2). } \]
A decoupled reference design with an appropriate fixed scalar reference has energy \(-19/10\) for both baths. Thus the preferred design reverses despite identical complete uncoupled one-particle spectral information.
The proof uses a rational trial state for one inequality and an exact positive-definite factorization for the other.
This is a failure witness, not a materials benchmark. Its consequence is precise:
Single-particle environmental spectroscopy cannot, by itself, certify a correlated chemical-design comparison. Higher-order environmental response or an independently justified bound on non-Gaussian effects is necessary in general.
