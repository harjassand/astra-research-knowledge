The strongest candidate I developed is using coherent atomic motion to select which crystallographically equivalent atoms an electron collision modifies.
The proposed capability is not simply faster irradiation or another way to excite a phonon. A coherent vibration would temporarily give otherwise equivalent atoms different momentum projections; a synchronized collision would convert that distinction into a retained structural change.

The last candidate has one advantage over the others: its elementary coupling is fixed by momentum conservation. It does not require inventing an unknown optical-to-chemical coupling strength.
That does not mean its useful reaction probability is known.
Suppose a crystal has several equivalent atoms in its primitive basis. A coherent optical phonon can give them different instantaneous velocities even though their chemical identities are identical.
For an atom of mass \(M\), initial momentum \(\mathbf P=M\mathbf v\), and collision impulse \(\mathbf q\),
\[ \Delta K = \frac{|\mathbf P+\mathbf q|^2-|\mathbf P|^2}{2M} = \frac{q^2}{2M}+\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v. \]
The first term is the stationary-target recoil. The second is a velocity-dependent energy transfer.
The proposed address is the sign and magnitude of \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf v_j\) for basis atom \(j\).
This identity is established mechanics, not a new physical law. The influence of lattice vibration on electron-induced damage is also established experimentally. The proposed extension is to impose a coherent, basis-dependent velocity pattern and convert it into a retained occupancy pattern. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.108.196102)
Trigonal Te has a three-atom helical basis. Its totally symmetric \(A_1\) vibration changes the helix radius. Consequently, the three radial displacement directions are separated by \(120^\circ\). [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1011.0090)
Represent the radial motion by
\[ \mathbf R_j(t)=\mathbf R_j^0+\mathbf e_jQ(t), \qquad \mathbf e_j= \left( \cos\frac{2\pi j}{3}, \sin\frac{2\pi j}{3}, 0 \right). \]
For a recoil direction in the basal plane at azimuth \(\theta\),
\[ v_{j,\parallel} = \dot Q\cos\left(\theta-\frac{2\pi j}{3}\right). \]
At \(\theta=0\), the projections are
\[ \boxed{v,\;-\frac v2,\;-\frac v2.} \]
Reversing the phonon velocity reverses that pattern. Directing the recoil along the helix axis removes this particular \(A_1\) velocity projection.
Thus, even a spatially broad beam could—in principle—prefer one basis class over the other two. The intended retained output is a nonzero vacancy or conversion imbalance,
\[ m_{\mathrm{ret}} = n_0-\frac{n_1+n_2}{2}, \]
measured after the coherent motion and electronic excitation have decayed.
This would be a restricted form of parallel atomic addressing. It would not provide arbitrary atom-by-atom assembly.
The potentially new capability is:
Converting a coherent phonon’s basis-dependent momentum pattern into a retained, sublattice-resolved material modification.
It is not coherent-phonon generation, pump–probe timing, electron-beam manipulation, or vibration-assisted damage individually.
Direct electron-beam modification is a serious baseline: experiments have already demonstrated Si dopants moving through graphene by electron-induced bond inversions, with retained, atomically resolved changes. The proposed method would have to add parallel crystallographic selection—not merely demonstrate that electrons can move atoms. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.113.115501)

For an electron with kinetic energy \(E\),
\[ p_ec=\sqrt{E(E+2m_ec^2)}. \]
In the heavy-target, near-backscattering approximation,
\[ q\simeq2p_e, \qquad T_0(E)=\frac{2E(E+2m_ec^2)}{Mc^2}. \]
These expressions determine the kinematic contribution. They do not determine whether an atom escapes, exchanges positions, recombines, or causes secondary damage.
Using \(M=127.60\,u\) for Te, the calculated stationary-target maximum transfers are approximately \(1.89\), \(6.67\), and \(12.8\) eV at electron energies of \(100\), \(300\), and \(500\) keV, respectively. The mass input is from the Royal Society of Chemistry; the recoil values are calculated here. [Periodic Table](https://periodic-table.rsc.org/element/52/tellurium)
For a locally sinusoidal radial oscillation with measured amplitude \(A\) and frequency \(f\),
\[ v_{\max}=2\pi fA. \]
Taking \(f=3.24\) THz as a numerical reference, at \(300\) keV:
\[ \boxed{ \delta T_{\max} \simeq 0.0855\ \mathrm{eV} \left(\frac{A}{1\ \mathrm{pm}}\right). } \]
The one-versus-other basis splitting is
\[ \boxed{ \Delta T_{0,1} \simeq 0.128\ \mathrm{eV} \left(\frac{A}{1\ \mathrm{pm}}\right). } \]
These are sensitivities to a measured atomic trajectory, not claims that a specified amplitude is achievable in a processing specimen. With damping or chirping, the correct input is the measured \(\dot Q(t)\), not simply \(2\pi fA\).
A sub-eV splitting could matter near a sufficiently sharp retained-event onset. Whether that onset is sharp enough is precisely what remains unknown.
Let the coherent kinetic energy per atom at the relevant phase be
\[ \epsilon_{\mathrm{coh}}=\frac12Mv^2. \]
Then
\[ \boxed{ |qv|=2\sqrt{T_0\epsilon_{\mathrm{coh}}} } \]
for aligned velocity and recoil.
For equal-amplitude motion, with consistent spatial averaging and no imported energy or other released energy reservoir,
\[ \epsilon_{\mathrm{coh}}\leq\epsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}, \]
giving the ceiling
\[ \boxed{ |\delta T|\leq2\sqrt{T_0\epsilon_{\mathrm{abs}}}. } \]
This shows a possible useful feature: a relatively small coherent energy can substantially bias a larger collision energy. The electron supplies most of the collision energy; the phonon changes how it is transferred.
But the bound does not permit independently choosing a large velocity, weak heating, and a long lifetime.

For the three radial velocities,
\[ \sum_jv_{j,\parallel}=0. \]
If the sites had identical scalar response functions \(g\), then
\[ \sum_jg(T_0+qv_{j,\parallel}) \]
would have no first-order velocity term.
In contrast,
\[ g(T_0+qv)-g(T_0-qv/2) = \frac32qv\,g'(T_0)+O(v^2). \]
Thus a sublattice-resolved observable can be first-order sensitive while the total damage signal is not.
The equal-response assumption is not guaranteed at a fixed beam direction. Nevertheless, this calculation identifies the correct measurement target: counting only total missing atoms could miss the proposed addressing operation.

For matched preparations with opposite coherent velocities, define equal-dose retained yields \(Y_j^\pm(E)\), and
\[ D_j(E)=\frac{Y_j^+(E)-Y_j^-(E)}{2}, \qquad S_j(E)=\frac{\partial}{\partial E} \frac{Y_j^+(E)+Y_j^-(E)}{2}. \]
In the small-signal, near-backscattering limit, where changing transferred energy dominates the response,
\[ \boxed{ \frac{D_j}{S_j} \approx \frac{qv_{j,\parallel}}{T_0'(E)}. } \]

For independent irreversible events, let \(H_t\) and \(H_u\) be the integrated hazards for target and unwanted modifications:
\[ P_t=1-e^{-H_t}, \qquad P_u=1-e^{-H_u}. \]
Requiring
\[ P_t\ge0.99, \qquad P_u\le0.01 \]
implies
\[ H_t\ge4.605, \qquad H_u\le0.01005, \]
so
\[ \boxed{\frac{H_t}{H_u}\ge458.2.} \]
This is an illustrative fidelity requirement, not a proposal to delete an entire Te sublattice.
Within this independent-event model, cycling does not repair inadequate selectivity: if every allowed exposure has a target-to-unwanted hazard ratio below the required value, a positive mixture of those exposures cannot exceed it. Healing, feedback, or structural changes would require a different model—not an assumption that repeated pulses solve the problem.
