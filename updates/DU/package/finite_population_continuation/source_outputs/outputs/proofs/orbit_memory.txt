The new proposed bridge is from the sphere-specific positive-composition argument to conjugation on all traceless Hermitian matrices. A uniform cap bound over **every** nonzero conjugacy orbit, including nearly degenerate spectra, supplies the missing interface. The resulting state exponent is `d−1`, rather than the ambient-space exponent `(d²−2)/2`.

## 1. Concrete consequence

Fix an integer `d≥2`. There is an explicit finite alphabet `A_d⊂SU(d)` of algebraic gates, independent of the horizon `T`, such that the following process has sharp classical state cost:

\[
 p_*(+\mid\rho,g_1,\ldots,g_t,E)
 =\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[E G_t\left(\frac{\rho}{2}+\frac{I}{2d}\right)G_t^*\right],
 \qquad G_t=g_t\cdots g_1.
 \tag{1}
\]

Here `ρ` is any d-dimensional density matrix, `0≤E≤I` is a terminal binary effect, and `t≤T`. A classical simulator receives `ρ` only at preparation and `E` only at the terminal query. Its persistent gate-dependent information is an `m`-valued state. Its preparation law may be arbitrary, its gate-aware stochastic transitions may be arbitrary and time dependent, and its terminal response may depend on `t,T,E`. The time and deadline are supplied. A seed, preparation label, stored word, or accumulated matrix outside that state is not free.

For uniform absolute probability error

\[
 0\le\epsilon\le\epsilon_d:=\frac{1}{8d(d^2-1)},
\]

the proposed theorem is

\[
 m_*(T;\epsilon)=\Theta_d((T+1)^{d-1}).
 \tag{2}
\]

The upper bound has zero probability error. The lower bound needs only `2(d²−1)` explicitly specified preparations and one fixed binary effect, at time `T`. It permits all stochastic kernels described above; it assumes no affine preparation rule, continuity, stationary kernel, reversibility, or regularity of the simulator.

Equivalently, the optimal dynamic state register has

\[
 \log_2m_*(T;\epsilon)=(d-1)\log_2(T+1)+O_d(1)
 \tag{3}
\]

bits. This is a finite-memory simulation theorem at fixed d. It is not a general computational speedup or a total-device memory separation. The compiler and read-only kernel descriptions are charged in Section 9. Constants are not uniform in growing d.

## 2. General transfer theorem

Let a compact group `G` act orthogonally on a real Euclidean space `V` of dimension `D`. Let Haar probability be `dg`. Assume there are constants `C≥1` and `k>0` such that, for every nonzero `x∈V`, every `y∈V`, and every `b>0`,

\[
 \Pr_{g\sim dg}(\|gx-y\|\le b)
 \le \min\left\{1,C\left(\frac b{\|x\|}\right)^k\right\}.
 \tag{4}
\]

Let `ν` be a finite probability on G. Suppose its regular averaging operator has an absolute mean-zero L² gap:

\[
 \|Q_Gf\|_2\le\lambda\|f\|_2,
 \quad\int_G f=0,
 \quad\lambda<1;
 \qquad\beta=1-\lambda^2.
 \tag{5}
\]

For row-stochastic matrices `P(g)` on m states define

\[
 (\mathcal T_P b)_j
 =\sum_g\nu(g)\sum_iP(g)_{ij}\,g b_i,
 \quad
 \|b\|_{1,2}=\sum_i\|b_i\|,
\]

and

\[
 F_m(T)=\sup_{P_1,\ldots,P_T}
 \|\mathcal T_{P_T}\cdots\mathcal T_{P_1}\|_{1,2}.
 \tag{6}
\]

The supremum includes arbitrary time-dependent, gate-aware kernels. Set

\[
 A(D,C,k)=4096D(64C)^{2/k},\qquad
 c(D,C,k,\nu)=\frac{\beta\log2}{A(D,C,k)+1}.
\]

**Transfer theorem.** Under (4)–(5),

\[
 F_m(T)\le2\exp\left[-c(D,C,k,\nu)\frac{T}{m^{2/k}}\right]
 \quad(m\ge1,T\ge0).
 \tag{7}
\]

Thus a group-orbit cap exponent becomes a sharp candidate positive-memory lifetime exponent. The group gap and the uniformity over all nonzero orbits are separate hypotheses. A spectral gap only on linear observables is insufficient for this argument.

### 2.1 Matched Gaussian potential

Let `γ_r=N(0,r²I_D)`, `f_μ=μ*γ_r`, and

\[
 \mathscr E_r(\mu)=D(f_\mu\|\gamma_r).
\]

For μ supported in the unit ball,

\[
 0\le\mathscr E_r(\mu)\le\frac{1}{2r^2}.
 \tag{8}
\]

Indeed, for `X∼μ` and independent standard Gaussian Z,

\[
 \mathscr E_r(\mu)
 =\frac{\mathbb E\|X\|^2}{2r^2}-I(X;X+rZ).
\]

If `η≤cxμ` in convex order, then

\[
 \mathscr E_r(\mu)
 \ge\mathscr E_r(\eta)+D(f_\mu\|f_\eta).
 \tag{9}
\]

To prove this, observe that

\[
 \ell_\eta(z)=\log\frac{f_\eta(z)}{\gamma_r(z)}
 =\log\int\exp\left(\frac{z\cdot v}{r^2}
 -\frac{\|v\|^2}{2r^2}\right)d\eta(v)
\]

is convex. Independent Gaussian addition preserves convex order. Subtracting the three divergences in (9) leaves
`∫(f_μ−f_η)ℓ_η≥0`. Compact support gives affine growth bounds on `ℓ_η`, so all integrals are finite. This is the precise source mechanism reused from Astra. An unmatched Gaussian reference would not establish this identity.

### 2.2 Conditional means evolve in convex order

Let `X_t=g_tX_{t−1}`, with gates independent and identically distributed according to ν. Let `S_t` be any m-state controller using kernel `P_t(g_t)` and fresh independent randomness. Put

\[
 v_i(t)=\mathbb E[X_t\mid S_t=i],\quad
 q_i(t)=\Pr(S_t=i),\quad
 \mu_t=\sum_iq_i(t)\delta_{v_i(t)}.
\]

Write `Qμ` for the average of rotated measures. Conditional on `S_t`, the variable `g_tv_{S_{t−1}}(t−1)` has conditional expectation `v_{S_t}(t)`. Hence

\[
 \mu_t\le_{cx}Q\mu_{t-1},\qquad
 M_t:=\int\|v\|^2d\mu_t(v)\le M_{t-1}.
 \tag{10}
\]

This uses the actual gate-dependent state distribution. It does not assume state probabilities are independent of the current gate.

### 2.3 The group gap decreases the same potential

Let `J` be Haar averaging on functions on V, and put `F=√f_μ`. Regular gap (5) implies

\[
 \|Q_VF-JF\|_2^2\le\lambda^2\|F-JF\|_2^2.
 \tag{11}
\]

Here is a direct proof avoiding representation-theoretic assumptions. For each x, apply (5) to `h↦F(h⁻¹x)−JF(x)` on G, then integrate over x. Haar invariance and invariance of Lebesgue measure under the orthogonal action convert the two integrated norms into (11). The invariant component is fixed by Q, so
`||Q_VF||²≤||JF||²+λ²||F−JF||²`.

Gaussian smoothing commutes with every orthogonal gate. Rotations preserve `γ_r`. Therefore

\[
 \mathscr E_r(\mu)-\mathscr E_r(Q\mu)
 =\sum_g\nu(g)D(g_\#f_\mu\|Qf_\mu)
 \ge\beta\|\sqrt{f_\mu}-J\sqrt{f_\mu}\|_2^2.
 \tag{12}
\]

For completeness, `D(p||q)≥||√p−√q||²`. Averaging gives at least
`2−2〈Q_VF,√(Qf_μ)〉≥2−2||Q_VF||≥1−||Q_VF||²`; use (11). Equations (9), (10), and (12) show that stochastic merging cannot replenish the potential spent by averaging gates.

### 2.4 Finite atoms force a noninvariant component

Suppose μ has at most m atoms in the unit ball and `M≥a`, `0<a≤1`. Set

\[
 s=\sqrt{a/2},\quad L=2\sqrt D,\quad
 b=\frac{s}{2}\left(\frac a{16Cm}\right)^{1/k},
 \quad r=\frac bL.
 \tag{13}
\]

Let E be the union of radius-b balls about those atoms whose norms are at least s. Their total weight is at least `a/2`, since `M≤s²+Pr(||X||≥s)`. Since `Pr(||Z||≤2√D)≥3/4`,

\[
 \int_Ef_\mu\ge3a/8\ge a/4.
 \tag{14}
\]

Every group orbit meeting E has norm at least `s−b≥s/2`. By (4) and a union bound, its Haar fraction in E is at most

\[
 Cm(2b/s)^k=a/16.
\]

Because JF is invariant and Lebesgue measure is invariant,

\[
 \int_E(JF)^2
 =\int_V\left[\int_G1_E(gx)\,dg\right](JF(x))^2dx
 \le a/16.
\]

The reverse triangle inequality on E now gives

\[
 \|F-JF\|_2^2\ge
 (\sqrt{a/4}-\sqrt{a/16})^2=a/16.
 \tag{15}
\]

### 2.5 Half-life and composition

If `M_T≥a`, it remains at least a at every earlier time. Use the same r from (13) throughout. Each step costs at least `βa/16` of potential, while (8) and (13) give

\[
 \mathscr E_r(\mu_0)
 \le\frac{16D}{a}\left(\frac{16Cm}{a}\right)^{2/k}.
\]

Consequently

\[
 T\le\frac{256D}{\beta a^2}
 \left(\frac{16Cm}{a}\right)^{2/k}.
 \tag{16}
\]

For any initial `b` with `Σ_i||b_i||=1`, realize it by
`Pr(S_0=i)=||b_i||`, `X_0=b_i/||b_i||`. Then
`b_i(t)=E[X_t1_{S_t=i}]`, and
`||b(t)||_{1,2}=E||E[X_t|S_t]||≤√M_t`.
If this norm exceeds `1/2`, take `a=1/4` in (16). It follows that

\[
 F_m(n_m)\le\frac12,\quad
 n_m=\left\lfloor\frac{A(D,C,k)}\beta m^{2/k}\right\rfloor+1.
\]

Submultiplicativity and `F_m(t)≤1` give

\[
 F_m(T)\le2^{-\lfloor T/n_m\rfloor}
 \le2e^{-(\log2)T/n_m},
\]

and `n_m≤(A+1)m^{2/k}/β` proves (7). No logarithmic block approximation of Haar measure is used.

## 3. Uniform SU(d) orbit-cap lemma

Take

\[
 V=\{H=H^*:\operatorname{Tr}H=0\},\quad
 D=d^2-1,\quad k=2d-2,
\]

with Hilbert–Schmidt norm and conjugation action. Define, for `1≤j≤d−1`,

\[
 q_j=j(d-j),\quad
 K_{d,j}=\frac1{q_j!}\prod_{r=1}^j
 \frac{(d-j+r-1)!}{(r-1)!}.
\]

and

\[
 C_d=[2\sqrt d(d-1)]^{2d-2}.
 \tag{17}
\]

**Orbit-cap lemma.** For every nonzero traceless Hermitian H, every Hermitian K, and every `b>0`,

\[
 \Pr_U(\|UHU^*-K\|_{HS}\le b)
 \le\min\{1,C_d(b/\|H\|_{HS})^{2d-2}\}.
 \tag{18}
\]

In particular the constant does not deteriorate with eigenvalue multiplicity or near degeneracy.

### 3.1 Complex Grassmann caps, proved directly

Let P be a fixed rank-j projection and Q a Haar rank-j projection. For `0<δ≤1`,

\[
 \Pr(\|P-Q\|_{HS}\le\delta)
 \le K_{d,j}\delta^{2j(d-j)}.
 \tag{19}
\]

To see this, draw a standard complex Gaussian `d×j` matrix and split it as `[A;B]`, where A is j×j. A is invertible almost surely. The column space is Haar on the Grassmannian and is the graph of `Z=BA⁻¹` over the first j coordinates.

With complex Lebesgue measure, the density of Z is

\[
 p(Z)=\pi^{-dj}\int
 e^{-\operatorname{Tr}A^*(I+Z^*Z)A}
 |\det A|^{2(d-j)}dA
 =p(0)\det(I+Z^*Z)^{-d}\le p(0).
\]

The Jacobian `|det A|^{2(d−j)}` comes from the complex linear map `Z↦ZA`. Gram–Schmidt on the Gaussian columns gives

\[
 p(0)=\pi^{-q_j}\prod_{r=1}^j
 \frac{(d-j+r-1)!}{(r-1)!}.
\]

If `s_r` are singular values of Z, then

\[
 \|P-Q\|_{HS}^2=2\sum_r\frac{s_r^2}{1+s_r^2}.
\]

When this is at most `δ²≤1`, each `s_r²≤1` and
`Σs_r²≤δ²`. Thus the cap is contained in the radius-δ Euclidean ball in `C^{q_j}`, of volume `π^{q_j}δ^{2q_j}/q_j!`. Bounding the density by p(0) proves (19). Its prefactor is at most one: write its numerator as

\[
 \prod_{\ell=0}^{d-j-1}\prod_{r=1}^j(r+\ell)
 \le\prod_{\ell=0}^{d-j-1}\prod_{r=1}^j(j\ell+r)
 =q_j!.
\]

For `δ>1`, the bound `δ^{2d−2}≥1` is automatic. Since `2j(d−j)≥2d−2`, all ranks share

\[
 \Pr(\|P-Q\|_{HS}\le\delta)
 \le\min\{1,\delta^{2d-2}\}.
 \tag{20}
\]

### 3.2 Reduction of arbitrary Hermitian caps to Grassmann caps

Let ordered eigenvalues of H be `λ_1≥⋯≥λ_d` and `R=||H||HS`. Trace zero implies the spectral range is at least `R/√d`. Therefore some adjacent gap satisfies

\[
 \gamma=\lambda_j-\lambda_{j+1}\ge\frac{R}{\sqrt d(d-1)}.
\]

Let P be the projection on the first j eigenspaces. In the eigenbasis,

\[
 \|WHW^*-H\|_{HS}^2
 =\|WH-HW\|_{HS}^2
 =\sum_{a,b}(\lambda_a-\lambda_b)^2|W_{ab}|^2
 \ge\gamma^2\|WPW^*-P\|_{HS}^2.
 \tag{21}
\]

The last equality for the projection norm follows from its two off-diagonal blocks. No perturbation theorem is needed.

If the cap in (18) is nonempty, choose one `U_0` in it. Every other U in it satisfies
`||U_0^*U H U^*U_0−H||HS≤2b`. By (21), it lies in a Grassmann cap of radius `2b/γ`. Haar invariance, (20), and the displayed gap lower bound prove (18), with (17). A scalar K component has no effect on the argument. Haar U(d) and Haar SU(d) give the same conjugation and Grassmann laws.

The uniform spectral spread, rather than a fixed multiplicity stratum, is what closes the degenerate-orbit interface. The cap exponent cannot be increased uniformly: for `H=P−I/d` with P rank one and `0<b≤√2`,

\[
 \Pr_U(\|UHU^*-H\|_{HS}\le b)
 =(b^2/2)^{d-1}.
\]

Indeed this is the event `1−|〈ψ,Uψ〉|²≤b²/2`; a Haar squared coordinate has the Beta(1,d−1) law. Conditional means can have these degenerate spectra even when the original inputs are chosen elsewhere. Thus the minimum nonzero orbit dimension `2d−2`, rather than the dimension of a generic orbit, is the uniform geometric parameter of this proof.

## 4. Positive-lift theorem for d-level conjugation

Assume the finite gate law has group gap (5). Define

\[
 a_d=\frac1{d-1},\quad
 A_d=4096(d^2-1)(64C_d)^{1/(d-1)},\quad
 c_{d,\nu}=\frac{(1-\lambda^2)\log2}{A_d+1}.
\]

The geometric part of this constant is polynomial in d:

\[
 A_d=16384(d^2-1)d(d-1)^2\,64^{1/(d-1)}
 \le2^{20}d^5.
\]

Only the imported spectral gap `1−λ²` is not made numerically effective here. This bound on `A_d` does not by itself give a uniform-in-d implementation or spectral-gap estimate.

Applying Section 2 to Section 3 gives

\[
 F_m(T)\le2\exp[-c_{d,\nu}T/m^{a_d}].
 \tag{22}
\]

Let `ρ_m=lim_TF_m(T)^{1/T}`, the joint spectral radius of the structured family. Section 5 constructs, for sufficiently large m,

\[
 \rho_m\ge1-4d m^{-a_d}.
 \tag{23}
\]

Together with (22),

\[
 1-\rho_m=\Theta_{d,\nu}(m^{-1/(d-1)}).
 \tag{24}
\]

This rate applies to every positive lift, not merely to a nearest-neighbor discretization, a fixed grid, or a stationary stochastic controller.

## 5. Exact positive interpolation from a projective net

Let `D_d={ρ−I/d:ρ density}`. Let `P_i=|ψ_i〉〈ψ_i|` be a net of pure projectors with projective squared chord distance

\[
 \min_i(1-|\langle\psi,\psi_i\rangle|^2)\le\tau
 \quad\text{for every unit }\psi.
\]

For `0<τ≤1/d`, set `α=1−dτ`, `v_i=P_i−I/d`, and `L=conv{v_i}`. Then

\[
 \alpha D_d\subseteq L\subseteq D_d.
 \tag{25}
\]

Proof: for any nonzero traceless H, take a maximum-eigenvalue eigenvector ψ. A nearby net point has expectation at least
`λ_max(H)−τ(λ_max(H)−λ_min(H))`. Trace zero gives
`λ_max−λ_min≤dλ_max`. Thus `h_L(H)≥αλ_max(H)=h_{αD_d}(H)`. Support-function containment proves (25).

For every unitary U and every i, `αUv_iU*∈L`. Choose row-stochastic barycentric coefficients satisfying

\[
 \sum_jP(U)_{ij}v_j=\alpha Uv_iU^*.
 \tag{26}
\]

For a finite alphabet these are finitely many linear feasibility problems. The dual positive-lift operator fixes the vector family `{v_i}` up to eigenvalue α, because conjugating back each equation gives `αv_i`. Thus `F_m(T)≥α^T` whenever the net uses at most m points.

A maximal `√τ`-separated projective net has at most `(4/τ)^{d−1}` points. Indeed the disjoint radius-`√τ/2` projective balls have Haar mass `(τ/4)^{d−1}`; the pure-state cap formula follows from the Beta(1,d−1) law of one squared coordinate of a Haar unit vector. Taking `τ=4m^{−1/(d−1)}` proves (23) whenever α is positive. Extra unused states may be padded.

## 6. Exact finite-horizon Born simulator

Use the projective net in Section 5 with

\[
 \tau=\frac1{2d(T+1)},\quad
 \alpha=1-\frac1{2(T+1)},\quad w_i=v_i/\alpha.
\]

Equation (25) says `D_d⊂conv{w_i}`. Encode `x=ρ−I/d` by any barycentric law on this inflated polytope. Equation (26) gives

\[
 \mathbb E[w_{S_t}]=\alpha^tG_txG_t^*.
\]

At time t and terminal effect E, read out with probability

\[
 h_{t,E}(i)=\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[
 E\left(\frac Id+\frac12\alpha^{-t}w_i\right)\right].
 \tag{27}
\]

The matrix in (27) is

\[
 \left(1-\frac12\alpha^{-(t+1)}\right)\frac Id
 +\frac12\alpha^{-(t+1)}P_i.
\]

It is a density matrix because Bernoulli's inequality gives
`α^{T+1}≥1/2`. Consequently every readout probability is in `[0,1]`. Its expectation is exactly (1). The internal damping is compensated by the allowed time-dependent readout; the compensation stays within the state cone throughout the horizon.

This argument is not asserted for visibility one, one fixed readout valid at all times, or access to unknown input states through a single uncopyable quantum sample. The input here is an explicit preparation parameter, and the target process has one terminal query.

## 7. Finite preparations prove the lower bound

Choose an algebraic Hilbert–Schmidt orthonormal basis `H_1,…,H_D` of V, `D=d²−1`, and one fixed `H_0` of HS norm one in that basis. Prepare, at the input to (1), one of

\[
 \rho_{\ell,\pm}=I/d\pm H_\ell/d.
 \tag{28}
\]

These are density matrices since `||H_ℓ||op≤1`. After the half depolarization in (1), the actual prepared state is
`I/d±H_ℓ/(2d)`. Use only the fixed effect

\[
 E_0=(I+H_0)/2.
\]

Let `X_0` be uniform on `{±H_ℓ}` and drive the simulator with independent ν gates. For each word,
`E[X_TX_T^T]=I_V/D`. Put `f=〈H_0,X_T〉`; then `E f²=1/D` and `|f|≤1`. For the terminal outcome `Y∈{−1,+1}`, the target conditional mean is `f/(2d)`. Therefore the target bounded score satisfies

\[
 \mathbb E[Yf]=1/(2dD).
\]

Uniform probability error ε changes the score by at most `2ε`. For `ε≤1/(8dD)`, a valid simulator has

\[
 \mathbb E[Yf]\ge1/(4dD).
\]

But Y is read from `S_T` alone for this fixed query. Thus

\[
 |\mathbb E[Yf]|
 \le\sum_i\|\mathbb E[X_T1_{S_T=i}]\|_{HS}
 \le F_m(T).
\]

Equation (22) now implies

\[
 m\ge\left(\frac{c_{d,\nu}}{\log(8dD)}T\right)^{d-1}.
 \tag{29}
\]

Together with Section 6 this proves (2). Only finitely many preparation labels were used; no continuum of terminal measurements was needed for the obstruction.

## 8. Explicit fixed gate alphabet and the imported theorem

For each adjacent pair `(j,j+1)`, embed

\[
 U_x=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2&-i\\-i&2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 U_z=\frac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}2-i&0\\0&2+i\end{pmatrix}
\]

as a two-dimensional block, acting identically on the other coordinates. Let `A_d` contain the identity and these `2(d−1)` gates and their inverses. Thus `|A_d|=4(d−1)+1`, and take ν uniform.

Their entries are algebraic and their determinants are one. The block angle is irrational: its doubled-angle cosine is `3/5`, and if it were a rational multiple of `2π`, `6/5` would be a rational algebraic integer. Powers of each block gate therefore contain its full one-parameter circle in their closure. The two axes generate the adjacent SU(2) block. Adjacent SU(2) blocks generate SU(d), as their elementary off-diagonal Lie algebra generators and commutators generate every off-diagonal matrix and traceless diagonal matrix. Hence the alphabet is dense.

The external input is the Bourgain–Gamburd spectral-gap theorem for finitely generated dense algebraic subgroups of SU(d): [A Spectral Gap Theorem in SU(d)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1108.6264). The later [Benoist–de Saxcé theorem](https://www.math.univ-paris13.fr/~desaxce/publications/spectral_gap.pdf) gives the corresponding compact simple-group statement. These established results were checked at their primary sources. Their proofs are not reproduced here. Symmetry makes the averaging self-adjoint; the positive identity weight also excludes an eigenvalue near minus one if one uses a top-gap convention. Therefore some λ<1 exists in (5).

No numerical all-frequency gap constant is certified by this work. The theorem's explicit constants are conditional on a valid gap bound λ; the existence of a positive d-dependent constant in (2) follows from the imported theorem.

## 9. Finite descriptions, acquisition, and inclusive costs

The state-count theorem permits exact real transition probabilities. For this explicit alphabet one can strengthen the upper construction to a finite algebraic compiler, with substantial costs charged rather than hidden.

### 9.1 Rational projective grid

Let

\[
 L_T=\left\lceil\sqrt{4d(d-1)(T+1)}\right\rceil.
\]

For each coordinate k, form vectors z with `z_k=1` and all other real and imaginary coordinates in the rational grid
`{−1,−1+1/L_T,…,1}`. Keep their rational projectors `zz*/||z||²`. There are at most

\[
 N_T=d(2L_T+1)^{2(d-1)}=O_d((T+1)^{d-1})
 \tag{30}
\]

projectors, and every entry has `O_d(log(T+2))` bits.

For any pure state, select a largest-magnitude coordinate and divide by it, making that coordinate one. Every other complex coordinate has modulus at most one. Rounding each real component to the nearest grid value changes z by at most `√(2(d−1))/(2L_T)`. Normalization changes the resulting unit vector by at most twice that amount. Its squared projective chord distance is therefore at most `2(d−1)/L_T²≤1/[2d(T+1)]`. This proves that the grid supplies the required net. The square root is used only to choose an integer grid resolution; all stored projectors are rational.

### 9.2 Stochastic row construction

Work in the fixed real number field `Q(√5)`, storing real and imaginary parts separately. Conjugating a rational projector by any alphabet gate keeps entries in that field. Each target `αUv_iU*` is in the convex hull of the grid vertices.

Carathéodory supplies a representation using at most `D+1=d²` vertices. A deterministic compiler enumerates subsets of `D+1` vertices, solves the augmented barycentric linear system exactly, and chooses the first solution with nonnegative coefficients. A smaller affine-independent support can be extended to a full affine basis with zero coefficients, so this enumeration succeeds even for boundary targets. The polytope has full affine dimension because it contains `αD_d` with α>0.

For fixed d, all these systems have fixed size; exact arithmetic and sign comparisons in `Q(√5)` cost polynomially many operations in the coefficient bit length. Determinant bounds give `O_d(log(T+2))` bits per coefficient. A conservative construction budget is

\[
 O_d(|A_d|N_T^{D+2}\operatorname{poly}(\log(T+2)))
\]

bit operations and `O_d(|A_d|N_T log(T+2))` output bits. This is deliberately inefficient but terminating, finite, and polynomial in T for every fixed d. No optimal compiler-time claim is made.

Preparation from an explicitly given rational/algebraic density matrix is a similar barycentric problem, with its input bit length charged. An unknown quantum state supplied only through samples does not provide that matrix for free.

### 9.3 Sampling and arithmetic

Rows have at most d² nonzero algebraic probabilities. Exact sampling is possible under ideal independent fair bits: refine a uniform binary fraction and certified intervals for the finitely many cumulative thresholds until the fraction interval is separated from all thresholds. Equality occurs with probability zero; the probability of requiring p additional binary digits is O_d(2^−p). Algebraic interval arithmetic in the fixed field gives expected polynomial bit cost in the stored row length. This is expected, with unbounded refinement, rather than a worst-case finite random-bit guarantee.

For rational terminal effects, (27) is rational after computing the rational power `α^{−(t+1)}`. A conservative exact implementation spends `poly_d(T,B)` bit operations and `poly_d(T,B)` temporary bits, where B is the effect's input bit length. The exact rational power can have `O(T log(T+2))` bits. These working bits and the read-only program are distinct from the dynamic m-state register, and must be included in a total-implementation comparison. They are not being called free memory.

The comparison assumes the gate labels and preparation/readout parameters are supplied. It proves no low-cost empirical acquisition procedure for a physical quantum device or a gate gap. If actual unitary implementations have operator-norm errors δ_t, telescoping gives a probability error at most `Σ_tδ_t`; thus finite physical precision requires an additional error budget. The exact mathematical state theorem itself does not supply hardware precision, stability, or energy costs.

## 10. A second exact consequence: memory versus numerical range

For arbitrary time-varying grids `W_t={w_{t,1},…,w_{t,m}}⊂V`, suppose every row-stochastic gate update is exactly unbiased:

\[
 \sum_jP_t(g)_{ij}w_{t,j}=g w_{t-1,i}.
\]

Let `B_t=max_i||w_{t,i}||`, `B_0>0`. Duality and (22) imply

\[
 \frac{B_T}{B_0}\ge\frac1{F_m(T)}
 \ge\frac12\exp[c_{d,\nu}T/m^{1/(d-1)}].
 \tag{31}
\]

This covers arbitrary grids, stochastic encoders, and time-dependent kernels. A single large coordinate on a rare state counts in B.

Conversely, use the net construction with at most m vertices and damping
`α≥1−4dm^{−1/(d−1)}`. Set `W_t=α^{−t}W`. The same stochastic rows become exactly unbiased and

\[
 B_T/B_0=\alpha^{-T}
 \le\exp[8dT/m^{1/(d-1)}]
\]

once `4dm^{−1/(d−1)}≤1/2`. Hence the range exponent is sharp. This is a theorem about positive finite-state stochastic interpolation, not arbitrary signed arithmetic or typical Monte Carlo variance.

## 11. Varying memory and a sharp dynamic bit-time law

The controller may have a different state count `m_t` at each time t. Large allocations at isolated times cannot repair earlier loss. This follows without applying a fixed-size block bound.

Suppose `X_0` has norm at most one and the terminal retained mean satisfies

\[
 \mathbb E\|\mathbb E[X_T\mid S_T]\|\ge r_0>0.
\]

Then `M_T≥r_0²`. This consequence already holds under the general hypotheses (4)–(5), with

\[
 \#\{0\le t<T:m_t\le M\}
 \le\frac{256D}{\beta a^2}
 \left(\frac{16CM}{a}\right)^{2/k},\qquad a=r_0^2,
\]

and hence `Σ_tlog₂m_t≥(k/2)log₂(T!)−O_{D,C,k,ν,r_0}(T)`. For conjugation on traceless d-level matrices, set `a=r_0²` and, for each threshold integer `M≥1`, use the single Gaussian scale from (13) with m replaced by M. Every step t whose input state count satisfies `m_t≤M` costs at least `βa/16` in that potential. All other steps still decrease the same potential by a nonnegative amount. Consequently

\[
 N_M:=\#\{0\le t<T:m_t\le M\}
 \le K_0 M^{1/(d-1)},\quad
 K_0=\frac{256D}{\beta a^2}
 \left(\frac{16C_d}{a}\right)^{1/(d-1)}.
 \tag{32}
\]

This bound does not depend on the sizes of the intervening large allocations. If `m_(1)≤⋯≤m_(T)` are the sorted input-state counts, taking `M=m_(j)` gives

\[
 m_{(j)}\ge(j/K_0)^{d-1}.
\]

Thus every such simulator satisfies

\[
 \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\log_2m_t
 \ge(d-1)\log_2(T!)-(d-1)T\log_2K_0
 =(d-1)T\log_2T-O_{d,\nu,r_0}(T).
 \tag{33}
\]

For the finite-preparation Born task of Section 7, one can take `r_0=1/(4dD)`. Its error threshold forces that retained correlation. Hence the leading dynamic bit-time coefficient in (33) is necessary even with arbitrary memory scheduling. The exact fixed-size upper bound uses
`Σ_t log_2m_t≤(d−1)Tlog_2(T+1)+O_d(T)`, so it matches the leading coefficient.

This measures the time integral of the gate-dependent state register. The fixed program, acquisition, and temporary arithmetic costs remain separately charged as in Section 9. Equation (33) is not a lower bound on the total implementation time of an unrestricted classical computer.
