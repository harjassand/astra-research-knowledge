Result: Gaussian rigidity and a positive alternative for arbitrary bounded observables
The strongest result from this investigation is a dimension-independent separation between two noise models for exact joint representations of noncommuting observables:
Gaussian noise permits the representation below exactly when the observables commute.
Explicit exponentially decaying noise permits it for every finite collection of bounded observables, whether or not they commute.

The positive construction has finite variance, moments of every order, an explicit operator-valued density, and positive finite approximations with a uniform error bound.
This concerns a precise task: one positive measurement whose every linear readout reproduces the corresponding linear combination of observables, with the prescribed additive noise. It is stronger than asking whether noisy versions of the individual coordinate observables are jointly measurable.
The proofs are given below and preserved in the accompanying note. I have not established the originality of these results or independently major foundational consequences. They are the strongest mathematical advance I can substantiate from this search, not a claim that the overarching mission is complete.

2. The exact task
Let \(A_1,\ldots,A_m\) be bounded self-adjoint operators on a complex Hilbert space. Finite Hermitian matrices are included.
Write
\[
A(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{m}t_jA_j.
\]
Let \(\nu\) be an ordinary probability law on \(\mathbb R^m\), with characteristic function
\[
\widehat\nu(t)=\int e^{it\cdot z}\,\nu(dz).
\]
We ask for a positive operator-valued probability measure, or POVM, \(M\) satisfying
\[
\boxed{
\int e^{it\cdot x}M(dx)
=
\widehat\nu(t)e^{iA(t)}
\qquad(t\in\mathbb R^m).
}
\tag{1}
\]
Here positivity means \(M(S)\succeq0\) for every measurable set \(S\), and normalization means \(M(\mathbb R^m)=I\).
For a quantum state \(\rho\), the classical output law is
\[
P_\rho(dx)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho M(dx)).
\]
Equation (1) says that, for every \(u\in\mathbb R^m\), the scalar output \(u\cdot X\) has exactly the distribution obtained by measuring
\[
A(u)=\sum_j u_jA_j
\]
and independently adding \(u\cdot Z\), where \(Z\sim\nu\).
The same measurement \(M\) must work for every state and every direction.
The two results
Theorem A — Gaussian rigidity.
For bounded self-adjoint \(A_1,\ldots,A_m\), equation (1) admits a POVM for a Gaussian noise law with any finite covariance matrix if and only if the operators commute pairwise.
This includes correlated Gaussian noise, arbitrarily large covariance, singular covariance, and infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces.
Theorem B — Positive exponential-tail regularization.
Put
\[
R=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\|A_j\|.
\]
For \(s>0\) with \(s\ge4R\), define
\[
q_s(x)=\frac{1}{\pi s}\operatorname{sech}(x/s),
\qquad
p_s(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{m}q_s(x_j).
\]
There exists an explicit POVM density \(M_s(x)\) satisfying
\[
\boxed{
(2-\sqrt2)p_s(x)I
\preceq M_s(x)
\preceq \sqrt2\,p_s(x)I
}
\tag{2}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
\int e^{it\cdot x}M_s(x)\,dx
=
\left[
\prod_{j=1}^{m}\operatorname{sech}
\left(\frac{\pi s t_j}{2}\right)
\right]e^{iA(t)}.
}
\tag{3}
\]
Thus the representation exists with independent hyperbolic-secant noise coordinates. Each coordinate has variance
\[
\frac{\pi^2s^2}{4}
\]
and exponentially decaying tails.
Consequently, two noise laws with exactly the same covariance can have opposite answers to the representation problem.
3. Why Gaussian noise fails
The obstruction is stronger than a Gaussian-specific calculation.
Independent-noise obstruction
Suppose equation (1) holds for two bounded observables \(A,B\), with independent noise coordinates \(X,Y\). Assume
\[
\mathbb E e^{rX}<\infty
\quad\text{for every real }r,
\qquad
\operatorname{Var}(Y)=v<\infty.
\]
Then
\[
[A,B]=0.
\]
The key is to reweight the proposed positive measure exponentially. Independence keeps the transverse noise variance fixed, while noncommutativity produces an exponentially growing negative covariance defect.
Step 1: Every exponential reweighting must remain positive
Let
\[
D_r=e^{rA},
\qquad
K_\ell(r)
=
D_r^{-1/2}
\left.
\frac{d^\ell}{dt^\ell}e^{rA+tB}
\right|_{t=0}
D_r^{-1/2},
\quad \ell=1,2.
\]
The first-coordinate marginal has all exponential moments. Analytic continuation of (1) in its first Fourier variable gives
\[
\int e^{rx+iuy}M(dx\,dy)
=
\mathbb E(e^{rX})\,\widehat\nu_Y(u)e^{rA+iuB}.
\]
Therefore
\[
N_r(dx\,dy)
=
\frac{
D_r^{-1/2}e^{rx}M(dx\,dy)D_r^{-1/2}
}{
\mathbb E(e^{rX})
}
\]
is again a positive operator-valued probability measure.
Writing \(\mu=\mathbb EY\), its first two \(y\)-moments are
\[
L_1=K_1+\mu I,
\qquad
L_2=K_2+2\mu K_1+\mathbb E(Y^2)I.
\]
Positivity of the integral of \((a+yb)^*N_r(a+yb)\) implies
\[
\begin{pmatrix}
I&L_1\\
L_1&L_2
\end{pmatrix}\succeq0.
\]
Taking the Schur complement yields the necessary inequality
\[
\boxed{
vI+K_2(r)-K_1(r)^2\succeq0
\qquad\text{for every }r.
}
\tag{4}
\]
Only a finite second moment of \(Y\) is needed. The twice differentiable characteristic function of the positive tilted measure implies that moment exists: apply Fatou’s lemma to \(2(1-\cos uy)/u^2\), then dominated convergence. This avoids silently assuming exponential moments for both coordinates.
Step 2: The defect is explicit for matrices
Diagonalize
\[
A=\operatorname{diag}(a_1,\ldots,a_d),
\qquad
d_{ij}=r(a_j-a_i).
\]
Differentiating the matrix exponential gives
\[
(K_1)_{ij}
=
B_{ij}\frac{2\sinh(d_{ij}/2)}{d_{ij}},
\]
and
\[
(K_2)_{ii}
=
2\sum_j|B_{ij}|^2
\frac{e^{d_{ij}}-1-d_{ij}}{d_{ij}^2}.
\]
Subtracting produces
\[
\boxed{
(K_2-K_1^2)_{ii}
=
\sum_j|B_{ij}|^2
\frac{2(\sinh d_{ij}-d_{ij})}{d_{ij}^2}.
}
\tag{5}
\]
The limiting value of each summand at \(d_{ij}=0\) is zero.
If \(A\) and \(B\) do not commute, choose an index \(i\) with the largest eigenvalue among indices coupled by \(B\) to a strictly lower eigenvalue. It cannot couple to a higher eigenvalue: that higher index would itself be coupled to a lower one, contradicting the choice.
Every nonzero contribution in (5) is therefore negative for \(r>0\), and at least one tends exponentially to \(-\infty\). No fixed \(v\) can satisfy (4).
For example, with
\[
A=
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
B=
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]
positivity would require
\[
\boxed{
v\ge
\frac{\sinh(2r)-2r}{2r^2}
\quad\text{for every }r>0.
}
\tag{6}
\]
The right-hand side diverges.
This is an exact counterexample for every finite noise variance, not a numerical failure to locate a suitable measurement.
4. Removing the finite-dimensional restriction
The matrix proof suggests an escalation: replace a highest coupled eigenvector by a pair of separated spectral regions. This works even when there are no eigenvectors.
Let \(E\) be the spectral measure of bounded \(A\). Define the spectral support of \(B\) relative to \(A\):
\[
S=
\left\{
(x,y)\in\sigma(A)^2:
E(U)BE(V)\ne0
\text{ for every open }U\ni x,\ V\ni y
\right\}.
\]
This is compact and symmetric. It lies in the diagonal exactly when \(A\) and \(B\) commute. To see the nontrivial direction, blocks across separated spectral sets vanish; increasing limits of separated sets then show that every spectral projection of \(A\) commutes with \(B\).
Suppose they do not commute. Let
\[
\delta=\max_{(x,y)\in S}|x-y|>0.
\]
Among pairs with \(a-b=\delta\), choose one with maximal high endpoint \(a\).
There cannot be a further supported coupling from \(a\) to \(a+\delta\), since that would give a larger high endpoint. By compactness, there is \(\gamma<\delta\), with \(\gamma>0\), and sufficiently small spectral intervals
\[
I=(a-\varepsilon,a+\varepsilon),
\qquad
J=(b-\varepsilon,b+\varepsilon)
\]
such that, writing
\[
P=E(I),\qquad Q=E(J),\qquad C=QBP,
\]
we have
\[
C\ne0,
\qquad
BP=E((-\infty,a+\gamma])BP,
\]
and
\[
g:=\delta-2\varepsilon>\gamma+\varepsilon.
\tag{7}
\]
The support assertion follows by covering any compact rectangle disjoint from \(S\) by finitely many zero-block rectangles and refining the spectral partitions.
Now
\[
K_1(r)=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}e^{urA}Be^{-urA}\,du.
\]
For \(K=QK_1(r)P\), differentiation under the integral gives
\[
r(A_QK-KA_P)
=
e^{rA_Q/2}Ce^{-rA_P/2}
-
e^{-rA_Q/2}Ce^{rA_P/2}.
\]
The first term on the right has norm at most \(e^{-rg/2}\|C\|\). The second has norm at least \(e^{rg/2}\|C\|\), by the spectral gap and the bounds on the inverses of the exponential factors. Hence
\[
\boxed{
\|QK_1(r)P\|
\ge
\frac{\|C\|\sinh(rg/2)}{r\|A\|}.
}
\tag{8}
\]
For the second derivative, the exponential differentiation formula gives
\[
K_2(r)=
2\int_{\substack{t_1,t_2,t_3\ge0\\t_1+t_2+t_3=1}}
e^{(t_1-1/2)rA}
B e^{t_2rA}B
e^{(t_3-1/2)rA}\,dt_1dt_2.
\]
After compression to \(P\), the middle factor has norm at most
\[
\|B\|^2e^{t_2r(a+\gamma)}.
\]
The two outer factors contribute at most \(e^{-t_2ra+r\varepsilon}\). Integrating over the simplex therefore yields
\[
\boxed{
\|PK_2(r)P\|
\le
\|B\|^2e^{r(\gamma+\varepsilon)}.
}
\tag{9}
\]
But (4) would force
\[
\|QK_1(r)P\|^2
\le
v+\|PK_2(r)P\|.
\]
Equations (7)–(9) contradict this as \(r\to\infty\): the left-hand side grows like \(e^{rg}/r^2\), with a strictly larger exponential rate.
This proves the independent-noise obstruction for all bounded self-adjoint operators.
Finally, diagonalize a Gaussian covariance by an orthogonal change of output coordinates. The transformed noises are independent Gaussians, so every transformed observable pair commutes. Inverting the coordinate change proves that the original tuple commutes. Conversely, convolving the joint spectral measure of a commuting tuple with Gaussian noise supplies (1).
That proves Theorem A.
5. Constructing a positive representation instead
The failed Gaussian approach suggests what must change: control derivatives relative to the noise density itself, uniformly throughout the tails.
For
\[
q_s(x)=\frac{1}{\pi s}\operatorname{sech}(x/s),
\]
define polynomials
\[
P_0(u)=1,
\qquad
P_{k+1}(u)=(1-u^2)P_k'(u)-uP_k(u).
\]
Then
\[
q_s^{(k)}(x)
=
s^{-k}q_s(x)P_k(\tanh(x/s)).
\]
Since \(\deg P_k\le k\), their coefficient sums satisfy
\[
\|P_{k+1}\|_{\ell^1}
\le
(2k+1)\|P_k\|_{\ell^1}.
\]
Thus, with \(c_k=(2k-1)!!\) and \(c_0=1\),
\[
|q_s^{(k)}(x)|
\le
c_ks^{-k}q_s(x).
\tag{10}
\]
For the product density \(p_s\), every derivative of total order \(k\) consequently obeys
\[
\left|
\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_k}p_s(x)
\right|
\le
c_ks^{-k}p_s(x).
\tag{11}
\]
Here we use \(c_ac_b\le c_{a+b}\).
Now define
\[
\boxed{
M_s(x)=
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!}
\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_k=1}^{m}
A_{i_1}\cdots A_{i_k}
\,\partial_{i_1}\cdots\partial_{i_k}p_s(x).
}
\tag{12}
\]
The zeroth term is \(p_s(x)I\). The \(k\)-th term has norm bounded by
\[
p_s(x)\frac{c_k}{k!}(R/s)^k.
\]
The generating function is
\[
\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{c_k}{k!}z^k
=
(1-2z)^{-1/2}.
\]
Therefore \(s\ge4R\) implies
\[
\|M_s(x)-p_s(x)I\|
\le
(\sqrt2-1)p_s(x).
\]
Each complete \(k\)-th summand is self-adjoint: adjoints reverse the operator word, and the reversed word appears with the same real derivative. We obtain
\[
(2-\sqrt2)p_s(x)I
\preceq M_s(x)
\preceq\sqrt2p_s(x)I.
\]
This proves positivity at every point, including arbitrarily remote tails.
The series converges absolutely in operator-valued \(L^1\). Every positive-order derivative integrates to zero, so
\[
\int M_s(x)\,dx=I.
\]
Under the Fourier convention in (1), integration by parts transforms the \(k\)-th term into
\[
\widehat p_s(t)\frac{i^k}{k!}A(t)^k.
\]
Summing gives
\[
\widehat p_s(t)e^{iA(t)}.
\]
Finally,
\[
\widehat q_s(t)
=
\operatorname{sech}\left(\frac{\pi st}{2}\right),
\]
which follows by substituting \(y=e^{2x/s}\) and evaluating the resulting beta integral. This proves Theorem B.
The decisive difference from Gaussian smoothing is now explicit:
\[
\boxed{
\text{The positive construction controls every derivative relative to the density, uniformly in }x.
}
\]
A Gaussian density does not satisfy such a bound: its relative derivatives grow polynomially with \(x\). The no-go theorem shows that, in this case, the failure is not merely a limitation of the construction.
6. Finite, positive approximations
The construction is not only an infinite formal expression.
Let \(M_{s,N}\) be (12) truncated at order \(N\). The same triangle bound proves
\[
(2-\sqrt2)p_sI
\preceq M_{s,N}
\preceq\sqrt2p_sI
\]
for every \(N\ge0\), and
\[
\int M_{s,N}(x)\,dx=I.
\]
Moreover,
\[
\frac{c_k}{k!}
=
2^{-k}\binom{2k}{k}
\le2^k.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
\|M_s(x)-M_{s,N}(x)\|
\le
2^{-N}p_s(x).
}
\tag{13}
\]
For every state, the resulting probability laws have total-variation distance at most
\[
2^{-N-1}.
\]
Every linear projection inherits this bound, uniformly over the direction. The truncation also reproduces the exact noisy operator moments of total degree at most \(N\).
For explicit matrices, ordered products can be grouped by multiindices:
\[
S_0=I,
\qquad
S_\alpha=\sum_{j:\alpha_j>0}A_jS_{\alpha-e_j}.
\]
There are \(\binom{m+N}{N}\) such objects up to degree \(N\). This supplies a finite density-evaluation procedure, but not a polynomial-time algorithm jointly in arbitrary \(m\) and \(N\).
The numerical checks accompanying the note tested the exact Pauli covariance formula against block-exponential derivatives and tested finite positive truncations at central and far-tail points. They found no discrepancy. Those checks supplement the proofs; they do not independently verify them.
7. What this establishes—and what it does not
The result establishes a complete Gaussian classification for the stated interface and a universal positive alternative for arbitrary bounded tuples. It is not restricted to qubits, a particular matrix algebra, or an approximately commuting family.
It also identifies a real structural distinction:
\[
\text{same covariance}
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\text{same exact joint-representation capability}.
\]
For independent noises, one coordinate having exponential moments at every real parameter is incompatible with noncommutativity when the transverse noise has finite variance. The successful hyperbolic-secant noise has exponential moments only for
\[
|r|<1/s.
\]
Thus its tail behavior is not incidental.
However, several boundaries remain essential.
This is an exact-representation theorem, not a uniform approximation barrier. Returning sufficiently broad Gaussian noise while ignoring the state already approximates the noisy law of each bounded \(A(u)\). For unit \(u\), the total-variation error is at most
\[
\frac{\|A(u)\|}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}},
\]
by conditioning on the spectral value and bounding the effect of translating a Gaussian. That error tends to zero as \(\sigma\) grows.
Boundedness matters. The result does not contradict Gaussian phase-space measurements for canonical, unbounded position and momentum. Bosonic Gaussian observables belong to a different setting. arXiv
Positive representation does not imply efficient recovery of noise-free quantum data. To undo the noise in a characteristic-value estimator, one divides by
\[
\widehat p_s(t),
\]
introducing the factor
\[
\prod_j\cosh\left(\frac{\pi st_j}{2}\right).
\]
This grows exponentially with frequency. Neither state acquisition nor general measurement implementation becomes free.
