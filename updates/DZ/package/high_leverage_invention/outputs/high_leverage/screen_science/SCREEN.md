# Focused primary-source screen: high-leverage science/computation openings

Date screened: 2026-10-12. This is an opportunity screen, not a novelty claim or endorsement of any reported scientific claim beyond its stated evidence. Rank is by combination of consequence, demonstrated capability, and a crisp next mathematical interface.

## 1. Adaptive enzyme engineering under sequence, assay, and construction constraints — strongest lead

**Demonstrated system and interface.** Singh et al. report a closed-loop platform using ESM-2 and EVmutation to initialize variants, an automated iBioFAB build/test pipeline, and low-N supervised models for subsequent rounds. It accepts an input protein sequence and a quantified fitness assay. For AtHMT, the campaign constructed 482 mutants; 70/74 sampled constructs had the intended mutations. For YmPhytase, it constructed 448 mutants; 20/21 sampled constructs were correct. The paper reports four rounds in four weeks, about 90-fold improvement in substrate preference and 16-fold ethyltransferase activity for AtHMT, and 26-fold activity improvement at neutral pH for YmPhytase. Each round uses one extra mutation layered onto the previous round; adjacent simultaneous mutations can require new primers. The authors say the low-N predictor correlates weakly with observed fitness, ESM-2 can be poor on some proteins, EVmutation needs homologs, and extension beyond two enzymes remains untested. This is a meaningful physical interface: sequences proposed → variants constructed → crude-lysate functional assay → next batch.

**Exact opening.** Treat each assay as a noisy, costly observation of an unknown sequence fitness function over a constrained mutation graph. Develop a sequential design rule with explicit accounting for variant synthesis, sequence verification, assay cost, and construction reachability. The mathematically interesting regime is structured but not fully known epistasis: what sparsity/order, smoothness, or local-factor assumptions are sufficient and necessary for identifying a high-fitness path without exhaustively sampling combinations? A useful theorem would give a minimax query bound and an adaptive procedure under those assumptions, plus a failure certificate when the assumptions are violated.

**Sharp test.** Preregister a held-out enzyme and assay; count all designed, attempted, failed, verified, and assayed variants. Under a fixed budget of at most 500 total constructs, require a ≥10× activity gain over wild type, independently re-expressed and re-assayed, and compare against same-budget random, single-mutant/greedy, and conventional Bayesian-optimization controls. Replicate across at least three held-out enzymes before claiming generality. Report both gain and assay/construct cost; sequence-prediction accuracy alone does not count.

**Primary source.** Singh et al., “A generalized platform for artificial intelligence-powered autonomous enzyme engineering,” *Nature Communications* 16, 5648 (2025), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-61209-y. Exact campaign and limitations: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61209-y

## 2. Rare-event path sampling beyond low-dimensional Brownian benchmarks

**Demonstrated system and interface.** FlowRES uses unsupervised normalizing flows to propose nonlocal path moves and sample transition-path ensembles without supplied collective variables, pre-generated path samples, or equilibrium-only assumptions. The primary paper tests passive/active Brownian particles on 2D potentials, including a two-route landscape and barriers; one reported transition-path ensemble took about 7 hours and was 7–17× faster than direct integration. The paper’s claim that efficiency does not fall as events become rarer is demonstrated in these model systems. This has broad reach because rare pathways govern kinetics in chemistry, biology, active matter, and reliability, but the reported evidence is not atomistic molecular simulation.

**Exact opening.** Port path-space proposals to high-dimensional atomistic systems with expensive force evaluations and competing mechanisms, while preserving the physical transition-path ensemble and path-time statistics. The core mathematics is a proposal/acceptance construction with tractable density ratios and stable learning under rare-event weights; the practical issue is amortizing training and proposal cost against the force-call budget. Do not assume a supplied committor or reaction coordinate.

**Sharp test.** On a blinded atomistic benchmark with at least two reactive channels and a reference ensemble, require (i) total-variation or prespecified path-observable error ≤0.05 for channel probabilities and path-time distributions, with uncertainty calibrated by independent runs, and (ii) at least 10× fewer force evaluations than direct dynamics at matched error. Increase the barrier systematically and report whether cost is approximately barrier-independent. Any result should count neural training, rejected proposals, and force calls and check that route populations are not lost.

**Primary source.** Asghar et al., “Efficient rare event sampling with unsupervised normalizing flows,” *Nature Machine Intelligence* 6, 1370–1381 (2024), https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00918-3. The paper’s own tested model class and scaling claim: https://www.nature.com/articles/s42256-024-00918-3

## 3. Autonomous synthesis with interventions selected from observed failure mechanisms

**Demonstrated system and interface.** The A-Lab integrates literature-trained recipe proposals, robotic powder dosing/heating/XRD, automated diffraction interpretation, and active learning for inorganic synthesis. It reports synthesis of 36/57 targets in 17 days; 353 recipes produced target phases about 30% of the time. The authors identify 11 of 17 failed targets as limited by sluggish kinetics and note that longer heating, higher temperature, better precursor mixing, or regrinding were outside the active learner’s action set. The paper also reports four targets inconclusive from XRD alone and notes multi-phase mixtures can be hard to identify. This makes the bottleneck identifiable: the learner optimizes within an incomplete action/measurement interface, and the observation can ambiguously map mixtures to a target label.

**Exact opening.** A sequential intervention learner for chemical synthesis must distinguish (a) a bad route, (b) an insufficient kinetic budget, and (c) a measurement ambiguity, while choosing which intervention or orthogonal assay to buy next. Formalize it as partially observed reaction-network control with costly interventions and imperfect phase-composition observations. The important result would be an identifiability condition and a sample-complexity bound for deciding among these failure classes, not another optimizer over the same fixed recipe coordinates.

**Sharp test.** On preregistered targets with blinded failure modes, compare fixed-action optimization to a learner allowed to select among time/temperature/regrind/mixing/precursor changes and an orthogonal phase/composition assay. At matched total instrument-hours, require ≥20 percentage-point improvement in independently confirmed target-majority yield, and fewer than 5% false-positive target calls on mixtures/near-neighbour phases. Confirm composition and phase by a method independent of the learner’s XRD classifier. Keep failed and ambiguous outcomes in the denominator.

**Caution and primary source.** This is a platform lead, not a clean scientific success baseline: manual re-refinement in the paper confirms target contribution in 36 of its stated successes, but not necessarily high purity; four more cases are inconclusive from XRD alone. Szymanski et al., “An autonomous laboratory for the accelerated synthesis of inorganic materials,” *Nature* 624, 86–91 (2023), https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06734-w. Article and reported failure modes: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w

## Recommendation to root

Start with **#1** if the mission is a new theorem plus a runnable experiment: the action space, measured objective, construction failures, and finite campaign scale are unusually concrete. Use #2 if the goal is a high-risk computational primitive and the team can reach atomistic force-field benchmarks. Treat #3 as a narrower robust-control/measurement problem; the scientific validation interface must be improved before optimization claims can carry much weight.
