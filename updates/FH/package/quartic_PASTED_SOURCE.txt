1. The object that changed the problem
Let \(F:\mathbb R^{2\times2}\to\mathbb R\) be a homogeneous quartic. For a mean-zero periodic gradient
\[
X=D\varphi,
\]
write normalized spatial averages as \(\mathbb E\), and define
\[
C=\mathbb E[\operatorname{vec}(X)\operatorname{vec}(X)^T],
\qquad
H=\mathbb E[D^2F(X)],
\]
\[
b=\mathbb E[DF(X)],
\qquad
c=\mathbb E[F(X)].
\]
The Hessian here is evaluated at the perturbation gradient \(X\), not at \(A_0+X\).
Quartic polarization gives the exact identity
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb E[F(A_0+X)-F(A_0)]
=
c+b:A_0+\frac12 A_0:H A_0.
}
\tag{1}
\]
Thus the entire dependence on the affine background is quadratic. Compatibility also supplies
\[
\mathbb EX=0,
\qquad
\mathbb E\det X=0.
\tag{2}
\]
Equation (1) alone does not advance quasiconvexity. Its potential difficulty is that a singular \(H\) might leave a cubic coupling \(b:A_0\) uncontrolled.
The first theorem rules out that mechanism—and proves substantially more than cancellation of the averaged cubic term.
2. A nullspace-closure principle without rotational symmetry
Theorem A — One-quadratic-constraint closure
Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional real vector space, let \(q\) be an indefinite quadratic form, and let \(F\) be a homogeneous quartic satisfying
\[
D^2F(A)[R,R]\ge0
\quad\text{whenever }q(R)=0.
\]
For every probability law \(\nu\) with finite fourth moment and
\[
\int q(X)\,d\nu(X)=0,
\]
the averaged Hessian
\[
H_\nu=\int D^2F(X)\,d\nu(X)
\]
is positive semidefinite. Moreover,
\[
\boxed{
A\in\ker H_\nu
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
DF(X)[A]=0
\quad\text{for }\nu\text{-almost every }X.
}
\tag{3}
\]
For planar matrices, take \(q=\det\). Its null cone is exactly the rank-one cone. Consequently, this theorem applies to every rank-one-convex homogeneous planar quartic, including the full 35-coefficient space.
It requires neither rotational symmetry nor a heat flow. It also does not assume that the law is a gradient law: the quadratic moment identity is sufficient for this particular conclusion.
Proof: how a quadratic equality forces a cubic identity
Homogeneous quartics satisfy the reciprocity identity
\[
D^2F(A)[X,X]=D^2F(X)[A,A].
\tag{4}
\]
Fix \(A\). The quadratic form
\[
Q_A(X)=D^2F(A)[X,X]
\]
is nonnegative on \(q(X)=0\). The indefinite version of the non-strict Finsler lemma therefore gives
\[
Q_A(X)=P_A(X)+\lambda_Aq(X),
\qquad P_A\succeq0.
\tag{5}
\]
This quadratic ingredient is established mathematics, not a new theorem of this investigation. Its indefiniteness hypothesis matters. arXiv
Averaging (5) and using (4) yields
\[
A:H_\nu A=\int P_A(X)\,d\nu(X)\ge0.
\]
Thus \(H_\nu\succeq0\).
Suppose now \(A\in\ker H_\nu\). Then \(P_A(X)=0\) almost everywhere, so the law is supported on
\[
U=\ker P_A.
\]
For every \(R\in U\) with \(q(R)=0\),
\[
D^2F(A)[R,R]=0.
\]
But
\[
t\longmapsto D^2F(A+tY)[R,R]
\]
is a nonnegative quadratic polynomial. Its derivative at its zero must vanish:
\[
D^3F(A)[Y,R,R]=0
\qquad\text{for every }Y.
\tag{6}
\]
Introduce the cubic
\[
\mathcal C_A(X)=DF(X)[A].
\]
Quartic symmetry turns (6) into
\[
D^2\mathcal C_A(Y)[R,R]=0.
\tag{7}
\]
Here the geometry of one indefinite quadratic equation becomes decisive.
If \(q|_U\) is indefinite, every quadratic form vanishing on its null cone is a scalar multiple of \(q|_U\). Hence
\[
D^2\mathcal C_A(Y)|_U=\lambda(Y)\,q|_U.
\]
The scalar \(\lambda\) is linear in \(Y\). Symmetry of the cubic tensor forces it to vanish.
Explicitly, diagonalize \(q|_U\). For each coordinate \(k\), choose \(i\ne k\) with \(q_{ii}\ne0\), possible because an indefinite form has at least two nonzero diagonal entries. Then
\[
\lambda(e_k)q_{ii}
=
D^2\mathcal C_A(e_k)[e_i,e_i]
=
D^2\mathcal C_A(e_i)[e_k,e_i]
=
\lambda(e_i)q_{ki}
=0.
\]
Therefore \(\mathcal C_A=0\) on \(U\).
If \(q|_U\) is semidefinite, its zero mean forces \(q(X)=0\) almost everywhere. Equation (6), with \(Y=R=X\), directly gives \(DF(X)[A]=0\).
This proves (3), including degenerate restrictions of the determinant.
What this operation actually supplies
For centered laws,
\[
\boxed{b\in\operatorname{ran}H.}
\tag{8}
\]
There is no uncontrolled linear term along a zero quadratic direction. Equation (1) is constant along \(\ker H\), and its minimum is attained:
\[
\boxed{
\min_{A_0}\mathbb E[F(A_0+X)-F(A_0)]
=
c-\frac12 b^TH^\dagger b.
}
\tag{9}
\]
The minimization formula itself is elementary. The proved content is that the necessary nullspace coupling holds automatically, even pointwise on the law’s support.
This does not yet make the minimum nonnegative. Arbitrary determinant-balanced laws can still violate Jensen. Differential compatibility must do more.
3. A rigidity theorem over the entire nine-coefficient cone
Now impose left-\(SO(2)\) invariance, but retain every coefficient:
\[
\begin{aligned}
F(A)={}&A_cu^2+B_cv^2+Cuv+Ew^2+Guw+H_0vw\\
&+Lqu+Mqv+Nqw.
\end{aligned}
\tag{10}
\]
There is no assumption that \(L=M\), that \(N=0\), or that the input variables have quarter-turn symmetry.
Identify the output columns \(x,y\) with complex numbers \(x_{\mathbb C},y_{\mathbb C}\). Define
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal E=
\left\{
F(A)=\gamma(\det A)^2+
|\alpha x_{\mathbb C}+\beta y_{\mathbb C}|^4:
\gamma\ge0,\ \alpha,\beta\in\mathbb C
\right\}.
}
\tag{11}
\]
Every member of \(\mathcal E\) is polyconvex: one term is convex in the matrix entries, the other convex in the determinant.
The result is not merely that these easily recognizable functions are quasiconvex. It is that they are the only possible exceptions to the following full-cone rank gap.
Theorem B — Exact exceptional-class characterization
For a left-\(SO(2)\)-invariant rank-one-convex homogeneous quartic \(F\), the following are equivalent:
1. Some positive-semidefinite second-moment matrix \(C\), with zero determinant moment and \(\operatorname{rank}C\ge3\), has singular averaged Hessian \(H_F(C)\).
2. There are three linearly independent rank-one matrices \(R_1,R_2,R_3\) for which\[
   D^2F(R_1)+D^2F(R_2)+D^2F(R_3)
   \]
   is singular.
3. \(F\in\mathcal E\).
In particular,
\[
\boxed{
F\notin\mathcal E,\quad \operatorname{rank}C\ge3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_F(C)\succ0.
}
\tag{12}
\]
Here “three independent directions” means three independent directions in the four-dimensional matrix space, not merely three spatial frequencies.
The mechanism behind the classification
Choose a nonzero \(A\in\ker H_F(C)\). From (5),
\[
D^2F(A)[X,X]=P_A(X)+\lambda_A\det X,
\qquad P_A\succeq0.
\]
The zero averaged value implies
\[
\operatorname{ran}C\subseteq\ker P_A.
\]
Thus
\[
\operatorname{rank}C\ge3
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{rank}P_A\le1.
\tag{13}
\]
The essential classification lemma is:
If a nonzero background of a left-\(SO(2)\)-invariant rank-one-convex quartic admits a determinant-corrected positive Hessian remainder of rank at most one, then the entire quartic belongs to \(\mathcal E\).

That is a restriction on the complete polynomial, not just its Hessian at a sampled point.
For an invertible background, an input-coordinate change sends it to \(I\). Use the matrix basis
\[
I,\qquad
J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad
K=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
L_0=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
In these coordinates the determinant has matrix
\[
\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1).
\]
Put
\[
f=F(I),\qquad a=DF(I)[K],\qquad b=DF(I)[L_0].
\]
Homogeneity and output-rotation covariance force
\[
D^2F(I)=
\begin{pmatrix}
12f&0&3a&3b\\
0&4f&-b&a\\
3a&-b&*&*\\
3b&a&*&*
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{14}
\]
Comparing this with
\[
vv^T+\lambda\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1)
\]
forces \(a=b=0\) and leaves only three cases.
The case \(f<0\) is impossible: the positive remainder’s kernel contains \(I\), the determinant is indefinite on that kernel, and the cubic-closure identity would force
\[
DF(I)[I]=4f=0.
\]
For \(f>0\), the complete invariant quartic, in conformal/anticonformal coordinates \(z,w\), reduces to
\[
F=fq^2+g|w|^4+
\operatorname{Re}\!\left(\eta |w|^2z\bar w\right).
\]
The cubic identity on the forced kernel eliminates \(\eta\). Rank-one convexity gives \(g\ge0\), producing (11).
For \(f=0\), the residual form can be normalized to
\[
F=
2\beta_0[\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w)]^2
+\gamma_0|w|^4
+h|w|^2\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w).
\]
At background \(z=\mathrm iZ,w=\mathrm iW\), test the rank-one directions
\[
(\delta z,\delta w)=(\sigma,1),\qquad \sigma=\pm1.
\]
The second derivative is exactly
\[
2\left[
(4\beta_0\sigma+h)ZW+
(2\gamma_0+h\sigma)W^2
\right].
\]
Nonnegativity for arbitrary \(Z\) and both signs forces
\[
\beta_0=h=0,\qquad \gamma_0\ge0.
\]
Again, the polynomial belongs to \(\mathcal E\).
A rank-one distinguished background is handled separately: the low-rank Hessian forces a zero of the convex binary rank-one restriction. Its degeneration then forces all three orientation-sensitive terms to vanish in appropriate coordinates. The complete proof includes this case rather than assuming invertibility.
4. The resulting global positivity theorem
Theorem C — Jensen positivity on every singular averaged-Hessian face
Let \(F\) be any rank-one-convex homogeneous quartic in the full left-\(SO(2)\) cone. Let \(\varphi\) be a periodic \(W^{1,4}\) potential, or a \(W^{1,4}_0\) potential on a bounded Lipschitz domain.
If
\[
H=\mathbb E[D^2F(D\varphi)]
\]
is singular, then
\[
\boxed{
\int_\Omega
\bigl[F(A_0+D\varphi)-F(A_0)\bigr]\ge0
\qquad\text{for every constant }A_0.
}
\tag{15}
\]
This is an actual compatible-gradient inequality. It is not a successful acoustic SDP or a reformulation of moment completion.
The constructive proof has two branches
If \(F\in\mathcal E\), formula (11) gives the positive construction directly. Jensen controls the convex fourth power; preservation of the mean determinant followed by scalar Jensen controls its square.
If \(F\notin\mathcal E\), Theorem B forces
\[
\operatorname{rank}C\le2.
\]
The gradient therefore takes values almost everywhere in the fixed matrix subspace
\[
\mathcal L=\operatorname{ran}C,\qquad \dim\mathcal L\le2.
\]
Compatibility now becomes decisive.
If the determinant vanishes identically on \(\mathcal L\), every direction in that subspace is rank one. Hence \(F|_{A_0+\mathcal L}\) is convex.
If the determinant restricted to \(\mathcal L\) is semidefinite, its zero mean confines the gradient to its nullspace, at most a rank-one line.
The remaining case is a two-dimensional \(\mathcal L\) on which the determinant is indefinite. Its rank-one cone consists of exactly two lines, spanned by
\[
R_1=a_1\otimes b_1,\qquad R_2=a_2\otimes b_2,
\]
with independent input directions \(b_1,b_2\).
Fourier compatibility forces every coefficient of \(D\varphi\) onto one of these lines, with its frequency parallel to the corresponding input direction. Therefore
\[
D\varphi(x)
=
R_1g_1(k_1\cdot x)+R_2g_2(k_2\cdot x),
\tag{16}
\]
where the profiles have mean zero and \(k_1,k_2\) are independent integer directions.
The two phases are jointly Haar-uniform. Integrate first over one phase and apply rank-one Jensen, then over the other:
\[
\mathbb EF(A_0+D\varphi)
\ge
\mathbb EF(A_0+R_2g_2)
\ge F(A_0).
\]
There is no bound on the number of harmonics in either profile. Approximation handles \(W^{1,4}\), and extension by zero into a larger torus handles zero-boundary potentials.
Rank-one subspaces and low-wave laminate geometry have substantial prior history. The contribution of the current argument is the full-cone rigidity theorem that forces this geometry from singularity of the averaged Hessian. arXiv
The exception is sharp even for resonant gradients
The exceptional class is not an artifact of arbitrary matrix laws. Take
\[
R_1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
R_2=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad
R_3=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
They are independent, rank one, and trace-free.
The actual potential
\[
\varphi(x,y)=
\bigl(\sin y+\sin(x+y),\ \sin x-\sin(x+y)\bigr)
\]
has
\[
D\varphi=R_1\cos y+R_2\cos x+R_3\cos(x+y).
\]
For \(F=q^2+|w|^4\) in the conformal/anticonformal coordinates above, this field has covariance rank three and a singular averaged Hessian with \(I\) in its kernel. Both ranks were checked exactly.
Thus Theorem B needs its explicit exception, including for genuine resonant oscillations.
5. Why the previous heat mechanism cannot simply become universal
Bruno and Pasqualotto’s heat-flow/exact-form method remains the relevant prior framework for the earlier theorem. The current work does not claim to have invented it. arXiv
I obtained a broader obstruction than failure of a chosen Gram representation.
Consider
\[
F_\eta(A)=(\det A)^2+\eta|A|^4,
\qquad \eta=\frac1{10000}.
\]
It is polyconvex and uniformly quasiconvex:
\[
\int_\Omega[F_\eta(A_0+D\varphi)-F_\eta(A_0)]
\ge
\frac{\eta}{3}\int_\Omega|D\varphi|^4.
\tag{17}
\]
Nevertheless, no one fixed real constant-coefficient, uniformly strongly parabolic, second-order linear diffusion makes this energy decrease for every periodic gradient. This includes arbitrary output-matrix coefficients, not just a scalar heat metric.
An exact compatible witness
Take
\[
\varphi_1=\cos x+\cos y,
\]
\[
\varphi_2=
\frac{\cos2x+\cos2y}{2}
+\frac43[\cos(x+y)+\cos(x-y)].
\tag{18}
\]
Under ordinary heat, exact trigonometric averaging gives
\[
\left.\frac d{dt}\mathbb E(\det D\varphi_t)^2\right|_{t=0}
=\frac13,
\]
and
\[
\left.\frac d{dt}\mathbb E|D\varphi_t|^4\right|_{t=0}
=-\frac{34621}{81}.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\left.\frac d{dt}\mathbb EF_\eta(D\varphi_t)\right|_{t=0}
=
\frac{235379}{810000}>0.
}
\tag{19}
\]
This is a compatible field with a rigorously positive derivative, not a numerical certificate residual.
Why another fixed linear metric cannot restore universality
Suppose some constant-coefficient uniformly parabolic linear operator decreased the energy for every periodic field.
Because \(F_\eta\) is invariant under input and output orthogonal transformations, conjugating that operator by square-torus dihedral symmetries preserves the assumed property. The instantaneous energy derivative is linear in the operator’s coefficients, so their average also preserves it.
Output averaging removes anisotropic and skew output components. Input averaging then produces a positive multiple of the ordinary Laplacian. Uniform parabolicity ensures that the multiple is positive. This contradicts (19).
No exact differential form or integrated divergence can change that derivative.
This is not a counterexample to quasiconvexity. Equation (17) already proves quasiconvexity. It shows that a universal proof must allow something beyond monotonicity of the whole energy under one fixed linear diffusion—such as separate treatment of polyconvex components, nonlinear evolution, or genuinely nonlocal control.
Nor should a restricted polynomial lift be mistaken for polyconvexity itself: recent work explicitly distinguishes polynomial polyconvex functions from convex polynomial lifts. arXiv
