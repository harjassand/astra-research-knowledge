# Independent comparator screen: isotope-selective graphene rewriting

**Date:** 2026-10-15
**Screen:** Prior art, endpoint fit, and retaining alternatives for preferentially ejecting $^{12}$C from graphene, filling vacancies from a natural-abundance carbon reservoir, and repeating to enrich a local region after the membrane is made.
**Decision:** The component effects are established, and close post-growth isotope-exchange methods already exist. The exact combination of isotope-selective knock-on removal, controlled natural-abundance refill, repeated local enrichment, and retained device quality was not found in this screen. Treat it as an unverified process hypothesis, not a novelty or capability claim. The strongest demonstrated alternatives currently win for the endpoints with published performance.

## Prior-art screen

1. **Electron-beam isotope selectivity and refill are both in the microscopy record.** Meyer et al. measured knock-on displacement in ordinary and $^{13}$C-enriched graphene over 80–100 keV and dose, establishing that isotope-dependent removal is measurable. They report no vacancies in clean graphene at 80 keV even beyond $10^{10}$ e$^{-}$ nm$^{-2}$, while displacement grows rapidly around 95–100 keV; at 100 keV, imaging can alter the structure. Susi et al. later used isotope-dependent ejection to estimate local isotope content, with reported spatial mapping precision better than 20%. Their 2016 paper describes this as isotope analysis and discloses application EP16183371; the published application EP3282468A1 claims isotope-concentration determination from the accumulated dose up to atom loss. The patent example shows a vacancy made at 95 kV and filled by the next frame, with frames covering roughly 1 × 1 nm² and taking 2.2 s each. It does not identify the refill atom's isotope or source, nor show that repeated refill accumulates $^{13}$C. The 100 kV HRTEM point was underestimated because refilling was missed. Thus ejection selectivity and vacancy refill are prior observations, while their use as a controlled enrichment cycle remains unshown. [Meyer et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.108.196102), [Susi et al. (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms13040), [EP3282468A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP3282468A1/en)

2. **Post-growth isotope substitution already has a close CVD analogue.** Wang, Suzuki, and Hibino first grew full-coverage graphene, then sequentially introduced hydrogen and $^{13}$CH$_4$ on hot copper. They report hydrogen-assisted etching of the $^{12}$C lattice and deposition of $^{13}$C atoms, producing $^{13}$C-rich network regions at grain boundaries and other defects. This is a direct precedent for exchanging carbon isotopes after monolayer coverage. It uses an enriched carbon source and a catalytic, high-temperature copper surface; the reported result is a defect- and boundary-associated label useful for mapping graphene microstructure, not arbitrary atom-scale rewriting of a finished device. [Wang et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1039/C4NR03824J)

3. **Isotope domains and their mobility have been demonstrated.** Senga et al. grew a $^{12}$C domain in a crack of pre-existing $^{13}$C graphene, then used STEM–EELS vibrational spectroscopy to map isotope composition and follow diffusion during annealing at 600 °C. Their isotope maps show homogenization over approximately 100 nm regions after two hours. This supports nanoscale isotope-domain synthesis and measurement, while showing that thermal history can erase local patterns. The work is growth/annealing, not repeated electron-beam isotope replacement. [Senga et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-04405-w)

4. **The strongest demonstrated device-quality comparator is enriched graphite followed by exfoliation.** Iwakiri et al. made high-pressure/high-temperature graphite with measured $^{12}$C:$^{13}$C ratios of 99.7:0.3 and 8.6:91.4, exfoliated flakes at roughly 50 μm scale, and fabricated Hall bars with mobility at least $10^5$ cm² V$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$ and mean free paths of at least a micron. These are demonstrated electronic-quality endpoints for isotope-enriched graphene. This method requires enriched carbon feedstock and high-pressure/high-temperature synthesis, but the papers provide no comparable cost-per-device figure. [Iwakiri et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.5.043212)

5. **Focused isotope-ion-beam growth is a patent comparator, not a demonstrated result.** US8647436B2 describes filtering a carbon ion beam by isotope and rastering it over a catalyst to grow enriched graphene and planar or vertical isotope junctions. It is a relevant claimed route for spatial isotope patterning, but the patent itself is not experimental evidence that the stated resolution, enrichment, or device quality was achieved. The better demonstrated patterning comparator remains isotope-feed CVD, including same-sheet regions from sequentially switched methane sources. [US8647436B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8647436B2/en), [Chen et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmat3207)

## Endpoint-matched comparison

| Intended endpoint | Strongest retaining evidence | Fit of the proposed operation |
|---|---|---|
| **Electron-spin coherence / spin qubits** | Isotope purification is proposed to reduce the $^{13}$C nuclear-spin bath, and enriched $^{12}$C graphene devices with high mobility have been demonstrated. However, a 2014 $^{13}$C-versus-$^{12}$C graphene spin-valve study found hyperfine effects very weak and no resolvable hyperfine signature. | The proposed preferential sputtering enriches $^{13}$C, opposite to the usual isotope-purification direction for reducing nuclear spins. A distinct target such as placing nuclear-spin-active $^{13}$C registers would need a concrete graphene-qubit operation and readout demonstration; none is established by this screen. [Wojtaszek et al. (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.89.035417), [Iwakiri et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevResearch.5.043212) |
| **Thermal/phonon control** | Chen et al. prepared regions of a single sheet with isotope fractions from 0.01% to 99.2% $^{13}$C by sequentially changing methane feeds. They measured $^{12}$C graphene conductivity above 4,000 W m$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ near 320 K, more than twice the value for a 50:50 isotope mixture. | The candidate would need to show that a written patch or isotope boundary changes heat flow in a useful geometry while retaining its lattice. Existing CVD controls composition in the same sheet and already demonstrates the key composition-to-thermal-property link. A local electron-beam process has no reported thermal-device result or throughput advantage. [Chen et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nmat3207) |
| **Isotope labeling / nanoscale materials science** | CVD isotope switching, post-growth exchange on hot Cu, and STEM–EELS isotope maps already provide ways to make and study isotope distributions. | A possible incremental advantage is rewriting a selected region of an already fabricated membrane without a deliberately enriched refill precursor. ## Cost and quality accounting

No source found reports an endpoint-matched numerical cost comparison among these routes. The electron-beam candidate would need to charge for the microscope and vacuum setup, irradiation and imaging dose, cycle time, controlled carbon feedstock or contamination management, isotope readout, defect repair or annealing, transfer/cleaning, and usable-device yield. CVD comparators use enriched methane and hot copper; the HPHT route uses enriched graphite precursors and high pressure/temperature. These papers do not report comparable dollars per patterned area or per qualified device. **Relative acquisition, energy, time, throughput, yield, and cost remain UNKNOWN.** Microscopy dose or per-vacancy time must not be presented as a manufacturing-cost comparison.

Lattice/device quality is also **UNKNOWN for the proposed cycle**. Observing a vacancy refill proves neither that the replacement is substitutional $sp^2$ carbon nor that a sequence of cycles restores pristine structure, isotope fraction, transport, thermal conductivity, mechanical integrity, or membrane performance. The closest reports either characterize isotope composition or demonstrate quality of separately grown/exfoliated isotope-enriched material; none validates the candidate's full process on the same device before and after writing.

## Kill/hold decision and decisive discriminator

Do not advance the broad idea as a new capability. Prefer CVD isotope switching or isotope exchange for isotope-patterned graphene, and enriched graphite exfoliation for the currently demonstrated high-mobility device endpoint. A narrow engineering experiment is still testable if the claimed benefit is specifically post-growth, local $^{13}$C enrichment using only a natural-abundance reservoir. Its gate should require a pre/post isotope map with enrichment above the reservoir baseline, an isotope-resolved and provenance-known refill source, a control for non-knock-on e-beam chemistry and unwanted contamination, and same-sample defect plus endpoint measurements. The retaining comparison must use the same target size, isotope contrast, spatial resolution, final device or thermal performance, and complete preparation/measurement costs as the best CVD or exfoliation route. If enrichment only follows contamination deposition, cannot be mapped without destructive ejection, or fails the selected device endpoint, stop.

No blanket novelty finding is made: this is a scoped literature screen, not a patentability opinion or an exhaustive search.
