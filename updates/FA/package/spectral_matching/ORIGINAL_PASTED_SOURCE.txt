I chose solver-free control of transport in disordered interacting matter.
The relevant difficulty is not merely that a Hamiltonian has unknown parameters. In an interacting system, the energy mismatch associated with a particle hop depends on neighbouring occupations. A controller that is appropriate for one configuration can suppress transport in another. An explicit configuration-dependent control table can consequently be much larger than the local Hamiltonian description.
The benchmark cannot be “identify the entire model, then optimise.” Noise-assisted transport has already been demonstrated experimentally, including in a ten-qubit network. Strongly interacting transport under dephasing has been studied using matrix-product simulations. These are established capabilities, not discoveries of this investigation. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.122.050501)
Likewise, spatially correlated noise engineering and analytical optimisation of dephasing in specialised chains already exist. A new contribution must outperform or bypass a limitation of those direct methods, rather than rediscover that moderate noise can improve transport. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/1711.01025v2)
I considered three mechanisms before concentrating on the construction below. Trajectory reweighting did not produce a new way to acquire the required favourable path measure. Noise-assisted classical simulation changed the physical target without supplying an affordable correction. The surviving approach instead changes the physical control operation: the system’s interaction operator generates the configuration dependence automatically.

Consider a number-conserving hopping system
\[ H_\varepsilon=D+\varepsilon T, \qquad D|x\rangle=E_x|x\rangle. \]
Here \(D\) contains diagonal disorder and interactions, while \(T\) moves particles. The energy mismatch of an allowed hop is
\[ \Delta_{xy}=E_x-E_y. \]
Assume a fixed baseline dephasing width \(\eta>0\) for every single-particle hopping coherence. For hard-core particles, independent local dephasing
\[ \mathcal D_0(\rho) = \frac{\eta}{4}\sum_i(Z_i\rho Z_i-\rho) \]
has this property: a hop changes two occupation bits.
The proposed additional operation is a random phase generated by the actual diagonal operator:
\[ U_s=e^{-isD}. \]
Importantly, this is not modulation of the full Hamiltonian \(D+\varepsilon T\).
Choose a symmetric process \(X_t\) with independent increments and characteristic function
\[ \mathbb E e^{i\omega X_t}=e^{-t\psi(\omega)}, \]
where
\[ \boxed{ \psi(\omega)= \frac{32}{25} \left[ \sqrt{\left(\frac{15\eta}{4}\right)^2+\omega^2} -\frac{15\eta}{4} \right]. } \]
The resulting linewidth of a hopping coherence is
\[ \boxed{\gamma_{\mathrm M}(\Delta)=\eta+\psi(\Delta).} \]
Thus the same stochastic control signal produces different linewidths for different, potentially occupation-dependent energy mismatches.
The controller does not numerically evaluate \(\Delta_{xy}\). The physical operator performs that dependence.
That is also where the access cost resides. The device must genuinely couple the noise to \(D\), without multiplying the hopping by the same signal. Generic materials are not assumed to provide this capability.
The probability distribution is not new: the exponent belongs to the normal-inverse-Gaussian family. Fractional-generator constructions for completely positive quantum dynamics also have prior literature. [Springer Link](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s007800050032)
The needed validity can nevertheless be proved directly. With \(A=32/25\) and \(m=15\eta/4\),
\[ \psi(\omega) = \frac{A}{2\sqrt{\pi}} \int_0^\infty (1-e^{-s\omega^2})e^{-m^2s}s^{-3/2}\,ds. \]
For every \(s>0\), \(e^{-s\,\mathrm{ad}_D^2}\) is an average of unitary phases generated by \(D\). The positive integral therefore defines a completely positive phase process.
This avoids independently assigning favourable linewidths to transitions that might not be jointly realisable by any quantum channel.
At each fixed finite system size, the weak-hopping limit gives transition rates
\[ q_{xy}(\gamma) = 2\varepsilon^2|T_{xy}|^2 \frac{\gamma}{\gamma^2+\Delta_{xy}^2}. \]
These are the second-order kinetic rates. They are not asserted to be the exact finite-\(\varepsilon\) quantum dynamics.
Now give the comparator more freedom than an ordinary laboratory controller: let it choose a separate linewidth for every configuration edge, subject only to \(\gamma\geq\eta\).
Its optimal choice is
\[ \gamma_*(\Delta)=\max\{\eta,|\Delta|\}. \]
Let \(q_*\) denote these independently optimised rates. The proposed common noise law satisfies
\[ \boxed{ 0.97\,q_{*,xy} \leq q_{\mathrm M,xy} \leq q_{*,xy} } \]
for every allowed edge, every detuning and every finite configuration graph.
This comparator does not need to be physically implementable. It is an upper bound on all controllers whose effect, within this kinetic model, is to select dephasing widths while leaving coherent detunings and hopping amplitudes fixed.
Normalize \(x=|\Delta|/\eta\), and write
\[ g(x)=\frac{\gamma_{\mathrm M}}{\eta} = \frac{8\sqrt{225+16x^2}-95}{25}. \]
For \(0\leq x\leq1\), the rate ratio is
\[ R(x)=\frac{g(x)(1+x^2)}{g(x)^2+x^2}. \]
Set \(t=\sqrt{225+16x^2}-15\geq0\). The inequality \(R(x)\geq97/100\) reduces exactly to
\[ 2t^3+\frac{502547}{10000}t^2-\frac{4891}{200}t+3\geq0. \]
The cubic term is nonnegative. The quadratic part has minimum
\[ 3- \frac{(4891/200)^2}{4(502547/10000)} = \frac{200375}{8040752}>0. \]
For \(x\geq1\), put \(r=g(x)/x\). Direct minimisation gives
\[ \sqrt{\frac{688}{1125}}\leq r\leq\frac{32}{25}. \]
The rate ratio becomes \(2r/(1+r^2)\). Checking the endpoints proves the bound; for example,
\[ \frac{2(32/25)}{1+(32/25)^2} = \frac{1600}{1649}>0.97. \]
The other endpoint also exceeds \(0.97\), with an exact positive rational margin after squaring.
The guarantee therefore does not rest on numerical sampling.
The kinetic rates are symmetric, so the matched and oracle processes have the same uniform stationary distribution within a connected particle-number sector.
Their Dirichlet forms satisfy
\[ 0.97\,\mathcal E_*(f,f) \leq \mathcal E_{\mathrm M}(f,f) \leq \mathcal E_*(f,f). \]
Taking the appropriate variational infima transfers the comparison to reversible capacities and spectral gaps.
For transport, let \(d_{xy}\) be the signed displacement of a hop. A directional transport coefficient of the corresponding reversible Markov additive process has the form
\[ \mathcal C_q(v) = \frac12\inf_f \sum_x\pi_x\sum_y q_{xy} \left[ v\cdot d_{xy}+f(y)-f(x) \right]^2. \]
Consequently,
\[ \boxed{ 0.97\,\mathcal C_*(v) \leq \mathcal C_{\mathrm M}(v) \leq \mathcal C_*(v). } \]
The conclusion holds on every finite configuration graph, including the exponentially large configuration spaces of interacting exclusion systems. Neither selecting the control law nor establishing this comparison requires constructing that graph computationally.
For finite-range interactions, bounded local hop detunings and hopping amplitudes bounded away from zero, comparison with constant-rate exclusion also supplies a positive, volume-independent kinetic transport bound at fixed density. But that absolute lower bound is not itself a unique advantage: sufficiently strong uniform dephasing can also provide a standard comparison bound.
The distinctive candidate contribution is therefore narrower and more precise:
A common operator-referenced noise process performs nearly optimal configuration-adaptive broadening without calculating the configuration-dependent control table.
This does not establish monotonicity of arbitrary nonequilibrium currents or every individual first-passage time. It also does not establish finite-temperature chemical efficiency.
The construction is not merely one arbitrary parameter choice.

because \(1-\cos(4z)\leq16(1-\cos z)\); a Gaussian component obeys the same inequality.
Consider the two detunings
\[ \omega_1=\frac23\eta, \qquad \omega_2=\frac83\eta. \]
Writing \(t=\phi(\omega_1)/\eta\), split at \(t=69/1000\).
If \(t\) exceeds that value, the first transition is overbroadened, and its rate ratio is at most
\[ \frac{13897000}{14284849} = 0.972848925\ldots . \]
If \(t\) is smaller, the inequality above prevents enough broadening of the second transition; its ratio is at most

Thus

The explicit 97% construction is within 0.285 percentage points of that upper bound. This is not an optimality theorem against coherent control, asymmetric noise, changes to hopping or finite-memory drives.
The ideal process has infinitely many small jumps. Treating it as a free physical resource would invalidate the proposal.
Its Lévy density is
\[ \nu(s)=\frac{A mK_1(m|s|)}{\pi|s|}, \qquad \nu(s)\leq\frac{A}{\pi s^2}. \]
Let \(W\geq\eta\) bound the energy mismatch of an allowed local hop. Retain only phase pulses with \(a\leq|s|\leq b\), choosing
\[ a=\frac{\pi\delta(3/4)}{2AW}, \qquad b=\frac{8A}{\pi\delta\eta}. \]
The omitted small and large jumps together change each relevant linewidth by at most a fraction \(\delta\). The finite-pulse controller therefore retains
\[ \boxed{ q_{\mathrm{finite}} \geq 0.97(1-\delta)q_*. } \]
An explicit implementation is available at the mathematical level. Use a Poisson proposal clock with a \(1/s^2\) pulse-size envelope, then accept a proposed pulse with probability \(m|s|K_1(m|s|)\). No spectral decomposition or many-body loss table is required.
The proposal rate is bounded by
\[ \Lambda \leq \frac{4A^2W}{\pi^2\delta(3/4)}, \]
and expected absolute phase delivered per unit target time is bounded by
\[ \frac{2A}{\pi}\log\frac ba. \]
These costs depend on local detuning bandwidth, not the full extensive bandwidth of the many-body Hamiltonian.
That distinction has a strict scope: local cutoffs suffice for the kinetic rate theorem. They do not prove an approximation to the full many-body channel or finite-hopping current.
For the dimensionless test \(\eta=1\), \(W=10\), \(\delta=0.01\), the executed calculation gives approximately 885 pulse proposals and 4.714 units of absolute phase per target-time unit. These are model costs, not favourable specifications attributed to an existing device.
A bounded actuator needs physical time to deliver those phases. Switching, continuing background evolution, pulse overlap, clock precision and noise-generation power remain chargeable. A transport improvement per ideal evolution time is not automatically an improvement per laboratory time.
The required coupling can be checked locally only under additional controls. If the actuator couples to \(D+E\) rather than \(D\), then
\[ |\psi'(\omega)|\leq A \]
implies that a local detuning mismatch \(e\) creates linewidth error at most \(Ae\).
In a programmable finite-range device, conditional Ramsey experiments could acquire this error bound using the system’s own two-site coherences and prepared neighbouring occupations. This does not assume an isolated sensor, but it does require preparation, readout and the ability to disable hopping during calibration.
For a bond depending on at most \(k\) neighbouring bits, there are \(O(n2^k)\) local contexts—not exponentially many global configurations. The note charges a conservative shot bound and identifies preparation bias, drift and uncontrolled long-range tails as additional requirements.

If the same actuator can cancel \(D\), and the task allows that operation, every detuning becomes zero. Then

For \(|\Delta|\geq\eta\),

The advantage of cancellation can therefore grow without bound.

The uniform-dephasing optimisation used a numerical search and refinement, not a rigorously certified global optimiser. The edgewise theorem, rather than that numerical search, supplies the strong comparison within the fixed-detuning kinetic class.
Full finite quantum-generator calculations also approached the kinetic predictions at weak hopping. At \(\varepsilon=0.02\), the discrepancies were approximately \(0.0113\%\) and \(0.0063\%\). At larger hopping they grew. These are finite floating-point diagnostics, not evidence of a uniform thermodynamic theorem.
It would be tempting to claim that sufficiently weak hopping makes the kinetic approximation uniformly valid as the system grows. That assertion is false for general observables.
Take \(N\) independent hard-core dimers, one particle in each, with
\[ H=J\sum_{i=1}^N X_i, \qquad D=0, \]
and independent dephasing \(\eta\).
For one dimer, the \(Z\)-autocorrelation satisfies
\[ c''+\eta c'+4J^2c=0, \qquad c(0)=1,\quad c'(0)=0. \]
For the product observable \(Z_1\cdots Z_N\), independence gives correlation \(c(t)^N\). Since
\[ c(t)=1-2J^2t^2+O(t^3), \]
its integrated quantum correlation obeys
\[ \int_0^\infty c(t)^N\,dt \sim \frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt2\,J\sqrt N}. \]
The corresponding kinetic approximation instead gives
\[ \frac{\eta}{4NJ^2}. \]
Their ratio grows as
\[ \sqrt{2\pi}\frac{J\sqrt N}{\eta}. \]
Thus even fixed, small \(J/\eta\) does not yield a volume-uniform approximation for all zero-frequency observable responses.
This counterexample does not refute a theorem for local physical currents. It identifies what such a theorem must exploit: current locality and conservation, rather than a general approximation to the entire many-body generator.
That is a sharper mathematical requirement than “supply an efficient many-body solver.”
