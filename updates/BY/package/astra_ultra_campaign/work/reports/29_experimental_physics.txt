29. Causal ESR pump–probe test of superconducting-circuit flux noise

Finding

The microscopic origin of 1/f magnetic-flux noise in superconducting circuits remains unresolved at the level that matters for control: surface spins are strongly implicated, but the identity of the noise-bearing subset and the dynamics that turn its spin degrees of freedom into a broad 1/f spectrum are not pinned down. A useful next discriminator is a reversible intervention: continuously saturate an electron-spin-resonance (ESR) line while measuring the same device's low-frequency flux-noise spectrum. A resonance-locked, power-dependent redistribution of noise would causally connect an ESR-active spin population to the noise. This is a measurement proposal and a conditional mechanism calculation, not an observation or a new law.

What is established and what remains open

Weak in-plane field spectroscopy on a capacitively shunted flux qubit measured a change from approximately 1/f to Lorentzian noise below 10 Hz and a suppression above 1 MHz, for fields up to 100 G. The authors discuss spin-cluster growth and inhomogeneous broadening as possible mechanisms, and explicitly leave their interpretation open [1]. In separate work, on-chip ESR of Al2O3 surfaces resolved paramagnetic peaks attributed to physisorbed atomic hydrogen and molecular oxygen-derived defects, with total spin density about 2.2 × 10^17 m^-2, comparable to the density inferred from SQUID flux noise [2]. This links plausible surface species to the right order of spin density, but density agreement is not a demonstration that those measured spins produce a particular device's 1/f spectrum.

There is important adjacent prior art. A resonator study combined ESR, frequency-noise and loss measurements on the same device before and after thermal spin desorption; the measured low-frequency frequency-noise power fell by roughly a factor of nine [3]. That is stronger than an across-sample correlation, but desorption is an irreversible material intervention, it measured resonator frequency noise rather than a qubit's magnetic-flux noise, and it cannot reveal which relaxation dynamics generate the spectrum. A flux qubit has also been used for ESR with a reported sensitivity near 20 spins in a one-second measurement [4], while Ramsey-based methods directly measure low-frequency qubit noise [5]. The proposed experiment joins these capabilities through reversible resonant saturation. The field-dependent flux-noise paper itself suggests searching for passive resonant peaks at higher frequency, while noting inconsistent reproducibility; a driven pump–probe response asks a more causal question than passive peak hunting [1]. A targeted search found no report of this exact same-device, ESR-saturated low-frequency flux-noise measurement; that is not a claim of an exhaustive literature search.

Proposed measurement

Use a field-compatible flux qubit with a characterized flux-noise spectrum and a transverse RF field, and first map any ESR line of the nearby surface-spin bath using a companion resonator or the qubit sensor. At fixed in-plane bias B, apply a continuous RF pump near the calibrated line and interleave it in randomized order with an equal-power detuned pump and pump-off blocks. Keep the static field and qubit bias fixed. Measure S_Phi(f) with Ramsey/echo at low frequencies and dynamical-decoupling or spin-locking spectroscopy at higher frequencies; monitor qubit T1, qubit frequency, resonator loss, and local temperature during every block. Filtering and a separate drive line should keep pump leakage out of the qubit and flux-readout bands. A resonant response should track the ESR ridge as B changes, rather than merely tracking delivered RF power.

The frequency window is practical. For g = 2, nu_ESR = g mu_B B/h = 27.99 GHz/T × B. At 30 G, the target is 83.98 MHz. At 20 mK, k_B T/h = 416.7 MHz, so h nu_ESR/(k_B T) = 0.202 and the thermal spin polarization is tanh[h nu/(2 k_B T)] = 0.100. The equilibrium longitudinal variance is then 1 − P^2 = 0.990 of its unpolarized value: saturation changes this variance by only about one percent. This operating point makes a large noise response hard to explain as a trivial change in thermal spin variance. The 30 G bias also lies within the range already used in weak-field flux-qubit experiments [1]. The required RF amplitude should be set by measured saturation, not guessed from an assumed surface-spin T1 or T2.

Quantitative mechanism and identifiability

For one independent, symmetric two-state magnetic fluctuator with correlation C(t) = V exp(−Gamma |t|), its Lorentzian spectrum is proportional to

    S(f) = 2 V Gamma / (Gamma^2 + (2 pi f)^2).

An on-resonance drive adds spin transitions. In the simplest Bloch/Markov approximation, write Gamma_eff = Gamma(1+s), where s is the calibrated saturation parameter, scaling on resonance as s = Omega_R^2 T1 T2 and falling with detuning as s/[1 + (2 pi Delta T2)^2]. Omega_R is the spin Rabi angular frequency and Delta is pump detuning. At 30 G, the small equilibrium polarization above permits treating V as constant to about 1% for this estimate. The predicted spectral ratio for the addressed fluctuator is

    R(f) = S_drive(f)/S_0(f)
         = (1+s)[Gamma^2 + (2 pi f)^2] / [Gamma^2(1+s)^2 + (2 pi f)^2].

Thus low-frequency power falls by 1/(1+s), high-frequency power rises by 1+s, and spectral weight moves across the Lorentzian corner rather than disappearing. For s = 1 the response crosses near f = sqrt(2) Gamma/(2 pi). An ensemble with a broad distribution of Gamma can produce 1/f noise; pumping an ESR-selected subset should create a resonant, power-broadened change in its weighted spectrum. A field–frequency ridge near nu = 27.99 GHz/T × B, and a low-to-high-frequency transfer whose scale follows pump power, jointly discriminate spin dynamics from a smooth RF-heating effect.

The response amplitude also exposes the identifiability limit. Let eta be the fraction of baseline measured flux-noise power due to the addressed spins, and q the fraction of that noise-bearing contribution actually saturated. Then Delta S_total/S_total = eta q [R(f) − 1]. If s = 1 and f is well below the relevant corner, the ideal response is about −0.5 eta q. For example, if independent ESR calibration establishes q >= 0.5 and the experiment bounds any resonant change to 2%, this simple model bounds eta below about 0.08. That bound is conditional on the ESR-calibrated and noise-bearing subsets being the same and on the Markov response model. Without a calibrated q, a null result cannot rule out unsaturated, broad, or exchange-coupled spins.

The strongest falsifier is therefore precise but scoped: verify saturation of an independently resolved ESR line, then find no repeatable noise change at that line over a declared frequency band and sensitivity, despite unchanged qubit/readout diagnostics. This would refute the claim that the saturated ESR-visible subensemble supplies a specified fraction of that band's flux noise under the tested device and conditions; it would not refute all spin-bath explanations. Conversely, a resonant response alone would not identify the chemical species or prove a spin-glass model. Hyperfine structure, field-angle dependence, pump-power broadening, and recovery after turning the pump off would constrain those next questions.

Status and next gate

Achieved here: a sourced, feasible intervention; a minimal driven-fluctuator calculation; an explicit conditional bound; and a falsifier that separates the tested hypothesis from broader claims. No experimental data were collected and no novelty or law is established. Potential significance: a reproducible causal response could map a surface-spin ESR population onto a measured flux-noise band and distinguish rate-redistribution models from purely material-correlation explanations. The next gate is a focused literature check for any already-published resonant-saturation/noise protocol, followed by a realistic driven interacting-spin calculation that predicts the response after convolution with the qubit's flux-coupling kernel and estimates noise-estimator uncertainty under drift.
