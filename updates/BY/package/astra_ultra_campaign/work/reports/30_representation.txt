Target and interface

The barrier is the apparent conflict between (i) acquiring a long observation stream and storing its complete history, and (ii) computing exact predictions or a posterior over a finite hidden state. A finite-dimensional real state looks like a resolution, but its dimension alone does not say whether a physical device or finite-bit computer can maintain it.

Fix a finite alphabet Σ, an m-dimensional row probability vector α, and supplied nonnegative rational matrices M_a (a∈Σ) such that T=Σ_a M_a is row-stochastic. At each step, from hidden state i the system jointly emits symbol a and moves to state j with probability M_a[i,j]. The acquisition interface supplies the observed symbol a_t once, at step t. The model matrices and α are assumed known; identifying or calibrating them is outside the result. For a word w=a_1…a_t, write M_w=M_a1…M_at and z_w=αM_w. Then P(w)=z_w 1 is the acquired-prefix likelihood. The exact filter state is q_w=z_w/(z_w1), when P(w)>0. On acquiring a, update z←zM_a and normalize only when needed. The outputs are the terminal-state posterior q_wβ for a supplied readout effect β∈[0,1]^m, and next-symbol probabilities q_wM_a1. A batch u composes with later batch v by M_uv=M_uM_v; this is associative, so a stream can be chunked without storing its letters after updating the forward state.

This is an operational representation, not an assumption of free access: one physical symbol is still acquired per step. With dense matrices, the original positive filter takes O(m²) rational scalar operations per symbol and O(m) scalar registers; a sparse M_a costs O(nnz(M_a)). It replaces replay of the O(t log|Σ|)-bit transcript with a forward state, though exact rational coordinates can still carry linear-in-t information. If weights are rational with at most B bits and m, |Σ| are fixed, choose a common denominator D for all entries. The unnormalized integer numerator vector after t steps has entries at most D^t (because each M_a is nonnegative and ΣM_a is stochastic), so exact posterior coordinates can require Θ(t log D) bits each. Thus the arithmetic-operation count is not a bit-cost bound. A straightforward exact implementation stores O(m t log D) bits and performs growing-integer arithmetic; a model with real registers hides this cost.

Representation theorem and limitation

The finite-rank Hankel representation is the right algebraic summary, provided the Hankel object represents the requested outputs. For a scalar word function f, define H_f(u,v)=f(uv), with rows and columns indexed by finite words. The supplied m-state realization gives H_f(u,v)=αM_uM_vβ, hence rank(H_f)≤m. Conversely, over a field, finite Hankel rank r is equivalent to a real weighted-automaton realization of dimension r; r is the minimum real linear state dimension. This is the classical Carlyle–Paz/Fliess theorem, restated in modern primary work on weighted finite automata. For several requested scores, use the block Hankel matrix for the vector of outputs; the component scores P(w) and αM_wβ suffice to compute a terminal posterior by division when P(w)>0. A rank test on prefix likelihood P(w) alone can miss a terminal hidden-state posterior: in the example below P(w)=2^−|w| has rank 1, while the terminal readout score f(w)=αM_wβ has rank 2. Indeed, the submatrix with prefixes and suffixes {ε,1} is [[0,1/4],[1/4,3/16]], whose determinant is −1/16. The theorem gives exact composition and a sharp test for the output scores' real-register dimension. It does not guarantee that a minimal realization has nonnegative coordinates or stochastic transitions. Positive-realization theory separately studies this constraint. Benvenuti and Farina give explicit systems whose minimal positive dimension exceeds ordinary McMillan degree, and exhibit a low-inner-dimension positive Hankel factorization that does not suffice to yield a positive realization of that same dimension. A negative-weight minimal realization may be a useful algebraic compiler target, but is not itself a probability vector or a physical hidden-state mechanism.

The sharp precision falsifier is a two-hidden-state positive source. Order state mass as q=(x,1−x), start at α=(0,1), and set

T_0 = [[1/2,1/2],[0,1]],       T_1 = [[1,0],[1/2,1/2]],
M_0=T_0/2,                     M_1=T_1/2.

Each row of M_0+M_1 sums to one, and each symbol has conditional probability 1/2 from either state. After observing a, the posterior updates by q↦qT_a, hence x↦x/2 for a=0 and x↦(x+1)/2 for a=1. Induction gives, for w=a_1…a_t,

x_t = a_t/2 + a_(t−1)/4 + … + a_1/2^t.

As w ranges over {0,1}^t, x_t ranges bijectively over {k/2^t : 0≤k<2^t}. An external terminal readout with β=(1,0) reports P(Y=1|w)=x_t. Therefore exact posterior output after t acquisitions distinguishes 2^t possible answers and needs at least t bits of state/output in the worst case. The process is positive, rational, and has only two latent states; the lower bound is not caused by a growing hidden-state dimension. I checked the matrix update exactly with rational arithmetic for every word through length 8; the formula above is the proof for all t. This also shows why rank or “two real registers” is not a fixed-bit acquisition guarantee. It does not refute fixed absolute-error approximation: in this example old bits decay geometrically, so a fixed tolerance can discard sufficiently old input.

Falsifiers and significance

For a claimed r-dimensional exact linear primitive, any finite block of the target-specific Hankel matrix with rank >r refutes it. For a claimed k-state positive hidden mechanism, every such block must have nonnegative rank at most k, since its entries factor as nonnegative forward-state rows times nonnegative future-readout columns; positive Hankel factorization is necessary but, in general, not sufficient for a valid transition mechanism. For a claim of bounded b-bit exact memory, the two-state construction refutes it at any transcript length t>b when the terminal posterior must be returned exactly. These checks separate three costs: real linear dimension, positive physical state dimension, and transcript-dependent precision. They can be used before launching a stream-inference experiment to identify which “small state” claim is actually being made.

The basic Hankel theorem is established prior art, not a discovery here. Carlyle and Paz’s 1971 paper and Fliess’s 1974/1975 work are foundational; current weighted-automaton research continues to study approximate Hankel-rank reduction, including optimal spectral-norm minimization under stated assumptions. The contribution in this route is the explicit acquisition contract plus the exact positive two-state precision witness, and the insistence that these resource layers not be collapsed. This is a useful audit primitive for streaming scientific measurements, but it does not solve unknown-model acquisition, and is not evidence of a new general discovery capability.

Next proof gate

Prove a uniform finite-bit approximation theorem for the strictly positive subclass: assume every entry of every M_a lies in [η,1] for a feasible fixed η>0 and Σ_aM_a is row-stochastic. Construct an explicit grid filter with b=b(m,η,ε) bits, independent of transcript length, and prove that every predictive or terminal-readout probability differs from the exact filter by at most ε for every word. Charge model-weight precision and per-symbol bit operations. A valid proof must provide a uniform contraction modulus for q↦qM_a/(qM_a1), control rounding after every update, and exclude zero-probability symbols; otherwise produce a positive-matrix counterexample. Until that gate is proved, only the exact operational semantics and the linear-bit lower bound above are established.
