The proposed experiment asks something different:
Can two independently controlled lattice coordinates convert a closed structural cycle into a net displacement of mobile ions?
One coordinate would alter the relative energies of occupied sites. The other would alter the relative ease of leaving those sites through different neighbouring pathways.
The intended sequence is to favour transfer into an intermediate site, change which exit is accessible, and then restore the original lattice configuration. An ion could finish in the next equivalent cell even though the host distortion has vanished.
This is a hypothesis about controlling both site occupation and escape direction, not simply lowering every diffusion barrier.
For an inversion-symmetric bulk crystal, in the long-wavelength electric-dipole limit, a polar current is inversion-odd. A bilinear combination of two inversion-odd infrared modes is inversion-even.
Therefore, two rotating infrared modes do not, by themselves, supply the proposed bilinear bulk transport mechanism. They may generate other responses, but not the claimed polar current without an additional symmetry-breaking ingredient.
A potentially allowed combination is an inversion-even, Raman-like coordinate with an inversion-odd, infrared-like coordinate. Their product has the correct inversion parity. The full crystal point group must still permit the particular current direction.
This is a necessary symmetry test, not proof that suitable modes exist with sufficient coupling. Finding an infrared peak and a Raman peak is not enough.
I derived the following diagnostic model to determine what the proposed structural control must accomplish.
It is not a calibrated model of β-alumina. It assumes independent ions, Markov hopping, metastable sites and a common attempt prefactor. Those assumptions become particularly consequential at terahertz frequencies.
Let a cell have length \(a\), with sites \(A_n\) and \(B_n\) separated by \(a/2\). Set their energies to
\[ E_A=-\frac{u}{2}, \qquad E_B=+\frac{u}{2}. \]
Let the two intervening saddle energies be
\[ \mathcal B+v, \qquad \mathcal B-v. \]
Thus \(u\) changes the preferred site, while \(v\) changes the preferred escape pathway.
With
\[ \beta=\frac{1}{k_BT}, \qquad k=\nu e^{-\beta\mathcal B}, \]
the transition rates follow from these same site and saddle energies. They obey instantaneous detailed balance; no hidden chemical affinity is inserted.
If \(p\) is the probability of occupying the \(B\) sublattice, then
\[ \dot p=\lambda(u,v)\,[p_{\rm eq}(u)-p], \]
where
\[ p_{\rm eq}(u)=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta u}}, \qquad \lambda(u,v)=4k\cosh(\beta v)\cosh(\beta u/2). \]
Eliminating the common occupation imbalance from the two rightward edge currents gives
\[ \boxed{\dot x=-\frac a2\tanh(\beta v)\,\dot p.} \]
Consequently, over a closed occupation-and-control cycle,
\[ \boxed{\Delta x=-\frac a2\oint\tanh(\beta v)\,dp.} \]
Here \(x\) is the unwrapped position: it records passage into another lattice cell rather than only the final sublattice occupation.
This identity shows exactly what the mechanism needs. Occupation must change while the exit asymmetry has one sign, and change back while that asymmetry has another sign. Merely increasing the rate of otherwise symmetric hopping produces no displacement in this model.
For small harmonic controls,
\[ u(t)=u_0+U\cos\omega t, \qquad v(t)=V\cos(\omega t+\phi), \]
define
\[ p_0=\frac{1}{1+e^{\beta u_0}}, \qquad \lambda_0=\lambda(u_0,0). \]
The displacement component odd under phase reversal is, to leading bilinear order,
\[ \boxed{ \frac{\Delta x(\phi)-\Delta x(-\phi)}{2} = \frac{\pi a}{2} \beta^2p_0(1-p_0)UV \frac{\lambda_0^2}{\lambda_0^2+\omega^2} \sin\phi +\text{higher-order terms}. } \]
This provides a specific conditional signature: amplitude dependence, sign reversal and a frequency penalty.
Two qualifications prevent overinterpretation.
First, the complete finite-rate displacement also contains a phase-even term proportional to \(\omega\cos\phi\). The whole signal need not reverse under \(\phi\mapsto-\phi\). The experiment must isolate the odd component.
Second, when \(\omega/\lambda_0=10\), the odd displacement per cycle is reduced by a factor of \(101\) relative to its slow-drive limit at fixed amplitudes. Faster driving is not automatically better.
Actual lattice modes need not oscillate at the same frequency. For a pulse experiment, the appropriate calculation uses the measured trajectory in the integral identity—not two conveniently chosen sinusoidal amplitudes.
The measured hopping-memory time is not automatically \(1/\lambda_0\). Nor is a macroscopic conductivity activation energy automatically an elementary hopping barrier. The β-alumina experiments explicitly distinguish correlated microscopic motion from long-time transport. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06827-6)
Moreover, instantaneous activated rates may fail when the lattice changes on the timescale of a transition path.
Therefore, this formula cannot yet predict a potassium β-alumina signal. Application requires either demonstrated timescale separation or a model retaining the relevant correlated ionic configurations and nonadiabatic dynamics.

The principal quantitative improvement over independently assigning favourable parameters is to constrain the two controls through the same lattice energy landscape.
Let \(Q\) denote accessible lattice coordinates, and let their harmonic elastic energy be
\[ E_{\rm el}=\frac12Q^\top KQ, \]
where \(K\) is positive definite on the chosen coordinate subspace.
Suppose the leading changes in site and saddle energies are
\[ u-u_0=g^\top Q, \qquad v=h^\top Q. \]
Define
\[ A= \begin{pmatrix} g^\top\\ h^\top \end{pmatrix}, \qquad y= \begin{pmatrix} u-u_0\\ v \end{pmatrix}. \]
Minimising elastic energy subject to \(AQ=y\) gives
\[ \boxed{ E_{\rm el,min}(y) = \frac12y^\top \left(AK^{-1}A^\top\right)^{-1}y, } \]
provided \(A\) has row rank two.
The minimiser is
\[ Q_*= K^{-1}A^\top \left(AK^{-1}A^\top\right)^{-1}y. \]
This follows directly from constrained quadratic minimisation. Any other feasible displacement adds a nonnegative elastic-energy term.
Its physical consequence is that strong site control and strong directional-barrier control cannot be claimed independently of lattice stiffness.
If \(AK^{-1}A^\top\) is nearly singular, independent control is energetically expensive. If it is rank-deficient, arbitrary two-dimensional control is unavailable within that linear coordinate subspace.
Even this is only a lower bound. Optical selection rules, damping, field screening and attainable pulse shapes can make the required trajectory inaccessible.
