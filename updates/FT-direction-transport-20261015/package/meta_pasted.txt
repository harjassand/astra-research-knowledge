The amplitude reduction retains the global direction-field obstruction
Global amplitude minimization does not remove the remaining quasiconvexity difficulty. I established this more precisely than the previous joint-nonconvexity example.
First, the direction functional can be nonconvex after every amplitude has been globally minimized, even for the convex energy \(F(A)=|A|^4\).
Second, a quantitative translation-limit theorem shows that direction fields converging smoothly to a constant can retain an arbitrary component of the original vectorial problem. After global amplitude minimization, their minimum converges to the original energy minimized over the other Cartesian component. Keeping the prescribed fourth-mass constraint does not avoid this result: it becomes a scalar mean constraint.
Third, even requiring global radial optimality about several different target-translation centers gives exactly the original Euler–Lagrange equation—not an additional global-minimum certificate.
No universal quasiconvexity theorem or negative compatible quasiconvexity test was obtained. These results establish limitations of the reduction, rather than another positive family.

1. What survives the audit
For a prescribed unit direction field \(\omega(x)\), write
\[
u=r\omega,\qquad \rho=r^4.
\]
Then
\[
\mathcal E_\omega(\rho)
=
\int_\Omega
\rho\,F\!\left(
D\omega+\omega\otimes\frac{\nabla\rho}{4\rho}
\right).
\tag{1}
\]
At each point, the function
\[
a\longmapsto F(D\omega+\omega\otimes a)
\]
is convex because differences of its matrix arguments have rank one. Its perspective is therefore convex in \((\rho,\nabla\rho)\). This reconstructs the preceding fixed-direction theorem without assuming quasiconvexity.    PHASE_DENSITY_COMPATIBILITY
The Picone identity also survives. For a positive reference amplitude \(r\), put
\[
A_r=D\omega+\omega\otimes\nabla\log r,\qquad
Q=\omega\otimes\nabla\log(s/r).
\]
Homogeneity gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
&F(D(s\omega))
-\frac14DF(D(r\omega)):
D\!\left(\omega\frac{s^4}{r^3}\right)\\
&\qquad=
s^4\left[F(A_r+Q)-F(A_r)-DF(A_r):Q\right]\ge0.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]
If
\[
\mu_F=\min_{\substack{|R|=1\\ \operatorname{rank}R=1}}F(R)>0,
\]
the scalar convex-quartic calculation strengthens the remainder:
\[
\boxed{
F(D(s\omega))
-\frac14DF(D(r\omega)):
D\!\left(\omega\frac{s^4}{r^3}\right)
\ge
\frac{\mu_F}{3}r^4
\left|\nabla\frac sr\right|^4.
}
\tag{3}
\]
The previous fixed-mass existence argument is also valid under its stated hypotheses: scalar convexity in \(\nabla r\), quartic coercivity at fixed \(\int r^4\), and compactness give an attained amplitude minimum.
These are legitimate conditional operations. Their underlying hidden-convexity, perspective and Picone mechanisms have established predecessors, particularly Brasco–Franzina’s treatment of their connections. The vectorial application uses rank-one differences; it does not make those general mechanisms new. arXiv

2. An exact counterexample to convexity of the fully reduced direction energy
The preceding report showed that the joint density–phase functional could have an indefinite Hessian. That alone did not establish what happens after minimizing the density.    PHASE_DENSITY_COMPATIBILITY
The following example closes that distinction.
On the \(2\pi\)-periodic square torus, use normalized averages and define
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal J(\theta)
=
\min_{\substack{r\ge0,\ r\in W^{1,4}\\ \mathbb E r^4=1}}
\mathbb E\left|D\!\left(re^{i\theta}\right)\right|^4.
}
\tag{4}
\]
The minimization includes amplitudes depending on both coordinates. It is not a minimization over a finite ansatz.
In density variables,
\[
\mathbb E|D(re^{i\theta})|^4
=
\mathbb E\left[
\rho|\nabla\theta|^4+
\frac{|\nabla\rho|^2|\nabla\theta|^2}{8\rho}
+
\frac{|\nabla\rho|^4}{256\rho^3}
\right].
\tag{5}
\]
At \(\theta_0(x,y)=y\),
\[
\mathcal J(\theta_0)=1,
\]
because the first term is \(\mathbb E\rho=1\) and the other terms are nonnegative. The unique normalized amplitude is \(r=1\).
Now take
\[
\theta_\pm(x,y)=y\pm\varepsilon\sin(x+y),
\qquad \varepsilon=\frac1{100}.
\]
Translation of \(x\) by \(\pi\) exchanges the two problems, so
\[
\mathcal J(\theta_+)=\mathcal J(\theta_-).
\]
For \(\theta_+\), use the admissible trial density
\[
\rho=1-8\varepsilon\cos(x+y)\ge\frac{23}{25},
\qquad \mathbb E\rho=1.
\]
Exact integration and a rational denominator bound give
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal J(\theta_\pm)
\le
\frac{486519245657}{486680000000}
<1.
}
\tag{6}
\]
But
\[
\frac{\theta_++\theta_-}{2}=y.
\]
Therefore
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal J\!\left(\frac{\theta_++\theta_-}{2}\right)
>
\frac{\mathcal J(\theta_+)+\mathcal J(\theta_-)}2.
}
\tag{7}
\]
This is a strict failure of convexity of the globally amplitude-minimized functional.
The rational calculation
Set \(c=\cos(x+y)\). Then
\[
|\nabla\theta_+|^2=1+2\varepsilon c+2\varepsilon^2c^2,
\qquad
|\nabla\rho|^2=128\varepsilon^2(1-c^2).
\]
The three numerator averages in (5) are
\[
1-12\varepsilon^2-\frac{45}{2}\varepsilon^4,
\qquad
8\varepsilon^2+4\varepsilon^4,
\qquad
24\varepsilon^4.
\]
Using \(1-8\varepsilon c\ge1-8\varepsilon\) in the two positive rational terms gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal J(\theta_+)
\le{}&
1-12\varepsilon^2-\frac{45}{2}\varepsilon^4\\
&+\frac{8\varepsilon^2+4\varepsilon^4}{1-8\varepsilon}
+\frac{24\varepsilon^4}{(1-8\varepsilon)^3},
\end{aligned}
\]
which is the rational number in (6).
The trial energy itself is exactly
\[
\frac{50880491}{40000000}
-\frac{7241\sqrt{69}}{220800}
\approx0.9996016618.
\]
It is not asserted to equal the global minimum. The upper bound and symmetry already prove (7).
The potentials are genuine smooth periodic maps, and the phase perturbation is two-dimensional. Nevertheless, this is not a quasiconvexity counterexample: the original integrand is \(|A|^4\), so all its energies are nonnegative. It disproves a proposed convexity mechanism for the reduced problem.
The interaction left by exact amplitude elimination
At a nonzero integer wavevector \(k\), the reduced Hessian is
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
D^2\mathcal J(k\cdot x)[h,h]
={}&
\mathbb E\left[
4|k|^2|\nabla h|^2+8(k\cdot\nabla h)^2
\right]\\
&-64|k|^2
\left\langle
k\cdot\nabla h,\,
(-\Delta)^{-1}(k\cdot\nabla h)
\right\rangle.
\end{aligned}
}
\tag{8}
\]
The inverse acts on mean-zero periodic functions.
For \(k=(0,1)\) and \(h=\sin(x+y)\),
\[
D^2\mathcal J(y)[h,h]=-8.
\]
The optimal first-order density response is
\[
\delta\rho=-8\cos(x+y).
\]
This formula eliminates all density modes. Near the positive constant density, the mass-constrained density Euler equation has an invertible Laplacian linearization. The implicit-function theorem supplies a positive critical branch; fixed-direction convexity and the Picone inequality make it the global amplitude-minimizing branch. Thus (8) is the Hessian of the true minimum, not of a restricted two-parameter trial.
Structurally, this is the usual reduced-Hessian formula
\[
D-C^*A^{-1}C.

3. Even radial optimality about every center adds only the Euler equation
There is a stronger way to test the proposed reduction: change the target-space origin.
Let \(u\) be smooth, and choose a constant vector \(c\) so that \(u+c\) never vanishes. Write
\[
r_c=|u+c|,\qquad
\omega_c=\frac{u+c}{|u+c|}.
\]
Theorem — Translated radial optimality
If
\[
-\operatorname{div}DF(Du)=0,
\tag{9}
\]
then for every such \(c\), and every nonnegative amplitude \(s\) with the same boundary trace as \(r_c\),
\[
\boxed{
\int F(D(s\omega_c))-\int F(Du)
\ge
\frac{\mu_F}{3}
\int r_c^4\left|\nabla\frac{s}{r_c}\right|^4
\ge0.
}
\tag{10}
\]
Conversely, in the planar target, if \(u\) has this global radial-minimum property for three translation centers whose differences span \(\mathbb R^2\), then \(u\) satisfies (9).
This concerns the unconstrained radial comparison with fixed boundary, a stronger comparison class than one with fixed fourth mass.
For the forward implication, apply (3) and test stationarity with
\[
\omega_c\left(\frac{s^4}{r_c^3}-r_c\right).
\]
The test vanishes on the boundary, and Euler’s identity converts the remaining stress pairing to \(4\int F(Du)\).
For the converse, use
\[
s=r_c(1+t\eta)
\]
with \(\eta\) compactly supported and both signs of \(t\). Radial optimality gives
\[
\int DF(Du):D[\eta(u+c)]=0.
\]
Subtract this identity for two centers:
\[
\int DF(Du):
[(c_i-c_0)\otimes\nabla\eta]=0.
\]
Spanning the target space recovers every component of (9).
Thus requiring global amplitude optimality about all centers does not supply a condition stronger than vectorial stationarity. This does not assert that a nonglobal stationary point exists for the particular target cone. It shows that these additional radial tests cannot be treated as a new global-minimum certificate.
For smooth positive mass-constrained minimizers, the angular equation leads to the same conclusion. With
\[
S=DF(Du),\qquad j_i=S_i\cdot Ju,
\]
left-rotation invariance gives
\[
S:JDu=0.
\]
The phase variation has derivative
\[
\delta\mathcal E=\int j\cdot\nabla h.
\]
Together with radial stationarity, angular stationarity is exactly
\[
-\operatorname{div}S=4\lambda |u|^2u.
\]
It is the remaining component of the original constrained Euler–Lagrange system.
4. The main limitation theorem: globally minimized, nearly constant directions retain arbitrary residual fields
The previous observation that a large target translation makes the phase small concerned a given map. The stronger issue is whether reminimizing the amplitude globally removes the original difficulty.
It does not.
Let \(\Omega\subset\mathbb R^2\) be a bounded Lipschitz domain. Let
\[
(g_1,g_2)=Bx
\]
be affine boundary data, and fix an arbitrary scalar field
\[
v\in g_2+W^{1,4}_0(\Omega).
\]
Assume \(F\) is a rank-one-convex homogeneous quartic with \(\mu_F>0\). No rotational symmetry is needed.
First define the ordinary Cartesian scalar minimum
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_F(v)
=
\min_{\substack{a-g_1\in W^{1,4}_0}}
\int_\Omega
F\begin{pmatrix}
\nabla a\\
\nabla v
\end{pmatrix}.
}
\tag{11}
\]
This minimum is attained uniquely. Varying \(\nabla a\) changes one matrix row, hence a rank-one direction. The quartic remainder gives
\[
\boxed{
I_v(a)-I_v(a_v)
\ge
\frac{\mu_F}{3}\|\nabla(a-a_v)\|_4^4,
}
\tag{12}
\]
where \(a_v\) is the minimizer.
This is standard scalar convex minimization already available in the original variables.
Construct the direction independently of the unknown amplitude
For large \(R\), put
\[
d_R=R+g_1,\qquad q_R=\frac{v}{R+g_1},
\]
\[
\boxed{
\omega_R=
\frac{(R+g_1,v)}
{\sqrt{(R+g_1)^2+v^2}}.
}
\tag{13}
\]
For an admissible first component \(a\), define
\[
\boxed{
u_R[a]
=
\left(
R+a,\,
v+(a-g_1)q_R
\right)
=
\left(
R+a,\,
\frac{(R+a)v}{R+g_1}
\right).
}
\tag{14}
\]
When \(R+a\ge0\), this is an amplitude times \(\omega_R\). Every nonnegative amplitude with the prescribed boundary is represented this way.
On the boundary,
\[
u_R[a]=(R+g_1,g_2),
\]
exactly, not asymptotically.
Let
\[
m_R(v)=
\min_{\substack{a-g_1\in W^{1,4}_0\\R+a\ge0}}
\int_\Omega F(Du_R[a]).
\tag{15}
\]
Theorem — Quantitative translation limit
For sufficiently large \(R\), the minimum in (15) is attained, its minimizing amplitudes are strictly positive, and
\[
\boxed{
|m_R(v)-\mathcal R_F(v)|\le\frac{C}{R}.
}
\tag{16}
\]
Every minimizing first component satisfies
\[
\boxed{
\|a_R-a_v\|_{W^{1,4}}\le C R^{-1/4}.
}
\tag{17}
\]
Meanwhile,
\[
\|\omega_R-e_1\|_{W^{1,4}}\le\frac{C}{R}.
\]
For smooth \(v\), the directions converge to \(e_1\) at rate \(O(R^{-1})\) in every fixed \(C^k\) norm.
The constants depend on the fixed field and boundary data, not on \(R\).
Proof
Write \(h=a-g_1\). The gradient is
\[
Du_R[a]
=
\begin{pmatrix}
\nabla a\\
\nabla v+q_R\nabla h+h\nabla q_R
\end{pmatrix}.
\tag{18}
\]
Since \(W^{1,4}\) embeds into \(L^\infty\) in this planar domain,
\[
\|\nabla(hq_R)\|_4
\le
\frac{C}{R}\|\nabla h\|_4.
\]
Quartic polynomial growth then gives the uniform comparison
\[
\boxed{
|I_R(a)-I_v(a)|
\le
\frac{C}{R}
\left(1+\|\nabla(a-g_1)\|_4^4\right).
}
\tag{19}
\]
The Cartesian scalar problem has a coercive bound
\[
I_v(a)\ge c\|\nabla(a-g_1)\|_4^4-C.
\]
For large \(R\), equation (19) gives the same bound for \(I_R\), uniformly in \(R\). The affine comparison \(a=g_1\) has uniformly bounded energy, so all minimizers are bounded in \(W^{1,4}\), hence in \(L^\infty\). Therefore \(R+a_R>0\) for large \(R\).
Existence follows by the direct method. Strong convergence of the lower-order factor \(h\), and convexity in the scalar gradient through the rank-one coefficient \((1,q_R)\), give lower semicontinuity.
Compare the two minimizers using (19). This proves (16). Then
\[
I_v(a_R)-I_v(a_v)\le\frac{C}{R},
\]
and (12) proves (17).
The direction in (13) uses the arbitrary second component \(v\) and the prescribed first boundary component \(g_1\). It does not use the unknown minimizing first component. The amplitude is genuinely minimized before the limit is taken.
That is why this result is stronger than merely translating a selected map.
5. Keeping the fourth-mass constraint does not evade the limit
The preceding theorem first removes the mass constraint to expose the mechanism. The exact constraint from the earlier investigation can also be retained.
Choose any reference
\[
a_{\rm ref}\in g_1+W^{1,4}_0,
\]
and prescribe
\[
M_R=
\int_\Omega
(R+a_{\rm ref})^4(1+q_R^2)^2.
\]
Require the competing amplitude to satisfy
\[
\int_\Omega(R+a)^4(1+q_R^2)^2=M_R.
\tag{20}
\]
On uniformly bounded \(W^{1,4}\) sets,
\[
\boxed{
\frac{M_R(a)-M_R(a_{\rm ref})}{4R^3}
=
\int_\Omega(a-a_{\rm ref})+O(R^{-1}).
}
\tag{21}
\]
Consequently, the fully mass-constrained amplitude minimum converges to
\[
\boxed{
\min_{\substack{a-g_1\in W^{1,4}_0\\
\int a=\int a_{\rm ref}}}
\int_\Omega
F\begin{pmatrix}\nabla a\\\nabla v\end{pmatrix}.
}
\tag{22}
\]
The energy error remains \(O(R^{-1})\), and the minimizers converge at rate \(O(R^{-1/4})\).
This is an exact constrained limit, not simply dropping the mass condition. To obtain a recovery competitor with precisely the required mass, add \(t_R\zeta\), where \(\zeta\) is a fixed zero-boundary bump with integral one. The scaled mass derivative tends to one, so an \(O(R^{-1})\) correction imposes (20) exactly. A corresponding correction proves the lower bound.
The fourth-mass constraint becomes a mean constraint on the eliminated Cartesian component. It does not introduce a new positivity mechanism.
Consequence for any putative counterexample
Suppose an actual smooth affine-boundary map \(u=(a,v)\) satisfies
\[
\int_\Omega F(Du)
<
|\Omega|F(B)-\varepsilon.
\]
Choose \(a_{\rm ref}=a\) above. The limiting minimum in (22) is no larger than the original energy. Therefore, for sufficiently large \(R\), the globally amplitude-minimized map in direction \(\omega_R\) still satisfies
\[
\boxed{
\int_\Omega F(Du_R^*)
<
|\Omega|F(B)-\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
}
\tag{23}
\]
It is a genuine compatible map with exact affine boundary \(Bx+Re_1\), and its direction converges to a constant. Subtracting the target translation leaves its gradient unchanged.
Thus:
If the original problem has a negative test, global amplitude minimization does not exclude it—even in asymptotically constant direction fields.

This is a conditional preservation theorem, not a constructed negative quasiconvexity test.
It also is not a computational hardness theorem. An equivalent representation could still reveal a useful inequality. What is ruled out is the inference that conditional convexity plus small direction variation has, by itself, removed the global sign difficulty.
6. What remains after eliminating amplitude
The translate-free residual problem is already
\[
\mathcal R_F(v)
=
\min_a\int_\Omega
F\begin{pmatrix}\nabla a\\\nabla v\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The unresolved inequality is
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_F(v)\ge|\Omega|F(B)
\quad\text{for every admissible }v.
}
\tag{24}
\]
This looks scalar in its remaining variable, but it is nonlocal: the minimizing \(a_v\) depends on the complete field \(v\).
At a sufficiently regular, nondegenerate minimizing branch,
\[
\boxed{
\mathcal R_F''(v)[h,h]
=
\inf_\eta
\int_\Omega
D^2F(D(a_v,v))
[D(\eta,h),D(\eta,h)].
}
\tag{25}
\]
It is the operator Schur complement of the first-row elliptic block. Pointwise rank-one convexity does not automatically sign this variable-coefficient, fully compatible quadratic form.
Equation (25) is not offered as a new positivity theorem. It identifies the same coupled interaction that reappeared in the explicit phase calculation.
There is a practical, limited benefit: Cartesian scalar minimization avoids a large target translation, winding coordinates and an artificial mass parameter. It has a unique global minimizer and a rigorous residual-error bound. If \(d\) is the dual norm of the scalar Euler residual at a trial \(a\), then
\[
0\le I_v(a)-\mathcal R_F(v)
\le
\frac34\left(\frac{3}{4\mu_F}\right)^{1/3}d^{4/3}.
\tag{26}
\]
This follows by optimizing
\[
dt-\frac{\mu_F}{3}t^4.
\]
It can support certified component elimination in a counterexample search. It is standard convex analysis, not a new global solution or a demonstrated computational advantage.
The external positive result remains properly scoped: Bruno–Pasqualotto’s quartic theorem assumes both left and right rotational invariance. It does not supply the missing bound for the complete left-invariant cone. arXiv
7. Research assessment
The amplitude reduction remains mathematically valid. The present investigation establishes why it should not be treated as evidence that the global obstruction has become easier:
Exact elimination can leave negative direction curvature. This occurs for a convex original energy, after global minimization rather than before it.
Additional radial optimality does not create a new global certificate. Across enough translation centers, it is equivalent to the original Euler equation.
Nearly constant minimized directions retain arbitrary residual fields. The quantitative translation theorem preserves the original affine-boundary energy problem, including the prescribed mass variant.
The work in this run is analytic and exact. It produced no negative quasiconvexity test. The negative quantities are a reduced-energy second difference and second variation; all original energies in that example remain nonnegative. The earlier positive interval certificate and contact classifications were not used to infer a new global conclusion.
Further concentrated inference on more fixed-direction certificates or the same alternating amplitude–phase searches is not presently justified. The reason is the precise behavior of this reduction, not the age or open status of Morrey’s problem.
A substantive reopening requires an actual bound on the mixed operator in (25), a global structure that does not follow merely from eliminating one convex block, or an independently certified negative compatible field. No such mechanism has been established here.
The checkpoint should therefore be preserved for independent verification. The amplitude problem has been solved conditionally; the original global interaction survives intact in the remaining direction variable.
