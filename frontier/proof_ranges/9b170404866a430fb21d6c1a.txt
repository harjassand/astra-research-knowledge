## 3. Why cubic noncommutation is necessary

### Proposition 2 — a zero-integral pulse cycle with positive growth

Let

\[
X=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad
Z=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]

and, for \(\varepsilon>0\), set

\[
A_\varepsilon=e^{\varepsilon X},\quad
B_\varepsilon=e^{\varepsilon Z},\quad
C_\varepsilon=A_\varepsilon B_\varepsilon
 A_\varepsilon^{-1}B_\varepsilon^{-1},
\]

\[
D_\varepsilon=A_\varepsilon C_\varepsilon
 A_\varepsilon^{-1}C_\varepsilon^{-1}.
\tag{7}
\]

The product \(D_\varepsilon\) consists of ten exponentials of symmetric trace-zero matrices. The sum of the ten exponent matrices is zero. It is realized by a piecewise constant symmetric driver with zero primitive at the end of the cycle (use the reverse order of the written factors as chronological order).

Direct multiplication gives

\[
\det D_\varepsilon=1,\qquad
\operatorname{tr}D_\varepsilon
 =2+16\sinh^6\varepsilon\cosh^2\varepsilon>2.
\tag{8}
\]

Its eigenvalues are positive and reciprocal, and

\[
\boxed{
\log\rho(D_\varepsilon)
=2\operatorname{arsinh}
 (2\sinh^3\varepsilon\cosh\varepsilon)
=4\varepsilon^3+O(\varepsilon^5).
}
\tag{9}
\]

In particular, repeating the cycle \(m\) times gives

\[
\|D_\varepsilon^m\|\geq
 \exp\{m\log\rho(D_\varepsilon)\},
\]

although the cumulative driver returns to zero after every cycle and remains bounded by \(\sqrt5\,\varepsilon\) throughout all repetitions.

An exact check of (8) can be made with \(q=e^\varepsilon\):

\[
\operatorname{tr}D_\varepsilon-2
=\frac{(q-1)^6(q+1)^6(q^2+1)^2}{16q^8}.
\]

On one cycle,

\[
\int\|[B,[B,S]]\|\,dt=K\varepsilon^3,
\]

where

\[
K=8\sqrt5+4\sqrt2+16\operatorname{arsinh}(1/2)
 +2\operatorname{arsinh}2+4\operatorname{arsinh}1
\approx37.6575525689.
\]

Thus both the certified correction and an actual positive growth exponent occur at cubic order. A general fourth-order replacement would be false.

### Smooth finite-time obstruction with a primitive tending to zero

The obstruction is not only an infinite-time, discontinuous-driver phenomenon. The expansion of the same matrix is

\[
D_\varepsilon=I+4\varepsilon^3 Z
 +8\varepsilon^4\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}
 +O(\varepsilon^5).
\]

For all sufficiently small positive \(\varepsilon\), its entries are nonnegative and

\[
D_\varepsilon\mathbf1\geq
 (1+2\varepsilon^3)\mathbf1
\]

componentwise. Choose \(\varepsilon_j=\delta(j+1)^{-1/3}\), with \(\delta\) sufficiently small. Realize cycle \(j\) inside an interval of length \(2^{-j}\), using disjoint smooth scalar bumps for the ten matrix pulses. Each bump has the required matrix integral and vanishes to every order at its endpoints. The propagator is unchanged by this reparameterization.

The resulting driver is smooth on \([0,T)\) for a finite \(T\). Its primitive returns to zero after every cycle and tends to zero as \(t\uparrow T\), because its excursion on cycle \(j\) is at most \(\sqrt5\varepsilon_j\). Nevertheless, the product acting on \(\mathbf1\) grows at least as

\[
\prod_j(1+2\varepsilon_j^3)=\infty.
\]

Therefore even a bounded primitive that extends continuously to zero at the endpoint does not control amplification in the general symmetric matrix equation. Its divergent cubic action identifies the failure.

Conversely, taking \(\varepsilon_j=\delta(j+1)^{-2/5}\) in the same construction gives finite total cubic action but divergent \(\int\|S\|\) and divergent \(\int\|[B,S]\|\). Theorem 1 still bounds the propagator and its inverse. This proves a strict improvement over those absolute-action sufficient conditions at the matrix level.

These are matrix-system counterexamples. They are not constructions of finite-energy Euler or Navier–Stokes singularities.
