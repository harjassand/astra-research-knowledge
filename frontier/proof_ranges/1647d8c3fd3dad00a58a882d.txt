I constructed a path-ensemble representation that supplies an exact cooperative source–sink law and a computable energy bound without enumerating the module’s configuration graph. It works for the original hard two-dimensional Kob–Andersen model at \(p=0.80\).
The quantitative result is nevertheless too weak:
\[ \boxed{10^{-11593}\le D(0.80)\le 0.238144.} \]
This is not an informative prediction. Positivity at high density was already established, and existing specialised results have much stronger asymptotic scope. The advance here is in constructing and accounting for the module, not in resolving dense cooperative transport. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2003.08495v2)
The strongest preserved operation replaces a list of microscopic histories with exact laws for the probability transported by those histories. Those laws compose through the construction and control congestion. A separate calculation identifies why the resulting full-frame representation remains quantitatively unsuitable.

A compact path description does not, by itself, give a compact transport certificate.
A legal route can be short while an enormous number of initial configurations funnel through the same intermediate transition. That multiplicity determines the current’s energy. Storing the route as a loop or recursive program does not remove it.
I examined three alternatives. Deterministic path compression retained a factorial history penalty. Quotienting configurations by vacancy count discarded information needed to establish legality. Randomising paths helped only after choosing the randomisation so that its entire transported probability law could be calculated.
The resulting representation attaches three objects to a path-generating operation:
\[ \text{exact source and endpoint law}, \qquad \text{legal microscopic implementation}, \qquad \text{second-moment bound}. \]
The second moment measures concentration relative to equilibrium. It replaces a worst-case count of inverse histories.
This does not invent cooperative paths, frameability or information-loss analysis. Those are established ingredients in specialised KA methods. The contribution candidate is the explicit density calculation and its composition into a finite certificate. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2003.08495v2)
Consider a strip of \(m\) sites containing \(K\ge1\) vacancies. Choose one of its vacant sites uniformly and cyclically rotate the strip so that the chosen vacancy reaches site zero.
An adjacent empty strip supplies a hard-KA implementation: every adjacent transposition inside the active strip has the required extra vacant neighbour at both endpoints. A rotation needs at most \(m(m-1)\) elementary instructions.
The important fact is not the forward route length. It is the exact output density.
Starting with Bernoulli equilibrium restricted to nonfull strips, the output density relative to the same equilibrium measure is
\[ \boxed{ \rho_m(\eta)=\frac{m}{K(\eta)}\mathbf1_{\{\eta_0=0\}}. } \tag{1} \]
Proof. For any output whose first site is vacant, each of the \(m\) rotations has one inverse input. Rotation preserves equilibrium weight and vacancy count. Each inverse contribution has weight \(1/K\). Their sum is \(m/K\).
Periodic configurations do not invalidate the identity: group elements are counted with their multiplicity.
Consequently, the exact squared-density moment is
\[ V_m(p) = m\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac1k {m\choose k}q^kp^{m-k}, \qquad q=1-p. \]
It has a scalar recurrence:
\[ \boxed{ V_1=q,\qquad V_m=\frac{mp}{m-1}V_{m-1}+1-p^m. } \tag{2} \]
For example, writing \(F_m=V_m/m\),
\[ F_m = \int_0^1\frac{(p+qt)^m-p^m}{t}\,dt = \sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{p^{m-k}(1-p^k)}k, \]
which immediately gives (2).
Thus the contributions of exponentially many strip configurations can be summed through one scalar recurrence.
Equation (1) is also optimal for endpoint concentration under these requirements.
Within a sector containing \(K=k\) vacancies, the target event \(\eta_0=0\) occupies a fraction \(k/m\) of the sector. Any particle-number-preserving kernel that sends the full sector measure into that event must have average output density \(m/k\) there.
Cauchy–Schwarz minimises the second moment when that density is constant. The balanced rotation attains equality in every sector.
This is optimality of the endpoint density—not of the microscopic current energy or the complete cooperative module. It nevertheless separates avoidable history-counting losses from the concentration imposed by the chosen target.
For a fixed geometric route, successive fresh strips consist of disjoint input sites. Their endpoint laws factor, up to deterministic relocation of coordinates. Their squared-density moments therefore multiply.
During an individual rotation, summing its at most \(m\) pivot branches gives an additional squared-density factor at most \(m^2\). This is conservative but explicit.
The calculation never assumes that a short program has low congestion. It carries a bound on the intermediate probability concentration through every program operation.
I also constructed a direct signed-current version of an empty-column exchange.
Suppose \(A\) is an empty column of length \(m\), and adjacent column \(B\) is nonfull. Superpose the legal column-exchange route for every vacant pivot in \(B\), rather than selecting the first vacancy.
The source multiplicity of the resulting current \(F\) is
\[ K=m-N, \]
where \(N\) is the total particle number in the two columns. This quantity is conserved by every used exchange.
Therefore
\[ H=\frac{F}{K} \]
is an exactly normalised current on its support, with
\[ \partial H = \mathbf1_{\{A\text{ empty},\,B\text{ nonfull}\}} - \mathbf1_{\{B\text{ empty},\,A\text{ nonfull}\}}. \tag{3} \]
The division is valid because the denominator is a microscopic invariant. Dividing by a number of facilitators that changes during the route would not be justified.
After cancellation, the numerator has exactly
\[ \boxed{8m-10} \]
vacancy-cylinder terms. Products of those terms are either inconsistent or another cylinder, so their equilibrium moments are evaluated exactly by intersection.
