## 2. A spectral extraction lemma

We use the classical Bernstein representation theorem: a completely monotone function on \((0,\infty)\) is the Laplace transform of a positive measure on \([0,\infty)\). If it has a finite continuous limit at zero, the measure is finite and its total mass is that limit. A reference is Christian Berg, *Stieltjes–Pick–Bernstein–Schoenberg and their connection to complete monotonicity* (2007), Theorem 1.5.

**Lemma.** Let
\[
g(t)=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-\lambda t}\,d\mu(\lambda)
\]
for a finite positive measure \(\mu\). Let \(S\subset[0,\infty)\) be locally finite. Suppose that, for every positive \(R\notin S\),
\[
g(t)=\sum_{s\in S,\ s<R}c_se^{-st}+O(e^{-Rt})
\quad(t\to\infty).
\tag{6}
\]
Then
\[
\mu=\sum_{s\in S}c_s\delta_s,
\qquad c_s\geq0.
\tag{7}
\]

**Proof.** Order the elements of \(S\). The bound before the first possible exponent excludes positive measure below that exponent: positive mass on \([0,b]\) would make \(g(t)\geq \mu([0,b])e^{-bt}\), contradicting a strictly faster upper bound. At the first allowed exponent \(s\), (6) gives
\[
\lim_{t\to\infty}e^{st}g(t)=c_s.
\]
Once the measure is known to be supported on \([s,\infty)\), dominated convergence identifies this limit as \(\mu(\{s\})\). In particular \(c_s\geq0\). Subtract that atom, leaving a positive measure, and repeat. Local finiteness lets the induction cover every bounded interval; the asymptotic bounds exclude all gaps. No remaining positive measure can lie beyond every finite cutoff. This proves (7). \(\square\)

The lemma is important here because it uses asymptotic expansions, not convergent Taylor series. A smooth remainder invisible to every Taylor coefficient cannot survive as positive Laplace mass beyond every finite decay rate.

## 3. Realizing arbitrary eigenbases by valid Markov generators

Fix \(q\in\Delta_n^\circ\), with \(n\geq2\). Work on the real Hilbert space
\[
H_q=\{u\in\mathbb R^n:\sum_iq_iu_i=0\},
\qquad
\langle u,v\rangle_q=\sum_iq_iu_iv_i.
\]
The admissible density deviations form the open set
\[
U_q=\{u\in H_q:u_i>-1\ \text{for all }i\}.
\]
Define
\[
F_q(u)=D_n(q(1+u),q),
\]
where \(q(1+u)\) denotes componentwise multiplication. Then \(F_q\) is continuous on \(U_q\), smooth near zero, and \(F_q(0)=0\).

The reset generator
\[
L_0=\mathbf1 q-I
\]
has off-diagonal entries \((L_0)_{ij}=q_j>0\). On \(H_q\), it equals \(-I\).

Choose **any** \(q\)-orthonormal basis \(e_1,\ldots,e_d\), where \(d=n-1\). Choose positive numbers \(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d\), rationally independent and sufficiently close to one, and define
\[
L\mathbf1=0,
\qquad Le_j=-\lambda_je_j.
\tag{8}
\]
The operator is \(q\)-self-adjoint and annihilates constants. Therefore its rows sum to zero and it satisfies detailed balance. Its matrix is arbitrarily close to \(L_0\), so all off-diagonal entries remain strictly positive. Thus it is an irreducible, reversible Markov generator.

This step does **not** assume that orthogonal rotations of probability vectors are stochastic maps. Instead, every orthonormal basis is realized as an eigenbasis of an actual Markov generator close to reset.

Reversibility gives the density-ratio evolution
\[
\frac{pe^{tL}}{q}-1=e^{tL}\left(\frac pq-1\right).
\tag{9}
\]
For \(u=\sum_jx_je_j\), this is \(\sum_jx_je^{-\lambda_jt}e_j\).
