## Verdict

I found no mathematical gap in the anisotropic source at the stated scope: finite-dimensional physical variable, arbitrary compact finite-order distribution, real Banach target, proper closed convex cone with nonempty interior, and an arbitrary prescribed open convex neighborhood of zero. Its claims of an exact moment domain, pointwise positive-definite potential Hessian, gradient diffeomorphism onto the domain, derivative-weighted integrability, and cone sandwich all have supporting arguments.

I also found no gap in the root's finite-ball proof, subject to retaining the source setting's proper-cone hypothesis or separately treating the trivial cone `C=E` case. The ball result is a localized consequence of the PR94 construction; the general anisotropic argument supplies a distinct geometric step.

## 1. Lower-dimensional polar and barrier divergence

The anisotropic source uses relative dimension `d=dim span(P)` rather than incorrectly assuming a full-dimensional polar. This matters for slabs, half-spaces, and domains containing lines.

The set `P` is compact because `D` contains a ball about zero. Separation proves `D={g:h_P(g)<1}` even when `P` is lower dimensional. A relative ball of positive `d`-volume exists because `P` spans its relative affine hull; its affine hull is linear since zero belongs to `P`.

At a finite boundary point `b`, a maximizing polar point `u0` satisfies `u0·b=1`. On the contracted set `(1-s)u0+sQ`, the denominator is at most a constant times `s`, while relative volume is a constant times `s^d`. The exponent `d+1` therefore gives a lower bound proportional to `1/s`. This proves the asserted barrier divergence. Monotonicity of `s_n` is unnecessary; positivity and convergence to zero suffice, as the source acknowledges.

When `d=0`, the polar is `{0}` and polarity gives `D=R^m`; there is no finite boundary. The source handles this case separately. This step passes.

## 2. Infimal-convolution minorant

The zero extension of the positive continuous tolerance need not be continuous at the boundary. The proof does not require that extension to be continuous.

The infimum of `extended_tolerance(h)+|g-h|` is 1-Lipschitz. It is zero off `D` by choosing `h=g`. At a fixed interior point, a small ball contained in `D` has a positive tolerance lower bound, and points outside that ball incur a positive distance. Hence the infimum is strictly positive there. These observations justify all uses of the minorant, including compact-sublevel reciprocal bounds and the implication `w(h)>0 => h in D`. This step passes.

## 3. The scalar curvature envelope

The source's `a_n` are finite and nondecreasing. Its step series is locally finite. For `t in [n,n+1]`, all transitions before index `n` have completed, so the telescoping series is at least `a_n`. Since the definition of `a_n` takes a supremum through the larger sublevel `Phi<=n+3`, it bounds the required reciprocal at `Phi=t`.

The scalar derivative is nonnegative and nondecreasing. The Hessian chain rule, together with `D^2 Phi >= 2I`, gives the exact prescribed lower Hessian bound. The scalar function also dominates the exhaustion, so both finite-boundary divergence and quadratic coercivity remain available. This step passes.

## 4. Surjectivity of the dual gradient

For every physical `x`, `U(g)-x·g` has a minimizer in `D`: quadratic growth prevents escape to infinity, and the barrier prevents approach to a finite boundary. Positive-definite Hessian makes the minimizer unique. Its stationarity equation is `grad U(g)=x`; the inverse-function theorem gives smooth dependence on `x`.

Thus the conjugate potential is finite and smooth on all of `R^m`, with gradient range exactly `D` and Hessian equal to the inverse dual Hessian. There is no inference of gradient surjectivity merely from boundary asymptotics. This step passes.

## 5. Higher derivatives after mollification

The transport inequalities follow from the 1-Lipschitz minorant and the bound `|d grad V0/dt| <= eta |v| w(grad V0)`. The resulting comparison is valid even arbitrarily near a nonsmooth boundary; no differentiability of `w` is needed, since it is Lipschitz and can be handled by the integral form of Gronwall.

The parameter choice ensures the multiplier over a physical neighborhood of radius `R+1` is at most `3/2`. Averaging the gradient estimate gives `w(grad V)>=w(grad V0)/2`, so the post-mollification gradient lies inside `D`.

For a multi-index of order at least two, selecting two derivatives on `V0` and transferring the remaining derivatives to the compact mollifier is valid. The second derivative is globally bounded, and the kernel derivatives have finite `L1` norms. This supplies every local higher derivative needed by the compact distribution and every fixed global higher derivative bound needed for weighted integrability. A Hessian-only proof would be insufficient; the source does not make that mistake. This step passes.

## 6. Exact domain and gradient surjectivity after mollification

The bounded perturbation satisfies `0 <= V-V0 <= eta/2`: the lower bound is Jensen's inequality, and the upper bound follows from the global Hessian bound, the mean-zero kernel, and its unit-radius support. Consequently all exponential integrals for the two potentials have exactly the same finiteness set, including boundary points.

The source separately proves the conjugate domain is exactly `D`. Interior equality follows from a supporting hyperplane to `U` and evaluation at `x=grad U(g)`. A finite conjugate value at a boundary point would, by convexity along the segment from zero, bound `U` near that boundary point, contradicting the barrier. Strict separation handles exterior points.

The tilted-integrability argument is valid: a convex finite potential with finite exponential integral has a convex finite-volume sublevel containing a ball. Such a sublevel is bounded, because the convex hull of a fixed ball and arbitrarily distant points has arbitrarily large volume. Convexity along rays then supplies positive linear growth. Its conjugate is therefore finite in a neighborhood of the tilt. Since the conjugate's domain is `D`, that tilt belongs to `D`.

After mollification, every `g in D` still yields a coercive `V(x)-g·x`, so it has a unique minimizer with `grad V(x)=g`. Positive-definite Hessian gives injectivity and a smooth local inverse, and hence a smooth global diffeomorphism. This step passes.

There is also a shorter independent boundary check in this worker's proof: separation gives `u·h<=c<=u·g` on `D`, and the conjugate formula gives `V0(tu+z)<=tc+V0(z)`. The same inequality survives compact mollification and forces divergence on a fixed transverse cylinder. This argument avoids any smooth-boundary or bounded-domain assumption. It is an alternative check, not a second count of the same conclusion.

## 7. Cone remainder constants and the normalization

The source's local `C^N` estimate uses the Hessian for orders zero and one, and the supplied jets for orders at least two. Its rough constant `m(1+R)^2` covers the conversion from coordinate Hessian bounds to Taylor bounds.

The Bell-number bound is adequate: every nonzero derivative of `exp(-F)-1` contains at least one derivative of `F`, and each remaining factor has magnitude at most one when the `C^N` norm of `F` is at most one. Leibniz's binomial sum is bounded by `(1+|g|)^N`, so no missing exponential-in-`N` product constant is apparent.

The interior-distance estimate gives the two cone inequalities by homogeneity and closedness. The lower one is interior because its coefficient is strictly positive. Compact-distribution estimates and the exponential gap at every interior tilt justify all derivative integrals and interchange with `T`, giving total output mass `e`.

The source states pointwise tilt weights. The same proof gives the stronger uniform compact-domain form: for compact `K subset D`, choose `r>0` with `K+r Bbar subset D`, take the maximum of `U` on that compact set, and obtain `V(x)>=h_K(x)+r|x|-M`. This establishes the compact-weighted derivative integrability stated in this worker's independent candidate.

## 8. Moment domains of positive log-concave densities

The source's classification sentence is justified, and strengthens a statement only about prescribed open domains. For any everywhere positive log-concave probability density, its negative logarithm is a finite convex function. If the exponential integral at a tilt is finite, the sublevel argument in Section 6 above gives positive linear growth at that tilt and thus finite exponential integrals in a neighborhood. Its full finite-moment domain is therefore open as well as convex and contains zero.

The word “everywhere” is material to this short argument's use of a finite convex potential on all physical space. The audited theorem includes that hypothesis.

## 9. Root finite-ball proof

The bounded slow-slope series reaches every finite stage, stays strictly below `a`, and tends to `a`. Thresholds delayed by the compact margin bound control the remote-tail errors. Its positive base slope keeps the radial potential strictly convex even on slope plateaus.

The bound `V_L(x)<=a|x|` is correct because `rho-1<=|x|/L`. The asymptotic `V_L(x)/|x| -> a` follows by averaging a slope tending to `a`. Interior moments and all weighted derivatives follow. On a fixed boundary cylinder, `a t-a sqrt(t^2+|z|^2)>=-a/(2t)` is the correct lower bound and proves divergence at every boundary tilt.

The logistic identity is also correct: for `p=(a/4)sech^2(a x/2)`, `p-a^-2 p''=(3a/8)sech^4(a x/2)`. The input noise domain is `(-a,a)` and the output domain is `(-2a,2a)`. This cancellation means one should not identify the output's moment domain with the scalar noise's domain.
