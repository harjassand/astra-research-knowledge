Let
\[ p_1,\ldots,p_n\in\mathbb R[z_1,\ldots,z_m] \]
be nonzero polynomials, and define binary outputs
\[ F_i(z)=\mathbf 1\{p_i(z)<0\}. \]
Assume the latent variable \(Z\) has a density strictly positive almost everywhere on \(\mathbb R^m\). Standard Gaussian input is one example. Polynomial zero sets then have probability zero.
Factor the output polynomials over \(\mathbb R\). Collect the distinct irreducible factors
\[ q_1,\ldots,q_s \]
that take both positive and negative values.
For each factor, record the outputs in which it occurs to odd multiplicity:
\[ v_j= \bigl(\operatorname{ord}_{q_j}(p_i)\bmod 2\bigr)_{i=1}^n \in\mathbb F_2^n. \]
Call \(v_j\) its factor mask. Let
\[ V=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb F_2}\{v_1,\ldots,v_s\}. \]
For any output \(c_0\) attained away from the polynomial zero sets,
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{aff}_{\mathbb F_2} \bigl(\operatorname{supp}F(Z)\bigr) = c_0+V. } \]
Equivalently, a parity
\[ a\cdot F(Z)\pmod 2 \]
is almost surely constant if and only if
\[ a\cdot v_j=0 \qquad\text{for every }j. \]
Thus all exact parity invariants are the kernel of a finite binary matrix.
Even powers do not change signs. An irreducible factor that is not sign-changing has a fixed sign away from its zeros; absorb these fixed signs into an offset \(b\in\mathbb F_2^n\).
Unique factorization gives
\[ F(z) = b+\sum_{j=1}^s v_j\,\mathbf 1\{q_j(z)<0\} \quad\text{in }\mathbb F_2^n. \]
Therefore the support lies in \(c_0+V\).
The reverse inclusion requires a geometric fact: each sign-changing irreducible factor can be crossed without crossing any other distinct factor.
Here is why. Choose two points where \(q_j\) has opposite signs, and use their connecting direction as one coordinate \(t\), with remaining coordinates \(y\). Opposite endpoint signs persist on nearby parallel lines.
Over \(\mathbb R(y)\), irreducibility in characteristic zero makes \(q_j\) relatively prime to its \(t\)-derivative. Their resultant is therefore nonzero. Likewise, the resultant of \(q_j\) with each other distinct irreducible factor is nonzero. Choose a nearby line avoiding the zeros of these finitely many resultant polynomials and the relevant leading coefficients.
On that line, the opposite endpoint signs force a real root of \(q_j\). The root is simple and is not a zero of any other factor. Crossing it changes the output by exactly \(v_j\).
Both sides contain open sets, so both output values have positive probability. Consequently every \(v_j\) belongs to the span of differences of actual support points. This proves the reverse inclusion. \(\square\)
The operation can be written
\[ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}p = \bigl(\operatorname{ord}_{q}p\bmod2\bigr)_ {q\text{ sign-changing}}, \]
with
\[ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}(pr) = \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}p+ \partial_{\mathrm{sgn}}r. \]
It converts an exact-support question about continuous polynomial maps into binary linear algebra.
But it does not recover arbitrary support constraints.
For example, take
\[ p_1=x(x+y),\qquad p_2=y(x+y),\qquad p_3=xy. \]
The output has even parity because
\[ p_1p_2p_3=x^2y^2(x+y)^2. \]
The factor masks span the full four-point even-parity code, but only
\[ 000,\quad101,\quad011 \]
occur. The fourth point, \(110\), is impossible.
The theorem extracts the affine hull, not the full support or its probabilities. This counterexample prevents a false escalation into a general compatibility theorem.
