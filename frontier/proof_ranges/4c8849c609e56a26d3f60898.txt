7. Computational resources and failed escape routes
The distinction between statistical samples and numerical precision is decisive.
The reconstruction needs roughly
\[
O\!\left(T\log\!\left(2+\frac TB\right)\right)
\]
precision bits at constant target accuracy—not exponentially many bits. Generating and evaluating its coefficients has polynomial bit cost in the degree, input encoding and requested working precision.
But acquiring that precision from independent noisy samples requires the exponential count in (1).
Therefore, the theorem does not rule out efficient continuation from a deterministic oracle that supplies arbitrary-precision \(G(s)\) at cost polynomial in the requested number of bits. That is a stronger access model.
It also does not establish computational hardness for these Hamiltonians when their complete matrices are given. They have only \(O(T)\) levels and can be simulated by ordinary matrix methods. The obstruction is specifically the information delivered through the specified measurements.
Several proposed shortcuts fail within that interface:
Positivity, a known spectrum and sum rules are insufficient. The two hypotheses satisfy them simultaneously. Their difference lies in spectral weights, not unknown eigenvalues.
Removing small signal amplitude is insufficient. The known reference keeps \(G(s)\ge1/4\), including at arbitrarily large \(s\). Logarithmic measurements retain the lower bound.
Unlimited imaginary time is insufficient. Increasing \(B\) changes the superexponential \(e^{\Theta(T\log T)}\) regime into an exponential \(e^{\Theta(T)}\) regime; it does not produce polynomial sample complexity.
Finite numerical output does not mean cheap acquisition. The returned curve has a compact description, but the data required to certify it are expensive.
The upper bound is an oracle sampling theorem, not a laboratory implementation. Preparation, decorrelation and per-query physical costs remain separate. It uses times no larger than \(\min(B,M/16)\). Quantization and independent systematic errors must fit the tolerance in (21). Since both \(G\) and \(\log G\) are \(1\)-Lipschitz in \(s\), sufficiently small absolute timing errors can be charged directly—but the required instrumental accuracy may itself be severe.
