## 2. Matrix theorem

### Theorem 1 — growth-tensor comparison

Let \(S:[0,T)\to\operatorname{Sym}(n)\) be locally integrable in operator norm. Define \(B(t)=\int_0^t S(s)\,ds\), and let \(U\) solve the equation above. Put

\[
\Lambda(t)=\int_0^t
 c(r(B(s)))\,\|[B(s),[B(s),S(s)]]\|\,ds.
\]

Then, for every \(t<T\),

\[
\boxed{
 e^{-2\Lambda(t)}e^{2B(t)}
 \preceq U(t)U(t)^T
 \preceq e^{2\Lambda(t)}e^{2B(t)}.
}
\tag{1}
\]

Here \(\preceq\) denotes the order on symmetric matrices defined by nonnegative quadratic forms. In particular,

\[
\|U(t)\|\leq
 e^{\lambda_{\max}(B(t))+\Lambda(t)},
\qquad
\sigma_{\min}(U(t))\geq
 e^{\lambda_{\min}(B(t))-\Lambda(t)}.
\tag{2}
\]

There is no smallness assumption on \(\int_0^t\|S\|\), and no convergence assumption on an infinite Magnus or Fer product. The single transformed generator used in the proof is exact.

The same proof applies to bounded self-adjoint operators on a real or complex Hilbert space, with adjoints replacing transposes and norm-Bochner-integrable drivers. It does not automatically apply to unbounded operators with time-dependent domains.

### Proof

Set \(V=e^{-B}U\). Differentiating the matrix exponential gives

\[
\dot V=RV,
\qquad
R=e^{-B}Se^B-\int_0^1e^{-\theta B}Se^{\theta B}\,d\theta.
\tag{3}
\]

Equation (3) is the classical first Fer remainder [1, Section 2.10.1, equations (99)–(101)]. It is reproduced to specify the exact starting identity, not as a novelty claim.

Write \(\operatorname{ad}_B(X)=[B,X]\). Because \(B,S\) are symmetric, odd iterates \(\operatorname{ad}_B^{2j+1}S\) are skew-symmetric and even iterates are symmetric. Therefore

\[
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{sym}R
&=\left(\cosh(\operatorname{ad}_B)
 -\frac{\sinh(\operatorname{ad}_B)}{\operatorname{ad}_B}\right)S\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{2j}{(2j+1)!}\operatorname{ad}_B^{2j}S.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4}
\]

The quotient is interpreted by its entire power series, so there is no division by a singular operator. The first terms of the full remainder are

\[
R=-\frac12[B,S]+\frac13[B,[B,S]]
 -\frac18[B,[B,[B,S]]]+\cdots.
\]

For every matrix \(X\), centering \(B\) at the midpoint of its spectrum gives

\[
\|[B,X]\|\leq r(B)\|X\|.
\tag{5}
\]

Factoring two commutators out of (4), applying (5) to the remaining ones, and summing the positive series yields

\[
\begin{aligned}
\|\operatorname{sym}R\|
&\leq \sum_{j=1}^{\infty}
 \frac{2j\,r(B)^{2j-2}}{(2j+1)!}
 \|[B,[B,S]]\|\\
&=c(r(B))\|[B,[B,S]]\|.
\end{aligned}
\tag{6}
\]

For any solution \(v(t)=V(t)v_0\),

\[
\frac{d}{dt}|v|^2=2v^T(\operatorname{sym}R)v.
\]

Thus

\[
e^{-\Lambda(t)}|v_0|\leq |V(t)v_0|
\leq e^{\Lambda(t)}|v_0|.
\]

All singular values of \(V\) lie between these bounds. Consequently

\[
e^{-2\Lambda}I\preceq VV^T\preceq e^{2\Lambda}I.
\]

Congruence by \(e^B\), using \(U=e^BV\), proves (1). Equation (2) follows. The differentiations are valid almost everywhere for the stated locally integrable driver, or first for smooth drivers and then by local approximation. ∎
