### 3.3 Restricted bilinear norm

Define

\[
 q_k(G)=\sup_{\substack{\|x\|=\|y\|=1\\\operatorname{SR}(x),\operatorname{SR}(y)\le k}}
                  |\langle x,Gy\rangle|. \tag{6}
\]

For any eta>0,

\[
 \Pr\{q_k(G)>\eta\}
 \le4\exp\left[36dk-\frac{d^2\eta^2}{16}\right]. \tag{7}
\]

Here is a full net proof, including the often problematic uniform extension step.

**Rank-net size.** Identify vectors with d-by-d matrices by vectorization. A rank-at-most-k unit Frobenius matrix has SVD U diag(s) V*, where U,V are complex d-by-k isometries and s is a nonnegative unit vector in R^k. The set of isometries has an eta_0-net in the operator norm of size at most (1+2/eta_0)^(2dk): use disjoint eta_0/2 balls in the ambient real 2dk-dimensional normed space, all contained in the ball of radius 1+eta_0/2. Choose the net centers within the isometries. The s set has a Euclidean eta_0-net of size at most (1+2/eta_0)^k, again with unit centers.

The three factor approximations give Frobenius error at most 3 eta_0. Thus the rank set has an epsilon-net, with centers still of rank at most k and norm one, of cardinality

\[
 (1+6/\epsilon)^{(4d+1)k}.
\]

Set epsilon=1/8, giving 49^((4d+1)k).

**Extension without a dimension logarithm.** Split the singular values of a rank-at-most-2k vector into two groups of at most k. The sum of the norms of the two pieces is at most sqrt(2). Applying this separately to x and y proves

\[
 q_{\min(2k,d)}(G)\le2q_k(G).
\]

Approximating each of x,y by its rank-k net center gives

\[
 q_k(G)\le Q_{\rm net}+2\epsilon q_{\min(2k,d)}(G)
 \le Q_{\rm net}+4\epsilon q_k(G).
\]

Therefore q_k<=2 Q_net. No sampled low-rank optimization is substituted for this universal bound.

**Gaussian tail.** For fixed complex x,y, the real and imaginary parts of <x,Gy> are Gaussian quadratic pairings with Hermitian test matrices (yx*+xy*)/2 and (yx*-xy*)/(2i), respectively. Each test has HS norm at most one. Hence

\[
 \Pr\{|\langle x,Gy\rangle|>u\}\le4e^{-d^2u^2/4}.
\]

Union over two nets, with u=eta/2, gives

\[
 4\exp\left[2(4d+1)k\log49-d^2\eta^2/16\right].
\]

For d>=2 the entropy is at most 36dk, since 2(4d+1)<=9d and log49<4. This proves (7).

### 3.4 The two regimes

For d>=C k, using eta=1/64 in (7) yields

\[
 \Pr\{q_k>1/64\}\le4e^{-d^2/131072}. \tag{8}
\]

For d>=C r^3, using eta=1/(64r) and k=r yields

\[
 \Pr\{q_r>1/(64r)\}\le4e^{-d^2/(131072r^2)}. \tag{9}
\]

For example, the exponent comparison in (9) is

\[
 \frac{d^2}{65536r^2}-36dr
 \ge\frac{d^2}{131072r^2},
\]

using d>=2^23 r^3. In either regime, the sum of the relevant failure probability and (4),(5) is less than one. Indeed at the smallest allowed d each exponent already exceeds 100. Thus the intersection of all required good events has positive probability. Boundaries can be avoided; one can take all useful inequalities strict.
