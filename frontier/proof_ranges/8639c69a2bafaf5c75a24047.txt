Result: tolerant quantum forecasting can be much harder than exact verification
The strongest result is a local-access impossibility theorem, rather than another imaginary-time reconstruction bound.
It establishes the following separation:
A simple local experiment can certify exact agreement with a proposed trajectory using polynomially many observations. But requiring the procedure to accept approximately correct trajectories can make certification superpolynomially expensive—even for a connected, gapped quantum system whose Hamiltonian is already known up to one bit.

The lower bound uses ordinary real-time evolution, permits arbitrary active controls at the accessible sites, and concerns the future state of just one qubit. It does not assume an imaginary-time filter or candidate-dependent many-body preparations.
The proof and finite-coefficient construction are complete within the assumptions below. Independent proof validation and historical novelty remain unresolved.

1. The distinction that matters
The previous active-filter theorem supplied a sound, one-sided certificate: passing established predictive accuracy, but failing did not establish inaccuracy. In particular, it could reject a candidate whose entire requested trajectory was already approximately correct. Its coherent preparations and programmable filter were also substantive experimental resources.    research_note    research_note
The new result shows that the absence of a tolerant converse is not necessarily a technical defect in a particular certificate.
Under restricted physical access, these can be fundamentally different tasks:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{Exact-agreement testing:}&\quad
E_T=0\ \text{versus}\ E_T\ge\varepsilon,\\
\text{Tolerant testing:}&\quad
E_T\le\eta\ \text{versus}\ E_T\ge 3/4.
\end{aligned}
\]
Here \(E_T\) measures the error in the requested prediction—not a norm distance between the complete Hamiltonians.
The construction makes the first task polynomial and the second superpolynomial under the same experimental interface.
2. The experimental model
Consider a qubit chain. A set \(S\) of sites is accessible for preparation and control; call these the ports.
Each experiment starts with the remaining qubits in the known vacuum state. Within \(S\), the experimenter may prepare arbitrary states, use arbitrary quantum memory, and apply arbitrarily strong controls, measurements, resets and feedback.
The unknown system undergoes native real-time evolution for at most \(\tau\) during that experiment. Splitting the evolution into many controlled segments does not evade this cap. Starting another experiment requires a fresh vacuum outside \(S\).
For the lower bound, I grant an additional capability: the entire chain may be returned as a quantum output at the end, with arbitrary collective processing across experiments. Thus the theorem is stronger than a lower bound restricted to local terminal readout.
All attempted, discarded and postselected experiments count. Bath resets, control operations and computation are granted free in the lower bound; charging them cannot make the task easier.
Two limitations are essential: the experimenter cannot prepare the inaccessible bath arbitrarily, and cannot retain one evolving physical sample beyond the per-experiment duration cap.
The prediction target
Let
\[
\mathsf N=\sum_x n_x,\qquad n_x=|1\rangle\langle1|_x.
\]
The proposed model is simply
\[
K=\mathsf N.
\]
Prepare
\[
|\psi\rangle=|+\rangle_a\otimes|0\rangle_{\text{other sites}},
\]
where \(a\in S\). The candidate predicts a known phase rotation of qubit \(a\).
Define
\[
E_T(H)=
\sup_{0\le t\le T}
D\!\left(\rho_H^a(t),\rho_K^a(t)\right),
\qquad
D(\rho,\sigma)=\frac12\|\rho-\sigma\|_1.
\]
Only this one-qubit reduced trajectory must be certified.
