The central issue is to construct \(A\) so that it simultaneously produces positive transition rates, long observational concealment, and a short controlled response.
Use a uniform Boolean state space \(\Omega=\mathbb F_2^d\), with Walsh characters
\[ \chi_a(x)=(-1)^{a\cdot x}. \]
These form an orthonormal basis. Choose a character \(f\), put \(\ell=2r-1\), and choose characters \(c_1,\ldots,c_\ell\) such that
\[ 1,\ f,\ c_1,\ldots,c_\ell,\ d_1,\ldots,d_\ell, \qquad d_j=fc_j, \]
are all distinct.
Define \(A\) on this basis by
\[ Af=d_1, \qquad Ac_j=d_{j+1}\quad(1\le j<\ell), \qquad Ac_\ell=f, \tag{3} \]
and set it to zero on every other basis character.
Then
\[ A1=A^*1=0,\qquad \|A\|_{2\to2}=1,\qquad A^3=0. \]
In particular, the hidden propagator is unusually simple:
\[ \boxed{ e^{tQ_r} = J+e^{-t} \left[ I-J+\varepsilon tA+\frac{\varepsilon^2t^2}{2}A^2 \right]. } \tag{4} \]
But observation introduces another operator:
\[ Dg=fg. \]
It acts by
\[ D1=f,\quad Df=1,\quad Dc_j=d_j,\quad Dd_j=c_j. \]
Interleaving evolution and observation therefore creates the long sequence
\[ f \xrightarrow{A}d_1 \xrightarrow{D}c_1 \xrightarrow{A}d_2 \xrightarrow{D}\cdots \xrightarrow{D}c_\ell \xrightarrow{A}f. \tag{5} \]
Every closed scalar observation word containing \(A\) must use at least \(2r\) copies of \(A\). Repeated observation cannot skip a rung.
This is the relevant distinction: simple hidden time evolution does not imply shallow observable structure.
There are \(2r\) nonzero Walsh entries in \(A\). In the ordinary state basis,
\[ A_{xy} = \frac1n \sum_{\text{ladder edges }a\to b} \chi_b(x)\chi_a(y), \]
so
\[ |A_{xy}|\le\frac{2r}{n}, \qquad K:=\max_x\sum_y|A_{xy}|\le2r. \]
With \(\varepsilon=1/(16r)\), equation (2) consequently gives
\[ \frac{7}{8n}\le q_{xy}\le\frac{9}{8n} \]
off the diagonal. Zero row and column sums of \(A\) establish both the generator property and the uniform stationary law.
The construction therefore produces an actual irreducible stochastic system, not a signed representation whose physical realization is deferred.
The necessary characters and symmetry can be acquired explicitly with \(n\le16r\).
For \(r\ge2\), take
\[ d=\max\{4,\ 3+\lceil\log_2(r-1)\rceil\}. \]
On character indices, define an involution \(\Theta\) fixing \(e_0,e_1\), sending
\[ e_2\mapsto e_2+e_0, \qquad e_3\mapsto e_3+e_1, \]
and fixing the remaining coordinate vectors. Put \(f=e_0\). Choose distinct \(z_j\) in the span of \(e_2,e_4,\ldots,e_{d-1}\), and set
\[ c_j=e_3+z_j,\qquad c_r=e_2,\qquad c_{2r-j}=f+\Theta c_j \quad(1\le j<r). \]
Their coordinate patterns verify the required distinctness. The case \(r=1\) uses four states.
The corresponding permutation \(R\) of microscopic states preserves \(f\) and satisfies
\[ RAR=A^*,\qquad RQ_rR=Q_r^*. \tag{6} \]
Since stationary reversal has generator \(Q_r^*\), this proves that the complete observed path law is exactly time-reversal invariant.
The permutation \(R\) is an auxiliary mathematical symmetry. It is not substituted for the physical reversal convention in the entropy-production formula.
For stationary observations at arbitrary times, moments are matrix products of \(D\) and the propagators in (4), between constant vectors.
The ladder property has two consequences.
First, at every collection of at most \(2r\) times, the complete joint distribution of \(Y\) is exactly the equilibrium distribution. Equality of all subset moments establishes equality of binary joint laws.
Second, at \(2r+1\) times there is a unique first nonzero odd contribution. For \(t_0<\cdots<t_{2r}\),
\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E\prod_{j=0}^{2r}Y_{t_j} = \varepsilon^{2r} e^{-(t_{2r}-t_0)} \prod_{j=1}^{2r}(t_j-t_{j-1}) >0. } \tag{7} \]
The equilibrium reference has every odd moment zero.
In particular,
\[ \mathbb E[Y_0Y_t]=e^{-t} \]
for every \(r\), exactly.
However, matching low-order observations is not sufficient to prove an exponential lower bound for a full trajectory. A continuous record includes much more information. The following argument closes that gap.
Decompose
\[ Q_r=\frac12(J-I)+B, \qquad B=\frac12(J-I)+\varepsilon A. \tag{8} \]
The operator \(B\) is itself a valid generator. The first term can be implemented by an independent Poisson clock of rate \(1/2\), which resets the microscopic state to an independent uniform state.
Give the passive observer these reset times as extra information. This only makes the testing problem easier.
Between resets, compare evolution under \(B\) with evolution under
\[ B_0=\frac12(J-I). \]
For a segment of length \(t\), expand every observation-record probability in a time-ordered perturbation series in \(\varepsilon A\). Label observations are the projections
\[ \Pi_\pm=\frac{I\pm D}{2}. \]
The ladder implies that every coefficient containing between one and \(2r-1\) insertions of \(A\) vanishes, irrespective of the number of observations.
To bound the remaining terms, use signed row-vector \(\ell^1\) norms. Markov propagation contracts this norm. Summing the two measurement branches preserves the aggregate norm. Each insertion of \(A\) costs at most \(K\le2r\). Thus
\[ \operatorname{TV}(\text{segment laws}) \le g_r(t) := \frac12\sum_{j\ge2r}\frac{(\varepsilon Kt)^j}{j!}. \tag{9} \]
The estimate is uniform over all finite observation grids. Refining rational-time grids extends it to the full observed path.
For an exponentially distributed segment length \(E\) of rate \(\lambda=1/2\),
\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E g_r(E) &= \frac12\sum_{j\ge2r} \left(\frac{\varepsilon K}{\lambda}\right)^j\\ &\le \frac12\sum_{j\ge2r}4^{-j} = \frac23\,16^{-r}. \end{aligned} \tag{10} \]
An interval of length \(T\) has expected \(1+T/2\) segment starts. Conditional on the reset times, segments start independently. Summing their coupling errors, including the truncated final segment, gives
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{TV} \left( \mathsf P_r^{[0,T]}, \mathsf P_{\mathrm{eq}}^{[0,T]} \right) \le \frac23\left(1+\frac T2\right)16^{-r}. } \tag{11} \]
Removing the extra reset-time information cannot increase total variation.
The optimal equal-prior testing success is \((1+\operatorname{TV})/2\). Equation (11) therefore proves the exponential observation-time lower bound.
