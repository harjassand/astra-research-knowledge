5. Construction: a small harmless component conceals a consequential phase shift
The missing mathematical ingredient was a positive spectral construction satisfying two requirements simultaneously:
- one system remains uniformly close to the simple candidate;
- the other has nearly identical local short-time structure but develops a large later phase error.
Positive moment matching
Put
\[
m=2d,\qquad \delta=\frac{\eta^2}{64d^2},
\]
and use the shifted Chebyshev nodes
\[
x_j=
\delta+(1-\delta)\frac{1-\cos(j\pi/m)}2,
\qquad j=0,\ldots,m.
\]
Let
\[
w_j=\prod_{k\ne j}\frac{-x_k}{x_j-x_k}.
\]
Lagrange interpolation gives
\[
p(0)=\sum_jw_jp(x_j)
\]
for every polynomial of degree at most \(m\). The weights alternate in sign.
Their total absolute mass is exactly
\[
\Lambda
=
T_m\!\left(\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}\right)
=
\cosh\!\left(2m\,\operatorname{artanh}\sqrt\delta\right).
\]
Set \(A=(\Lambda+1)/2\), and define probability measures
\[
\nu_g
=
\frac{\delta_0+\sum_{j\text{ odd}}|w_j|\delta_{x_j}}{A},
\qquad
\nu_b
=
\frac{\sum_{j\text{ even}}w_j\delta_{x_j}}{A}.
\tag{7}
\]
Their moments agree through degree \(2d\).
The good measure has mass \(1-\rho\) at zero, where
\[
\boxed{
\rho
=
\frac{\Lambda-1}{\Lambda+1}
=
\tanh^2\!\left(m\,\operatorname{artanh}\sqrt\delta\right)
\le\frac{\eta^2}{9}.
}
\tag{8}
\]
The bad measure has mass at least \(1-\rho\) at \(\delta\).
Therefore, for every time,
\[
1-\Re F_g(t)\le2\rho,
\]
whereas at
\[
t_*=\frac{\pi}{\delta}<T,
\]
\[
\Re F_b(t_*)\le-1+2\rho.
\tag{9}
\]
The first response has only a small persistent deviation. The second accumulates an almost opposite phase.
The contamination in (8) is not chosen arbitrarily. It is optimal for this moment-matching problem: applying the same Chebyshev polynomial to any competing measures gives
\[
(1-\rho)\Lambda-\rho\le1,
\]
hence \(\rho\ge(\Lambda-1)/(\Lambda+1)\). The construction attains equality. This is an application of established Chebyshev extremality, not a claim to have invented that approximation theorem.
Converting the measures into local Hamiltonians
Orthonormalizing the monomials produces Jacobi matrices \(A_g,A_b\), with first-site spectral measures \(\nu_g,\nu_b\).
Matching moments through \(2d\) makes every hopping coefficient and all but the final diagonal coefficient identical. A trace calculation gives
\[
\boxed{
A_b-A_g
=
\frac{1+\delta}{2}|d\rangle\langle d|.
}
\tag{10}
\]
Thus a substantial change at one distant boundary implements the spectral distinction.
Place \(A_g\) on the left arm and \(A_b\) on the right, joined by a hopping
\[
\gamma=\frac{\eta}{16T}.
\]
Reflecting the arms defines the other hypothesis. Promote these matrices to qubit Hamiltonians with hopping terms and add
\[
\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}n_xn_y.
\]
These are connected interacting chains. Their full spectra coincide by reflection.
The hopping-plus-onsite part has one-particle matrix bounded below by \((1-\gamma)I\). On a path, its fermionic diagonalization makes the many-body operator at least \((1-\gamma)\mathsf N\). The added density interactions are nonnegative. Hence the vacuum gap is at least \(1-\gamma>0.9\).
For the target input, the dynamics remain in vacuum plus one excitation. Equations (8)–(9) give good-case error below \(\eta/2\) and bad-case local error at least \(1-\rho\) before adding the joining bond. Duhamel’s identity bounds the bond’s effect through \(T\) by
\[
\gamma T=\eta/16,
\]
preserving (1).
The target trajectory is low-excitation, although the validation protocol is allowed to inject many excitations. I am not claiming a hard finite-density many-body prediction theorem.
