## 2. Why mechanical matching survives different isotope moduli

For signed sequence s = (1,-1,-1,1,-1,1,1,-1),

    sum_j s_j j^n = 0, n=0,1,2.

The generating polynomial is

    sum_j s_j x^j = (1-x)(1-x^2)(1-x^4).

Its third-order zero at x=1 proves the moment identities. They also hold for the exact layer integrals over equally thick adjacent intervals. This is a known Prouhet/Thue–Morse construction, not a new mathematical sequence [8,9].

For a common-orientation, perfectly bonded linear plate with reduced in-plane stiffness Q(z), the laminate matrices are

    A_el = integral Q(z) dz,
    B_el = integral z Q(z) dz,
    D_el = integral z^2 Q(z) dz.

The difference Q_A(z)-Q_B(z) is a piecewise signed multiple of Q_L-Q_H. All three matrices are therefore equal for A and B, for arbitrary fixed isotope-dependent Q_L and Q_H. Thermal force and bending resultants also match when each isotope has a fixed local Q alpha product and temperature is uniform. The code verifies the underlying moment identities with unequal coefficients and includes an unbalanced equal-inventory counterexample.

This is a statement about a restricted static mechanical model. It does not match interfacial slip, adhesion, wrinkles, twist-domain response, local strain near an edge, spatially variable contamination, nonlinear bending or dynamic acoustic surface response. Nuclear-spin-dependent environmental effects are also not removed merely by matching isotope counts. They require separate bounds if a specifically electromagnetic interpretation is claimed.

Using arbitrary unequal coefficients in the identity tests is not a claim that monolayer elastic moduli can be inserted unchanged into a 90 nm bulk laminate. The equality is coefficient-independent within the model. Actual film stiffness and strain must be measured.
