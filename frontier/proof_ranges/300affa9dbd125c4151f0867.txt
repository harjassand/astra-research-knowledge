For \(n\) sites with bounded local interaction scale \(J\), a common bound \(M=O(nJ)\) gives the sufficient observation count
\[ O\!\left( \frac{n^2J^2T^2}{\varepsilon^2}\log\frac1\alpha \right). \]
That is not a claimed optimal dependence on \(n\) within the local subclass.
Imaginary-time filtering itself remains an experimental-resource assumption. Ordinary Gibbs-state samples are not automatically a filter acting coherently on a submitted input. No such conversion is assumed.
A proposed \(K\) must be fixed before the validation data, or checked on fresh data after selection.
The theorem avoids learning \(H\), but it does not eliminate the cost of producing and implementing the proposed model. It is also specific to the prepared initial state, not a certificate for every possible input.
Take
\[ H=0,\qquad K=M|1\rangle\langle1|, \qquad \psi=\sqrt{1-z^2}|0\rangle+z|1\rangle. \]
The two density trajectories differ by at most \(2z\), for every time.
Nevertheless, every paired-probe experiment has rejection probability at least half of
\[ p_w= \frac{z^2(1-z^2)(1-a)^2}      {1-z^2+a^2z^2} >0. \]
Thus, with \(z=\varepsilon/4\), the model is already within \(\varepsilon/2\) throughout the trajectory, but the strict zero-rejection procedure can reject it for large \(T\).
This establishes the exact limitation:
Passing provides a valid predictive certificate. Failing does not prove that the requested predictions are inaccurate.
A fully tolerant test separating approximately correct trajectories from substantially incorrect ones remains unresolved here.
If implementation errors can change each rejection probability by at most \(\eta\), the conservative certificate becomes
\[ \mathcal E_T \le 28MT\sqrt{p_U+\eta}, \]
where \(p_U\) is a statistical upper confidence bound.
Long-time accuracy therefore requires
\[ \eta = O\!\left(\frac{\varepsilon^2}{M^2T^2}\right). \]
