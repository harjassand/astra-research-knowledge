## Quantitative bounds and examples

The CO₂ centrifuge study observed a broad rotational distribution with a high-energy tail at $J=364$, $E_{\rm rot}=6.14\,\mathrm{eV}$ per molecule. It reports 4 or 7 mJ pulse energy at the sample in the experimental conditions reviewed here and a 10 Hz amplifier rate. The article does not report the number of molecules in the high-$J$ tail or the fraction of that pulse energy transferred to them, so both are `UNKNOWN` here. At 380 Torr and an assumed 300 K, the ideal-gas number density is $1.22\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$. The reported hot vibrational bands appear after about 500 ps, or three mean collision times, giving an inferred mean collision interval of about 167 ps. These are timing constraints, not measurements of ergotropy survival: the hot bands show rotational-to-vibrational transfer, while collisions may also dephase without destroying all extractable energy.

The source reports the largest CO₂ rotational states exceed the ground-state bond dissociation energy, but a centrifugal barrier prevents those molecules from dissociating. Its quasi-classical trajectory calculations find rotation-to-vibration transfer is strongest around moderate excitation: up to 25% probability per collision for one bending quantum at $J=100$, with little contribution from $J>200$. Thus the very high energy tail that makes the largest activation packet is not the state range most effective at transferring energy into vibration. At high $J$, rigid-rotor transition energies also fail: the paper reports rigid-rotor Raman-shift errors as large as 61.5 cm⁻¹ (0.29 eV). The script's $J=364\to362$ gap is explicitly only a low-J-constant scale estimate, not a high-J prediction.

The ergotropy code includes a deliberately idealized state-space example. For a rigid rotor with all $m$ states accessible, a uniform mixture over the $2J+1$ magnetic substates at one $J$ has a passive rearrangement energy of only 1.74% of its initial energy at $J=57$, and 0.59% at $J=170$. The corresponding ideal rearrangement ceilings are therefore 98.26% and 99.41%. This is not a measured ensemble ergotropy, an extraction efficiency, or a yield prediction. It assumes all $m$ substates within the same electronic and nuclear-spin block can be unitarily rearranged. The SiO⁺ sample's actual density matrix and an allowed extraction protocol are not measured.

The SiO⁺ experiment quotes the $j\approx170$ internal-rotation scale as roughly $100k_BT$ at 300 K, or 2.59 eV per ion. This substantial stored-energy scale is still not a recovery result: the actual optical input, fraction stored in the rotor, and energy returned to an optical or microwave mode were not measured.

At 380 Torr, a Poisson collision model using the inferred 167 ps interval gives probabilities of no collision of 94.2%, 74.1%, 54.9%, and 36.7% during 10, 50, 100, and 167 ps respectively. This is a timing exposure estimate only. `derive.py` also reports a toy deceleration result that assumes every collision destroys all remaining extractable work while the rotor's angular momentum is lowered linearly. That model is explicitly adverse and unvalidated; its return fractions are not evidence about real CO₂ recovery. No empirical unloading rate, state survival curve, or collision-to-ergotropy-loss hazard was found.

If one 6.14 eV activation packet were required for each CO molecule, its ideal energy floor would be 592.4 kJ/mol or 5.875 kWh/kg CO. This is calculated from the reported high-$J$ tail, not measured process consumption. It already exceeds the ISPT/TNO 2023 modelled SOEC system consumption of 3.3 kWh/kg CO in its Air Liquide use case by a factor of 1.78, before laser efficiency, excitation fraction, unloading, separation, or nonproductive collisions. The SOEC figure is a feasibility model, not a demonstrated plant result. The comparison rejects this one-packet, one-CO route even if energy recovery from every unchanged reactant were perfect.

The reaction-rate experiment on SiO⁺ reports $0.18(3)\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ when pumped to extreme rotation versus $0.05(2)\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ without optical pumping. At the pumped rate, the single-ion mean reaction interval is about 333 s. The H₂ density was $7(1)\times10^6\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, equivalent at 300 K to about $2.2\times10^{-10}$ Torr and roughly $1.75\times10^{12}$ times below the CO₂ 380-Torr density. The reaction occurs in a dilute ion trap; continuous optical pumping prepares a narrow distribution, and the experiment measures a rate enhancement, not an energy-recovery cycle. The authors' ion-count proxy, laser drift, and mass resolution also limit direct product identification. Conditional renewal arithmetic in `CALCULATIONS.json` shows why high recovery alone is insufficient at low productive probability; it is not a reconstruction of the ion-trap experiment, which does not provide the one-attempt survivor return and holding-work data the model would need.
