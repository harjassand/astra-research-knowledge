\section{Decisive limitations and counterexamples}

\subsection{The extensive order of loss is unavoidable for this fixed readout}
Take independent qubits with $H(h)=\sum_i h_iZ_i$ at $h=0$, with common visibility $\eta$. The SLD Fisher matrix for the $h_i$ is
\[
 F_Q=\beta^2 I_n.
\]
In the fixed random-Pauli measurement, only the $Z$ basis (probability $1/3$) detects the local derivative, and detector visibility multiplies the score by $\eta$. Hence
\begin{equation}
 F_M=\frac{\beta^2\eta^2}{3}I_n.
 \label{eq:sharpness}
\end{equation}
For the collective direction $u=(1,\ldots,1)$, the loss is $\beta^2(1-\eta^2/3)n$. A universal subextensive loss bound for this fixed protocol is false. This is not evidence that entangling measurements are necessary in the example: measuring every qubit in $Z$ is already optimal when that direction is selected in advance.

\subsection{Independent detector noise cannot be replaced by a marginal-visibility condition}
The following fully explicit family satisfies the assumptions of the main theorem on the Hamiltonian and parameter dictionary, but not its detector-independence hypothesis. Consider
\begin{equation}
 H_n(t)=-\frac Jn\sum_{i<j}Z_iZ_j+tM_n,
 \qquad M_n=\sum_i Z_i.
 \label{eq:CW}
\end{equation}
The individual parameter dictionary may be the single-site $Z_i$, with $k=\Delta=1$; $t$ is the all-ones direction. The background has $\ell=2$ and incident weighted strength at most $J$ at $t=0$.

At $t=0$ the state is spin-flip symmetric and $\E M_n=0$. A configuration whose minority spin count is $r\in[n/4,n/2]$ has energy at least $3Jn/8$ above an aligned configuration, since its energy penalty is $(2J/n)r(n-r)$. Counting at most $2^n$ configurations and retaining the two aligned configurations in the partition function gives
\[
 \Pr(|M_n|\le n/2)\le2^{n-1}e^{-3\beta Jn/8}.
\]
For fixed $3\beta J/8>\log2$, therefore,
\begin{equation}
 \Var(M_n)\ge\frac{n^2}{4}\left(1-2^{n-1}e^{-3\beta Jn/8}\right)
 =\Omega(n^2).
 \label{eq:macroscopic}
\end{equation}
Because the parameter commutes with the Hamiltonian, $F_Q(t)=\beta^2\Var(M_n)=\Theta(n^2)$ at zero.

Now let one common detector bit, with probability $p\in(0,1/2)$, reverse \emph{all} recorded signs on a copy. Every individual site has visibility $\eta=1-2p>0$, but its noise is correlated with every other site. For this diagonal model the clean record law obeys $q_t(b,-s)=q_{-t}(b,s)$: non-$Z$ outcomes are conditionally fair, and the diagonal Gibbs distribution is reversed by a global spin flip. The noisy law is
\[
 \widetilde q_t=(1-p)q_t+p q_{-t}.
\]
At zero, $\widetilde q_0=q_0$ while $\partial_t\widetilde q_0=\eta\partial_tq_0$. Consequently
\begin{equation}
 F_{M,\mathrm{common}}(0)=\eta^2F_{M,\mathrm{clean}}(0)
 \le\eta^2F_Q(0).
\end{equation}
The information loss is at least $(1-\eta^2)F_Q(0)=\Omega(n^2)$, not $O(n)$. Thus positive per-site marginal visibility alone is insufficient for the theorem. No claim about preparing this finite-temperature phase mixture rapidly is made.

\subsection{Strong covariance conditioning does not make observed likelihood convex}
Let a single qubit have
\[
 H(x,z)=xX+zZ,\quad r=\sqrt{x^2+z^2},\quad f(r)=\tanh(\beta r)/r.
\]
Conditioned on measuring in the $Z$ basis, the $+1$ probability is $(1-zf(r))/2$. The negative log likelihood for that observation, up to a basis-selection constant, is
\[
 \mathcal L(x,z)=\log2-\log(1-zf(r)).
\]
At $x>0,z=0$,
\begin{equation}
 \nabla^2\mathcal L=
 \begin{pmatrix}0&f'(x)\\ f'(x)&f(x)^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad
 \det\nabla^2\mathcal L=-f'(x)^2<0,
 \label{eq:nonconvex}
\end{equation}
where
\[
 f'(x)=\frac{\beta x\,\operatorname{sech}^2(\beta x)-\tanh(\beta x)}{x^2}<0.
\]
The strict sign follows by differentiating $\tanh y-y\operatorname{sech}^2y$, whose derivative is $2y\operatorname{sech}^2y\tanh y>0$ for $y>0$.

This example lies in a bounded one-site parameter domain with positive thermal covariance. It decisively disproves the inference ``strongly convex log partition function implies a convex observed-data likelihood for local quantum measurement.'' It does not prove computational hardness of all fitting algorithms or rule out a different convex formulation.

\subsection{Other scope boundaries}
The information comparisons are local in parameter space. They do not directly bound finite-displacement testing error, prove global optimization convergence, or solve nuisance-parameter identifiability. Unknown temperature can be confounded with an overall Hamiltonian scale. Exact fixed-charge restrictions can have null parameter directions. At zero temperature full rank is lost. Preparation errors and correlated sampling can invalidate the certificate. None of these interfaces is silently included.

\section{Relationship to prior work and scientific significance}

The proof deliberately separates established ingredients from the assembled theorem. The static-variance/BKM comparison is the Brooks--Harris inequality, reviewed and generalized in \cite{BrankovTonchev}; quantum variance is an established object \cite{FrerotRoscilde}. Random Pauli shadow measurements and their use for local observables are established \cite{HKP}; noisy variants are also established \cite{KohGrewal}. Thermal Hamiltonian-parameter QFI bounds were studied directly in \cite{GarciaPintos}. The moment-score Schur complement and SLD measurement bound are standard information inequalities, with proofs above.

The specific result established here is an \emph{additive, coefficient-matrix, size-independent information-loss bound for a specified parameter-independent noisy product readout}, together with a variance-only operational certificate. The bounded covariance \emph{differences} are central. This differs from merely lower bounding optimal QFI, estimating unitary-encoding QFI, measuring only a small subsystem, or proving Hamiltonian identifiability.

The targeted search did not locate this exact complete statement. That is not an exhaustive novelty or priority clearance. In particular, an inequality obtained by combining established results can be independently useful without being a new foundational principle in the historical sense.

There are already arbitrary-temperature Hamiltonian-learning algorithms \cite{BLMT}, and near-optimal local lattice-learning results \cite{CAN}. September 2026 quantum score matching provides a distinct learning construction \cite{DongLeng}; October 2026 stationarity tests address Gibbs and metastable-state learning \cite{Bergamaschi}. Their algorithmic assumptions, parameter-dependent or derived observables, and associated information functionals are not replaced by a classical-Fisher comparison. We claim neither the first efficient local-learning algorithm nor a computational improvement over them.

What is gained is a concrete answer to a different question: when equilibrium response is collectively enhanced, a blind local detector need not forfeit that enhancement, and the enhancement's accessibility can be certified from its records without a forward many-body calculation. Under a verified global covariance floor, the same theorem would further control readout efficiency for every direction. Without that floor, poorly conditioned directions remain poorly conditioned.

\section{Reproducibility, provenance, and remaining obligations}

The accompanying \texttt{finite\_checks.py} performs 38 finite floating-point checks (seed 20261012): 36 two- to four-qubit Gibbs instances with noncommuting directions, additional background terms absent from the parameter dictionary, three inverse temperatures, and two detector visibilities; and two maximally mixed sharpness cases. It directly enumerates the product POVM, differentiates the Gibbs state, computes the measured and quantum information/covariance matrices, and tests the stated matrix inequalities. The declared tolerances passed. The smallest recorded inequality eigenvalue was approximately $-2.79\times10^{-16}$, and the largest response-identity discrepancy was $8.44\times10^{-15}$. The exact nonconvex formula was also evaluated at $x=\beta=1$. The numerical tests do not prove the universal theorem, establish originality, or independently validate physical assumptions.
