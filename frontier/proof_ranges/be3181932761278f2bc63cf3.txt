### Sharpness of the local coefficient

Take

\[
B=\begin{pmatrix}r/2&0\\0&-r/2\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
S=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Then \([B,[B,S]]=r^2S\), while

\[
\operatorname{sym}R=
 \left(\cosh r-\frac{\sinh r}{r}\right)S.
\]

Equality holds in (6). Hence \(c(r)\) is the smallest universal coefficient depending only on the spectral width for that pointwise inequality. This does not prove that all global constants in (1) are optimal.

### Geometric interpretation

At a time when a differentiable eigenframe exists, write \(B=QDQ^T\), with \(D=\operatorname{diag}(b_i)\) and \(\Omega=Q^T\dot Q\) skew-symmetric. Then

\[
Q^TSQ=\dot D+[\Omega,D].
\]

In this frame,

\[
\bigl(Q^T[B,[B,S]]Q\bigr)_{ij}
=-(b_i-b_j)^3\Omega_{ij}\quad(i\ne j),
\]

and the diagonal is zero. The correction measures eigenframe rotation weighted by the **cube** of the integrated anisotropy gap. Radial/eigenvalue changes alone do not contribute to it. Equation (6), unlike this interpretation, needs no smooth choice of eigenvectors and remains valid at degeneracies.

For a nonsymmetric driver \(A=S+W\), with \(W^T=-W\), an exact orthogonal frame \(O'=WO\) reduces the equation to the symmetric driver \(O^TSO\). The theorem then concerns its co-rotating primitive. Acquiring that frame is an additional task; it must not be treated as free data.
