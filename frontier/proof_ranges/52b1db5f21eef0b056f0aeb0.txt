3. The mathematical mechanism
The operation removes weak connections and contracts strong connections, but uses separate additive budgets in conductance and resistance rather than an uncontrolled threshold.
A terminal-flux sandwich
Delete a set of edges \(D\), with total conductance
\[
S=\sum_{e\in D}c_e.
\]
In the retained graph, select connected clusters to contract, without merging \(s\) and \(t\). Choose a spanning tree within each cluster, giving a forest \(F\), and define
\[
r=\sum_{e\in F}\frac1{c_e}.
\]
Let \(H\) be the resulting reduced terminal component, with capacity \(C_H\).
Then
\[
\boxed{
\frac{C_H}{1+C_Hr}
\;\leq\;
C_G
\;\leq\;
C_H+S.
}
\tag{1}
\]
This bounds the requested terminal response, not all eigenvalues, all transition probabilities, or the complete dynamics.
Why the bound holds
Deletion error. A capacity-minimising potential can be clipped to \([0,1]\). Restoring a deleted edge therefore adds at most its conductance to the trial energy. If \(C_d\) is the capacity after deletion,
\[
C_d\leq C_G\leq C_d+S.
\]
Contraction error. Contraction lowers effective resistance. Conversely, lift the reduced graph’s unit electrical flow back through the cluster trees. The reduced electrical flow decomposes into simple terminal paths of total flow one. Consequently, each chosen tree edge needs to carry absolute flow at most one. The additional dissipated energy is at most \(r\).
Writing \(R=1/C\),
\[
R_H\leq R_d\leq R_H+r.
\]
Combining these inequalities gives (1).
