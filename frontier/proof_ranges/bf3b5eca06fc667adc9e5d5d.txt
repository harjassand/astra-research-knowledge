## 2. Ideal arbitrary-control identifiability

**Theorem 1 (polarization identifies the unordered law in the ideal limit).** Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be exchangeable probability laws on \(\mathcal A^n\), where \(\mathcal A\) is a known age domain. Suppose:

1. the actual feed-label probability can be programmed to any measurable \(p:\mathcal A\to[p_-,p_+]\), with \(0\leq p_-<p_+\leq1\);
2. the exact whole-product count distribution is available for every such program, with the control and product-time clocks aligned; and
3. the conditional independent-label model (1) is valid.

If \(P_{\mu,p}(K=k)=P_{\nu,p}(K=k)\) for every \(k\) and every allowed \(p\), then \(\mu=\nu\). Equivalently, ideal arbitrary waveforms and exact count histograms identify the full unordered \(n\)-atom entry-age law. Atom positions and microscopic reaction states remain outside the identified object.

**Proof.** It is enough to use the top-count bin
\[
H_\mu[p]:=P_{\mu,p}(K=n)=\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{i=1}^n p(A_i).
\tag{2}
\]
This is a homogeneous degree-\(n\) polynomial functional. Set \(p_0=(p_-+p_+)/2\). For any measurable \(q:\mathcal A\to[0,1]\), \(p_0+tq\) is an allowed control for all \(t\) in some open interval around zero. The function \(t\mapsto H_\mu[p_0+tq]\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\); its coefficient of \(t^n\) is \(H_\mu[q]\). Exact values on an interval determine this coefficient, so equality of the observations implies \(H_\mu[q]=H_\nu[q]\) for every \(q\in[0,1]^{\mathcal A}\).

Now take bounded nonnegative test functions \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\). The coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_n\) in
\[
H_\mu\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^n t_j f_j\right]
\]
is
\[
\sum_{\pi\in S_n}\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{j=1}^n f_{\pi(j)}(A_j)
=n!\,\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{j=1}^n f_j(A_j),
\tag{3}
\]
where the final equality uses exchangeability. The coefficient is determined by values of \(H_\mu\) on a positive open box of sufficiently small \(t_j\). Taking \(f_j=\mathbf 1_{B_j}\) in (3) recovers \(\mu(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)\) for every measurable rectangle. Rectangles determine the probability measure \(\mu\), hence the law of the unordered multiset. \(\square\)

The proof is an identifiability theorem, not a finite-cost reconstruction algorithm. It uses exact probabilities for an open family of waveforms and, on an unbounded age domain, control over the entire relevant prehistory. Full count histograms are sufficient because they contain \(P(K=n)\); the other bins can improve estimation or support other target functionals.

The interval-valued control assumption matters. The source telegraph implementation uses only two composition levels. Arbitrary timing of a binary low/high waveform is not by itself equivalent to access to every real-valued \(p\).

**Proposition 1 (two-level controls need not identify the full law).** Use six atoms and three possible ages \(0,\tau,2\tau\). Let \(N=(N_0,N_1,N_2)\) be the occupancy vector. In model E, choose uniformly among the even permutations of \((0,1,5)\); in model O, choose uniformly among the odd permutations. For any binary waveform on these three nodes, let \(B\) be the subset of nodes assigned the high isotope probability. The number \(J_B\) of atoms at high probability is \(\sum_{i\in B}N_i\). If \(B\) is a singleton, this has the same uniform \(\{0,1,5\}\) law in E and O; if \(B\) has two nodes, it is six minus a singleton count and also has the same law; empty/full \(B\) give constants. Conditional on \(J_B\), the whole-product count law is the sum of \(J_B\) Bernoulli(\(p_{\rm high}\)) and \(6-J_B\) Bernoulli(\(p_{\rm low}\)) labels. Thus **every binary waveform on this grid gives the same whole-product count distribution**, even across all eight possible masks and at any number of repeated readouts. Yet the unordered laws differ: the event \(N=(0,1,5)\) has probability \(1/3\) in E and \(0\) in O. This countermodel shows that the full-law conclusion of Theorem 1 cannot be claimed for an interface restricted to two feed levels. The selected \(Q_6\) happens to agree for this pair; it remains a separately observable functional.
