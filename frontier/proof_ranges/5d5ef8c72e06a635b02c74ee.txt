4. The main theorem and its proof mechanism
Let \(\mu_\zeta\) denote the invariant law of the complex process. The central result derived in the note is
\[
\boxed{
Z(\zeta)\,\mathbb E_{\mu_\zeta}O(X)
=
\int_{\mathbb R^n}O(x)e^{-S_\zeta(x)}\,dx
}
\]
for every holomorphic polynomial \(O\), throughout the certified parameter rectangle.
The proof is supplied in the packet, but has not received independent review.
First: construct an analytic stochastic operation
Use the implicit step
\[
Y_{k+1}+h\nabla S_\zeta(Y_{k+1})
=
Y_k+\sqrt{2h}\,\xi_k,
\qquad \xi_k\sim N(0,I_n).
\]
Strong accretivity proves existence and uniqueness of this root inside the tube. The root map contracts by
\[
q_h=(1+h\lambda)^{-1}.
\]
Its Jacobian is nonsingular, so the holomorphic implicit-function theorem makes each finite trajectory holomorphic in \(\zeta\).
Uniform polynomial moments allow expectations to pass through parameter contour integrals. Contraction then gives a holomorphic stationary expectation for each step size. A uniform-in-time discretisation estimate allows \(h\to0\).
This avoids assuming analyticity in stochastic time—an assumption with known pitfalls in complex Langevin arguments. DOI
Second: identify the real anchor
For real \(\zeta\) near zero, the process stays real and samples the ordinary strongly convex Gibbs law. Therefore
\[
Z(\zeta)\,\mathbb E_{\mu_\zeta}O(X)
=
\int O(x)e^{-S_\zeta(x)}dx
\]
holds on a real interval.
Both sides are holomorphic functions of \(\zeta\). The identity theorem extends their equality to the complex rectangle.
Third: prove nonvanishing without assuming it
Crucially, the preceding argument establishes a product identity. It does not first divide by the potentially tiny or vanishing \(Z\).
Taking \(O=W\) gives
\[
Z'(\zeta)=Z(\zeta)\,\mathbb E_{\mu_\zeta}W(X).
\]
The coefficient on the right is holomorphic. Since \(Z(0)>0\), this scalar differential equation implies that \(Z\) has no zeros in the rectangle.
Only then is division legitimate:
\[
\boxed{
L=\int_0^1 i\,\mathbb E_{\mu_{it}}W(X)\,dt.
}
\]
The answer is obtained by estimating polynomial moments, rather than resolving an exponentially small phase average.
General necessary-and-sufficient correctness conditions already exist in the literature. The proposed distinction is a finite coefficient test for this model family, not the first mathematical criterion for complex Langevin correctness. arXiv
5. Why stability alone does not suffice
A concrete counterexample separates contraction from correctness.
Consider the real target
\[
S(z)=\frac{z^4}{4}-\frac{z^2}{2}.
\]
Its desired real-cycle second moment is positive.
Now use the process
\[
dZ=(Z^3-Z)\,dt+i\sqrt2\,dB.
\]
Starting on \(Z=iY\) gives
\[
dY=-(Y^3+Y)\,dt+\sqrt2\,dB.
\]
This process is strongly contracting. Nevertheless, it produces
\[
\mathbb E[Z^2]=-\mathbb E[Y^2]<0.
\]
Independent quadrature gives
\[
\text{desired real-cycle moment}\approx1.04179729649,
\]
\[
\text{contractive imaginary-cycle moment}\approx-0.46791991697.
\]
Thus a stable stochastic computation can return an answer with the wrong sign. The analytic anchor identifies the intended integral through a stronger argument than convergence.
This example illustrates the known integration-cycle obstruction; it is not presented as the first discovery of wrong-cycle convergence.
