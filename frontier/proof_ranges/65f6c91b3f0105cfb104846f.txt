## 4. Lower bound for the complete active interface

**Theorem.** Suppose an algorithm can independently reset the preparation and, within each reset, use arbitrary adaptive random stopping, all the primitive and product settings above, arbitrary visible readouts and arbitrary observation time. If at most r resets are used, the total variation distance between its null transcript law and its transcript law averaged over independent uniform xi_i is at most

    r(r-1)/(2M).                                      (8)

This remains true for a strictly stronger oracle that, immediately after every reset and at no cost, reveals the entire preselected pair (H,Z).

**Proof.** Given (H,Z), the generator and all control kernels are the same in every model. A controller can be simulated with common fresh randomness conditional on these preselected variables. Probes, random clocks, postselection and all their visible records are therefore a common stochastic channel from one oracle sample (H,Z) per reset. They cannot distinguish better than those oracle samples. If the algorithm adapts across resets or stops early, simulate r independent oracle samples in advance and reveal only the requested ones; its complete transcript is still a common channel of them.

For each oracle sample write I for the pair ID and W=(-1)^b Z. Under the null, the I's are independent uniform on {1,...,M}, and the W's are independent fair signs. Under alternative xi,

    P(W=w | I=i,xi)=(1+alpha w xi_i)/2.

The b bits are independent fair ancillary variables in both cases. Now average over a single xi vector chosen at the start and kept fixed across all resets. Conditional on the event that all sampled I's are distinct, each W uses a fresh independent unknown xi_i; after averaging it is fair and independent of all other W's. Hence the two complete oracle data laws agree exactly on that event. The I law and collision probability are common. A union bound over reset pairs gives (8). Data processing proves the active-transcript assertion. This proof is finite and does not use any asymptotic conditional-independence theorem. QED.

For a tester having both null and worst-alternative error at most 1/3, the sum-of-errors bound requires transcript TV>=1/3. Thus

    r(r-1) >= 2M/3,
    r = Omega(sqrt(M)) = Omega(2^(m/2)).               (9)

This applies, in particular, to either of these promised outputs:

* an additive estimator of D_cond with error at most 1/(1024T) and success probability at least 2/3;
* a sound upper certificate C with probability at least 2/3 under every model, which also returns C<=1/(512T) with probability at least 2/3 under the perfect null predictor.

Threshold C or the estimate at 1/(512T). Equation (6) separates the two cases. An upper bound that always returns a large value is not excluded: perfect-predictor completeness is part of the promise being tested. Expected-reset budgets satisfy the same order lower bound when uniformly bounded under the null and each alternative, by truncation at a constant multiple of the budget and the same sum-of-errors argument.

Here T=O(m), inverse requested accuracy is O(m), k=6, there is one target action, primitive count 12, product-rate bound 4, and synthesis budget 2/3. Consequently no budget polynomial jointly in these interface parameters, independent of hidden or history count, can universally acquire the less conservative quantity while recognizing all perfect predictors.

This is not a coverage failure between training and deployment. Acquisition and evaluation use exactly the same repeatable preparation and the same target process. Every context is equally likely, every sign conditional on context has positive constant mass, the visible initial label is fixed, and the hard association is present in the actual occupation histories being tested. The missing resource is repeated conditional information about one context among exponentially many contexts. A physical realization of arbitrary xi, its preparation cost and its exponential description are not asserted cheap. The lower bound supplies even the generator and latent sign readout for free, making this initial-association barrier explicit.

The statement uses a finite reducible episodic model and an unknown preparation. It is not a lower bound for an additional promise of a known preparation, irreducibility with a uniform mixing bound, or a supplied small conditional predictive representation.

## 5. Why a bounded single-copy observable cannot remove the variance

A tempting attack is to search for another positive product-flux identity whose expectation is D_cond. That would have to overcome a basic affinity obstruction. The average of the preparations in (2), over independent uniform xi, is exactly P_0's hidden preparation. Every fixed one-reset active experiment is a common stochastic channel of that preparation, so the expectation of any integrable one-reset statistic is affine in it. But D_cond is zero at P_0 and strictly positive at every P_xi. Therefore no one-reset statistic, bounded or otherwise integrable throughout this finite family, can be an unbiased estimator of D_cond. This includes arbitrary one-episode adaptive products and random stopping; it does not merely exclude the existing Gram identity.

With r resets, if a fixed statistic Z_r satisfies |Z_r|<=B and is unbiased for D_cond on this family, (6) and (8) force

    1/(256T) <= |E_mix Z_r-E_0 Z_r|
              <= 2B TV(mix,0)
              <= B r(r-1)/M.

Thus, for r>=2,

    B >= M/[256 T r(r-1)].                            (10)

A polynomial number of resets cannot hide the obstruction in an unbiased bounded-magnitude readout; its required range is exponential. Unbiasedness is stronger than the certificate promise, while (9) already covers arbitrary biased estimators and adaptive decision procedures with finite success guarantees.

The failed linear-identity attack changes the mathematical task: the unknown object is a conditional mean square, not a same-state rate square. Products multiply edge gains inside one hidden draw. They do not turn that draw into two independent draws from its visible-history posterior.

## 6. The birthday bound is a matched resource cost

The sqrt(M) scale is not only a loose lower-bound artifact in the stronger oracle. It is attained, up to constants for fixed alpha, by ordinary episodes of this family.

Run an episode until A, discarding it if tau>T-1. Acceptance has probability at least 2/3 and is independent of H and Z. On acceptance record the context and B_1, the indicator that A is left during its first unit. Put

    f_z = 1-exp(-(c+delta z)),
    a_0=(f_+ + f_-)/2,
    d_0=(f_+ - f_-)/2>0,
    V=(-1)^b (B_1-a_0)/d_0.

Then |V|<=K for an absolute constant K and

    E[V | I=i,xi]=alpha xi_i,
    E_0[V | I=i]=0.

For q independent accepted episodes form the observable collision statistic

    S = sum_{j<l} 1{I_j=I_l} V_j V_l.

Under the null E S=0. Under every fixed alternative,

    E S = A_q alpha^2/M,    A_q=q(q-1)/2.

Disjoint-pair covariances vanish. A pair summand has second moment at most K^4/M. For two distinct summands sharing one index, the alternative covariance is

    alpha^2 [E(V^2)-alpha^2]/M^2 <= alpha^2 K^2/M^2;

the null covariance is zero. There are 3 binom(q,3) unordered pairs of summands sharing one index. Consequently

    Var_0(S) <= A_q K^4/M,
    Var_xi(S) <= A_q K^4/M + 6 binom(q,3) alpha^2 K^2/M^2. (11)

Threshold S at half its alternative mean. Chebyshev's inequality and (11) give errors at most 1/3 for q=C sqrt(M), with a sufficiently large absolute C, uniformly as M grows; enlarging C covers the finite small M cases. Since alpha, K are constants and accepted episodes have constant probability, O(sqrt(M)) expected resets suffice. A fixed reset cap with the same order and a larger constant gives the same success guarantee. Each episode costs at most T of observation. No active probe is needed for this upper comparison.

Thus the hard family exposes a genuine matched birthday cost, not an unidentifiable functional or a claim that every conditional problem requires exponential samples. There are finitely many context probabilities; enough repeated contexts identify them.
