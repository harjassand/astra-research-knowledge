# Prior-art comparison for the separated first potential theorem

Date: 16 October 2026. Internal scope audit; historical originality UNKNOWN.

Read target: ../ga_sharp_invention/REPORT.md, complete report as present during this audit. The new result has first potential \(f=F(x)+H(y)\), arbitrary periodic second potential \(g(x,y)\), the exact unchanged \(r=1\) quartic \(J\), every scalar conformal background, and an explicit positive quartic margin.

**Adjudication.** The qualitative one-profile cases already follow from a stronger classical theorem about triangular gradient measures, for every rank-one convex integrand. The full two-profile class is not admitted by that theorem, by Müller's diagonal-support theorem, or by ordinary iterated rank-one Jensen on coordinate slices. A broader classical implication for this entire class has not been established or excluded here. The exact nonlinear stress and numerical margin should remain labelled new to this investigation, with historical priority UNKNOWN.

## 1. The strongest exact primary comparator inspected

[Harris–Kirchheim–Lin, Two-by-two upper triangular matrices and Morrey's conjecture, arXiv:1708.07849v2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.07849v2), dated 26 April 2019 in the acquired PDF, was read directly. Theorem 2.4 and its complete three-part proof on pages 3–5 establish that every compactly supported homogeneous gradient Young measure on the upper triangular \(2\times2\) matrices is a laminate. The diagonal appendix and its Haar/Jensen proof were also read. The proof projects to the diagonal case, uses fixed transformations preserving gradient measures, lifts finite rank-one splittings, and passes to general compact measures by disintegration and approximation. Its nonlinear extension assumes \(\det X=0\) and \(X_{12}>0\).

## 2. What is immediately inherited

If \(H\) is constant, then \(b=f_y=0\), so the perturbation gradients lie in the lower triangular subspace. Fixed row and column swaps turn it into the upper triangular subspace. If \(F\) is constant, then \(a=f_x=0\); after a row swap the perturbation lies in an upper triangular subspace. Affine translation of the integrand handles any background.

For a smooth periodic map, periodic repetition at smaller scales gives a compact homogeneous gradient Young measure with the same gradient distribution. The triangular theorem therefore supplies the qualitative Jensen inequality for any rank-one convex integrand, including the unchanged \(J\), in either one-profile case with arbitrary \(g\). Smooth approximation extends the quartic conclusion to the corresponding \(W^{1,4}\) class.

This inherited theorem is broader in its integrand and background quantifiers than the new one-profile restrictions. It does not by its statement supply the particular quantitative \(1/256\) quartic margin or a theorem for the union of both profile directions.

## 3. Why the two-profile case does not meet that support hypothesis

Take the admissible smooth periodic map
\[
f(x,y)=\sin x+\sin y,\qquad g(x,y)=\sin x\sin y.
\]
Then
\[
(a,b,c,d)=(\cos x,\cos y,\cos x\sin y,\sin x\cos y).
\]
Each coordinate has mean zero, and their covariance matrix is exactly
\[
\operatorname{diag}(1/2,1/2,1/4,1/4).
\]
It is positive definite. If the gradient support lay in an affine hyperplane, some nonzero linear functional of these mean-zero entries would vanish identically, contradicting positive covariance. Thus the support affinely spans the full four-dimensional matrix space.

An invertible fixed linear map preserves affine dimension. Consequently no fixed left/right multiplication, rotation, or affine matrix change can place every admitted gradient of this example into a three-dimensional triangular support subspace. Translation by \(sI\) does not alter this obstruction. The example also has nonzero determinants and sign-changing \(b=\cos y\), so the source's determinant-zero, positive-entry nonlinear extension is not applicable.

Full affine span does **not** imply that the measure is nonlaminate. It establishes the failure of the cited support hypothesis only. This audit does not claim a counterexample to a general laminate assertion.

## 4. The direct slice-Jensen shortcut has a separate failure

For the same example, at \(y=0\),
\[
D\phi(0,0)=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
D\phi(\pi/2,0)=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
Their difference is \(\operatorname{diag}(1,-1)\), of rank two. Thus varying \(x\) at fixed \(y\) does not stay on a rank-one line even though \(f_x\) depends only on \(x\) and \(f_y\) only on \(y\). The unrestricted \(g_y\) changes simultaneously. Rank-one convexity alone does not justify applying an ordinary slice Jensen inequality to that variation. The analogous problem occurs for the other slice direction.

A more elaborate laminate decomposition or a valid rank-one-convex extension could still exist. Neither follows just by saying that the first potential separates. The nonlinear divergence-free stress and positive residual in the new report are an actual proof for this particular integrand, rather than an established application of the cited diagonal/triangular theorem.
