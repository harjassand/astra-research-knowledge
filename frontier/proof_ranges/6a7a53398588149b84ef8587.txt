Local thermal information cannot be erased by an arbitrarily large boundedly coupled bath
The strongest result from this investigation is a local thermal-faithfulness theorem, together with a finite-energy-band strengthening and an explicit counterexample delimiting its scope.

The central conclusion is:

At fixed temperature, subsystem dimension, and coupling strength, increasing the bath’s size or internal energy scale cannot make every thermal fluctuation of a nonzero local operator arbitrarily small.

The finite-dimensional proof is supplied below. Original priority and independently major foundational significance remain unestablished. In particular, this does not yet settle the stronger global many-body inverse problem.

1. Selecting the obstruction
I compared three directions before committing to the thermal-information problem.

Direction	Decisive target and possible mechanism	Assessment
Bosonic propagation	Obtain useful causal bounds under weaker, physically accessible occupation assumptions, using occupation-weighted propagation estimates.	Near-linear bosonic light-cone results already exist under occupation hypotheses. I did not obtain a demonstrated improvement over those results. 
Nonequilibrium relaxation	Derive observable relaxation guarantees from verifiable local structure, rather than relying only on a dissipative spectral gap; a suitably controlled Lyapunov metric was the candidate mechanism.	Gap–relaxation mismatch is already established in nonnormal Lindbladian examples. Merely constructing a different metric would not suffice without controlling its conditioning and acquisition. 
Thermal inverse problems	Prevent local information from becoming uncontrollably ill-conditioned, with explicit dependence on temperature and interaction strength. The mechanism developed here retains spectral multiplicities under bounded perturbations.	This produced a complete local theorem and a stronger finite-band statement. The global extension remains unresolved in this investigation.
The starting point was not the claim that finite-temperature Hamiltonian learning is generally unavailable. Polynomial-time algorithms at fixed, arbitrary temperature already exist, and recent work gives strong local-learning guarantees. The opportunity was a sharper structural inequality, not rediscovering basic learnability.
