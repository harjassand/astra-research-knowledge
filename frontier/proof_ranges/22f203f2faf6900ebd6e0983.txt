## 8. The nonlinear scientific obstruction remains

Even perfect knowledge of the linearized impedance does not determine unrestricted finite-amplitude response. A stronger elementary counterexample also applies to bounded-amplitude EIS experiments.

Suppose all characterization currents satisfy |I|<=a. Add the memoryless voltage contribution

\[
V_{\mathrm{extra}}(I)=c\,\operatorname{sign}(I)(|I|-a)_+^3,
\qquad c\geq0.
\]

It is zero for every such characterization input. It is monotone, and I V_extra(I)>=0, so it is passive. Nevertheless, at I_p>a it adds c(I_p-a)^3 to the voltage. Identical small-amplitude data and passivity alone therefore cannot give a uniform high-amplitude voltage guarantee over this broader class.

This is an information-theoretic counterexample, not a claim that the tested cell literally contains that element, and not a historical-novelty claim. Additional material restrictions or nonlinear measurements may rule it out. Those restrictions and measurements must be supplied and charged.

Unknown initial relaxation, different rest histories, state-of-charge motion, temperature variation, frequency calibration, and nonlinear response are separate from floating-point error. Neither an exact dual certificate nor an attractive fitted curve bounds those physical effects automatically. The published pseudo-open-circuit and pulse information may resolve part of the ambiguity; established competitors are entitled to use it under a matched acquisition contract.
