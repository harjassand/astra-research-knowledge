Abstract
    

We present a general protocol for estimating  M  observables from only  \mathcal{O}(\log(M)/\varepsilon^{2})  copies of a Gibbs state whose Hamiltonian is accessible. The protocol uses single-copy, nonadaptive measurements and uses a total Hamiltonian simulation time of  \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(\beta M/\varepsilon^{2}) ; we show that the sample complexity is optimal in a black-box setting where exponential time Hamiltonian simulation is prohibited. The key idea is a new interpretation of quantum Gibbs samplers as detailed-balance measurement channels: measurements that preserve the Gibbs state when outcomes are marginalized. Consequently, shadow tomography of thermal states admits a general efficient algorithm when the Hamiltonian is known, substantially lowering the readout cost in quantum thermal simulation.

1 Introduction




Simulation of quantum many-body systems is widely expected to be a major application of future quantum computers. Consequently, preparing quantum thermal and ground states—central tasks in this vision—has motivated a broad array of sophisticated quantum algorithm proposals [TOV+11, SM21, CKBG25, DLL24, JI24, DCL24, RWW23] as well as experimental demonstrations [MMS+24].




Nevertheless, many end-to-end application in chemistry and materials science ultimately requires estimating a large collection of observables. Because quantum measurements are inherently destructive, this task leads to a rich set of fundamental problems in quantum state tomography [HHJ+16, OW16, KOS07, HWM+22, BMBO20, ZRM21, WHLB23, KGKB25, PSTW25]. Despite important progress on some aspects of this problem [Aar18, HKP20], the best general-purpose efficient protocol for estimating many observables is simply to measure, discard, and repeat the already expensive state-preparation procedure. This multiplicative overhead from tomography threatens to narrow the regime of quantum advantage in quantum simulation, especially considering that the competing classical approaches (e.g., tensor network [Sch11, Orú19, VNS+23]) often provide an explicit description of the state where extracting observables has merely additive costs.




In this work, we introduce a general measurement protocol for estimating many thermal observables that is:


• 



Sample efficient. The number of thermal samples scales quadratically with the precision guarantee and logarithmically with the number of observables.


• 



Computationally efficient. The protocol uses single-copy measurements. The total (controlled) Hamiltonian evolution time scales linearly with the inverse temperature and the number of observables, and quadratically with the precision.






Generally, shadow tomography has shown that many observables can be estimated information-theoretically from a small number of copies of an unknown quantum state. However, it relies on entangled operations across many copies and appears computationally inefficient. Classical shadow methods can provide computationally efficient protocols, but only for restricted classes of observables, such as low-rank or few-body operators [KGKB25]. By contrast, our results are both sample- and computationally efficient and apply to arbitrary observables, including Green’s functions and high-weight operators that commonly arise in quantum simulation.




The key idea behind our protocol is to repurpose recent quantum Gibbs sampler constructions as




measurement channels that preserve the Gibbs state when outcomes are ignored.



Operationally, the measurement protocol can be viewed as running a carefully designed Gibbs sampling algorithm, where the Kraus operators are tailored to the desired observables. Conceptually, the mechanism for estimating multiple observables resembles drawing statistical inferences from trajectories of classical Markov chains. Our results show that the destructive nature of quantum measurements can be effectively circumvented by exploiting quantum detailed balance, and the algorithmic use of quantum trajectories can be surprisingly powerful. Even though our work focuses entirely on tomography and keeps the thermal state preparation as a black box, we expect the interplay between the algorithmic and tomographic nature of quantum Gibbs samplers to be a fertile ground [JLL26].




More broadly, our protocol suggests that many black-box tomography problems admit fundamentally different solutions when additional structure of the initial state—such as knowledge of the Hamiltonian—is available [CK25]. In such cases, this structure can translate into orders-of-magnitude reductions in state-preparation costs in practical quantum simulation workflows.




1.1 Prior work




Due to the destructive nature of quantum measurements, the tomography of general unknown quantum states has become a rich and rapidly evolving field. A major breakthrough was the proposal of shadow tomography [Aar18], which showed that  M -bounded observables can be estimated using only polylogarithmically many copies of an unknown quantum state. However, the original formulation focuses primarily on information-theoretic learnability. Implementing the protocol appears computationally challenging, as it relies on entangled measurements across many copies and classical post-processing steps such as matrix multiplicative weight updates of exponentially large matrices. It remains unclear whether these procedures admit efficient classical or quantum algorithms.




More recently, the widely celebrated framework of classical shadows [HKP20] demonstrated that the central promise of shadow tomography can be achieved efficiently for certain classes of observables, particularly low-rank or low-weight operators. An appealing feature of the protocol is that the observables need only be specified after the measurements are performed. However, the sample complexity depends on a quantity known as the shadow norm, which can grow prohibitively large for high-rank or high-weight observables. Today, there has been a rapidly expanding family of shadow-based tomography protocols that interpolate between different trade-offs in copy complexity and observable classes [CLL24]. Nevertheless, it remains open whether one can achieve sample complexity scaling logarithmically in the number of observables for completely general observables.




A complementary line of work seeks polynomial improvements over the naive  M/\epsilon^{2}  scaling using advanced quantum algorithmic techniques [HWM+22, vACGN23, Sin25]. While these approaches provide meaningful speedups in certain regimes, it remains unclear whether improvements beyond quadratic factors are achievable within this framework.




Our protocol offers a different conceptual route for reducing tomography costs: implicit knowledge about the initial state. In many quantum simulation settings, the Hamiltonian is known, even though preparing the corresponding Gibbs state may be computationally hard. We show that this structure can be exploited to effectively reuse Gibbs samples by designing measurement protocols that satisfy quantum detailed balance. Concretely, we construct a Gibbs sampler whose Kraus operators are associated with the desired observables, allowing measurements to be performed while preserving the Gibbs state when outcomes are ignored. The key distinction from the general shadow tomography problem is that the thermal setting is fundamentally computational: information-theoretically, the Gibbs state is already specified by the Hamiltonian. Remarkably, access to the Hamiltonian alone suffices to achieve shadow-tomography-like sample complexity while avoiding the destructive overhead of repeated state preparation.





1.2 Problem setup




Given an  n -qubit Hamiltonian  \bm{H}  and inverse temperature  \beta , consider the Gibbs state




 \displaystyle\bm{\rho}_{\beta}=\frac{e^{-\beta\bm{H}}}{\mathrm{Tr}[e^{-\beta\bm{H}}]}. 






We are interested in bounded Hermitian observables




 \displaystyle\bm{A}_{1},\ldots,\bm{A}_{M}\quad\text{such that}\quad\bm{A}_{i}=\bm{A}_{i}^{\dagger},\quad\|{\bm{A}_{i}}\|\leq 1\quad\text{for each}\quad 1\leq i\leq M. 






Our goal is to estimate all thermal expectations




 \displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{\rho}_{\beta}\bm{A}_{i}]\in[-1,1]\quad\text{to precision}\quad\epsilon, 



with failure probability  \delta  on a (fault-tolerant) quantum computer.




We make the following assumptions:


• 



(Gibbs samples.) We assume black-box access to independent copies of the Gibbs state  \bm{\rho}_{\beta} .


• 



(Hamiltonian simulation.) Our protocol requires black-box access to controlled Hamiltonian time evolutions  e^{\pm i\bm{H}t} .


• 



(Access to observables.) We assume block-encoding access to each observable  \bm{A}_{i} . This assumption is automatically satisfied when  \bm{A}_{i}  is implemented by a self-adjoint unitary circuit, such as a Pauli string.



In order to state our results in a clean form we use the notation  \widetilde{\mathcal{O}}(T)=\mathcal{O}\!\left(T\mathrm{Poly}\!\log(T+M+\beta+\left\|\bm{H}\right\|+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}+\frac{1}{\delta})\right) .

1.4 Proof ideas




Recently, a new wave of quantum algorithms has emerged for preparing thermal states using detailed-balance Lindbladians or quantum channels [TOV+11, SM21, CKBG25, DLL24, JI24, DCL24, RWW23]. Operationally, these algorithms can be viewed as implementing carefully controlled system–ancilla interactions. When the ancilla is traced out, the resulting effective dynamics satisfy an exact structural property—quantum detailed balance [CKG23]—that guarantees the Gibbs state as a fixed point.




The key idea of our protocol is a conceptual extension of these quantum Gibbs-sampling algorithms. Rather than discarding the ancilla, we observe that the ancilla—recording which transition or “jump” has occurred—already functions as a metrological device. This perspective leads us to introduce the central primitive of our algorithm: detailed-balance measurement channels.




Definition 1 (Detailed-balanced measurement channel).



Given a Hermitian observable  \bm{A}  with  \|{\bm{A}}\|\leq 1  and a full-rank quantum state  \bm{\rho} , we say a collection of Kraus operators  {\bm{K}_{i}}  with  \sum_{i}\bm{K}_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{K}_{i}=\bm{I}  forms a  \bm{\rho} -detailed-balance measurement channel for  \bm{A}  if:


• 



(detailed-balanced on average) When the measurement outcomes are marginalized, the resulting channel  \mathcal{N}=\sum_{i}\bm{K}_{i}[\cdot]\bm{K}_{i}^{\dagger}  satisfies detailed balance with respect to  \bm{\rho} .


• 



(informative outcomes) A known linear combination of the outcome probabilities  \langle\bm{K}_{i}^{\dagger}\bm{K}_{i}\rangle_{\bm{\rho}}  provides an unbiased estimator of  \langle\bm{A}\rangle_{\bm{\rho}} .







It will be useful to distinguish between the averaged channel and the full measurement apparatus (including the appended ancilla):




 \displaystyle\mathcal{E}[\bm{\sigma}]:=\sum_{i}\bm{K}_{i}\bm{\sigma}\bm{K}_{i}^{\dagger}\otimes|i\rangle\!\langle i|,\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\mathrm{Tr}_{anc}[\mathcal{E}]=\mathcal{N}. 






Using the above detailed-balanced measurement channel as a black-box primitive, we can sketch the main estimation algorithm (Figure 1). Consider performing the following sequential measurement procedure  r  times on  r  independent copies of  \bm{\rho} :





 \displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(1)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{E}^{(1)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{N}^{(1)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{N}^{(1)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]=\bm{\rho}, 




 \displaystyle\cdots 




 \displaystyle\mathcal{E}^{(r)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{E}^{(r)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]\quad\text{such that}\quad\mathcal{N}^{(r)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{N}^{(r)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]=\bm{\rho}. 






For each run, we record the outcome of every  \mathcal{E}_{i}^{(j)} .
To estimate the observable  \bm{A}_{m} , we simply marginalize over all outcomes except those in the  m -th column. Due to the detailed-balance property, the distribution of these outcomes is identical to the distribution obtained by directly measuring on the Gibbs state:





 \displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{(1)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{E}^{(1)}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{N}^{(1)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]=\mathcal{E}^{(1)}_{m}[\bm{\rho}], 




 \displaystyle\cdots 




 \displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{(r)}_{M}\cdots\mathcal{E}^{(r)}_{m}\cdots\mathcal{N}^{(r)}_{1}[\bm{\rho}]=\mathcal{E}^{(r)}_{m}[\bm{\rho}].

2 Techniques




The main algorithmic primitive is to instantiate the detailed-balance measurement channel via a quantum Gibbs sampler. Conceptually, it may be cleaner to think in discrete time, but we also introduce the continuous-time version for completeness. Once such primitives are instantiated using off-the-shelve Gibbs sampling framework (with some specification), the proof of the main results (1) follows naturally.




2.1 Detailed-balance measurement channels




A natural instantiation of a detailed-balance measurement channel is a detailed balanced Gibbs sampler with carefully designed Kraus operators.




Lemma 1 (Efficient detailed-balance measurement channels from Gibbs sampling).



For any self-adjoint observable  \bm{A},  and Hamiltonian  \bm{H},\beta , there is a set of Detailed-balanced measurement operators  \{\bm{K}_{i}\}  and a  c=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\log^{2}(\beta\left\|\bm{H}\right\|)}\right)  such that  \left\|\bm{K}_{1}^{\dagger}\bm{K}_{1}\right\| ,  \left\|\bm{K}_{2}^{\dagger}\bm{K}_{2}\right\|\leq c  and




 \displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{K}^{\dagger}_{1}\bm{K}_{1}\bm{\rho}]-\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{K}^{\dagger}_{2}\bm{K}_{2}\bm{\rho}]=\frac{c}{2}\mathrm{Tr}[\bm{A}\bm{\rho}]. 



Furthermore, this channel can be implemented to diamond norm error  \varepsilon  using  \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\beta)  Hamiltonian simulation time and  \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)  uses of an  a -qubit block-encoding for the observable  \bm{A}  with the help of  \mathcal{O}(a+1)  many ancillas.





For measuring one specific observable, this bears no benefit than directly measuring the  \bm{A} . However, having the averaged channel detailed-balanced will become crucial for estimating many observables.

2.4 Tight sample bounds




Theorem 2.



Let  \varepsilon,\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{10}) ,  M\geq 1 . Then, we can choose  n=\Omega\big(\log(\frac{\log(M)}{\varepsilon\delta})\big)  such that there is a family of classical Hamiltonians  H\in\{0,1\}^{2^{n}\times 2^{n}}  and an inverse temperature  \beta=\mathcal{O}\left(n\right) 
for which estimating  M  bounded observables  |O_{i}|\leq 1  each to precision at least  \varepsilon  with success probability at least  1-\delta  requires  \Omega\big(\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\big)  Gibbs samples even if we are allowed to make  2^{\frac{n}{3}}  (controlled) quantum queries to the phase oracle  \left|j\right\rangle\rightarrow(-1)^{H_{\!jj}}\left|j\right\rangle .





Before proving the theorem let us mention that the above is a tight bound as according to the Chernoff-Hoeffding bound (1) we can estimate each individual observable to precision  \varepsilon  with success probability at least  1-\frac{\delta}{M}  using  \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\log(M/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)  Gibbs samples. By the union bound, we get the desired result.




Our proof’s core is a reduction to the known sample complexity lower bound  \Omega\left(\frac{m+\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\right)  for learning a distribution on  m  elements to total variation distance  \varepsilon  with success probability at least  1-\delta  [Can22, Theorem 1.3],[Dia19, Lecture 4]. We apply this lower bound to a distribution on  m  elements induced on  m  bins of the configuration space by a specific zero-temperature Gibbs state corresponding to a classical  \{0,1\} -valued Hamiltonian, that is simply a uniform distribution over about  2^{\frac{n}{3}}  zero-energy states, while all excited states have energy  1 . Because the spectrum is binary, after seeing a sample, querying that element does not reveal any new information. Also because the zero energy states are exponentially rare (have frequency about  \sim 2^{\frac{-2n}{3}} ) it requires an exponential amount of queries to obtain any previously unseen sample. This tells us that the query access is essentially useless, unless an exponential number of queries are used.




We use the following lemma to show that the zero-temperature state of our classical binary Hamiltonian on  n -bits is close to a reasonable temperature Gibbs state, so that the simplification of working with a zero-temperature Gibbs state is not too restrictive:




Lemma 4.

 \|\rho_{\beta}-\rho_{\infty}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\leq e^{-r}.

Let  0\leq\!X\!\leq 1  be a bounded random variable and  p:=\mathbb{E}[X] . Suppose we take  k  i.i.d. samples  X_{i}  of  X  and denote the averaged outcome by  S_{k}=\frac{X_{1}+X_{2}+\cdots+X_{k}}{k} . Then we have for all  \varepsilon>0 




 \displaystyle\Pr[S_{k}\geq p+\varepsilon] 
 \displaystyle\leq e^{-D_{KL}(p+\varepsilon\parallel p)k}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2(p+\varepsilon)}k\right), 

(3)


 \displaystyle\Pr[S_{k}\leq p-\varepsilon] 
 \displaystyle\leq e^{-D_{KL}(p-\varepsilon\parallel p)k}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2p}k\right), 

(4)


where  D_{KL}(x\parallel p)=x\ln\frac{x}{p}+(1-x)\ln\left(\frac{1-x}{1-p}\right)  is the Kullback–Leibler divergence between Bernoulli random variables with mean  x  and  p  respectively.

 \displaystyle S_{k}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}X_{i}\leq\frac{2}{ck}\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}Z_{i}. 

(5)





The main idea is that we construct a coupling between the binary random variables  Z_{i}=\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{i}>0\}}  and  k  independent binary Bernoulli random variables  B_{i}  such that  \mathbb{E}[B_{i}]=c . We define the random variable  Z^{\prime}_{i}  recursively as follows. Let  Z^{\prime}_{0}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{0}\leq\Pr[Z_{0}]=1\}}  and let




 Z^{\prime}_{i}:=\mathbf{1}_{\{U_{i}\leq\Pr[Z_{i}=1|(Z_{0},Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{i-1})=b]\}}\text{ if }(Z^{\prime}_{0},Z^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{i-1})=b\in\{0,1\}^{i}.

 \displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\Pr[Z^{\prime}_{\ell}=b_{\ell}|(Z^{\prime}_{0},\ldots,Z^{\prime}_{\ell-1})=(b_{0},\ldots,b_{\ell-1})] 




 \displaystyle=\prod_{\ell=0}^{k-1}\Pr[Z_{\ell}=b_{\ell}|(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{\ell-1})=(b_{0},\ldots,b_{\ell-1})]=\Pr[(Z_{0},\ldots,Z_{k-1})=b]. 



On the other hand, the coupling is constructed in such a way that  Z^{\prime}_{i}\leq B_{i} . Therefore, we have that




 \displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}Z^{\prime}_{i}\leq\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}B_{i}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(k,c). 

(6)





By symmetry, Equation 5 and the above coupling and Equation 6 we have

Appendix B Learning distributions given by uniform sampling over a subset




Lemma 7.



Let  f\colon\Delta_{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}  be  L -Lipschitz with respect to the metric  D  and total variation distance:




 D(f(p),f(q))\leq L\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\qquad\text{for all }p,q\in\Delta_{m}. 



Assume that any algorithm which, given i.i.d. samples from an unknown distribution  p\in\Delta_{m} , outputs a value  v  such that




 D(f(p),v)\leq 2\varepsilon 



with probability at least  1-3\delta , must use at least  S+1  samples.




Let




 K\;\geq\;\max\left\{\frac{Lm}{2\varepsilon},\frac{Sm}{2\delta},\frac{S^{2}}{2\delta}\right\},\qquad\text{and}\qquad n\geq\log_{2}(mK). 



Given an unknown distribution  p^{\prime}\in\Delta_{m}  on  m  elements, partition  \{0,1\}^{n}  into  m  bins  B_{1},\dots,B_{m}  of sizes differing by at most  1 .
Suppose that there is a uniformly random (unkown) subset  I\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}  with  |I|=K , so that
 p^{\prime}_{i}\;=\;\frac{|I\cap B_{i}|}{|I|} .
Then any algorithm that outputs  v  such that




 D(f(p^{\prime}),v)\leq\varepsilon 



with probability at least  1-\delta , must use at least  S+1  i.i.d. uniform random samples from elements of  I .





Proof.



We first reduce arbitrary distributions to distributions whose probabilities are multiples of  1/K . Consider the “rounding function”  q=r(p)  that carefully rounds the probabilities to multiples of  1/K  while keeping their sum equal to  1 , where  q_{i}  is defined recursively as




 q_{1}=\frac{\lfloor p_{1}K\rfloor}{K},\qquad q_{1}+q_{2}=\frac{\lfloor(p_{1}+p_{2})\,K\rfloor}{K},\qquad\ldots 






It is easy to see that  |q_{i}-p_{i}|\leq 1/K , and therefore




 d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,r(p))\leq\frac{m}{2K}\leq\min\!\left\{\frac{\delta}{S},\frac{\varepsilon}{L}\right\}. 






Let




 \mathcal{F}_{K}:=\bigl\{q\in\Delta_{m}:q_{i}\in\tfrac{1}{K}\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }i\bigr\}. 



We claim that even on the restricted family  \mathcal{F}_{K} , estimating  f  to accuracy  \varepsilon  with confidence  1-2\delta  requires at least  S+1  samples.




Indeed, suppose for contradiction that there exists an algorithm  \mathcal{A}  using at most  S  samples such that for every  q\in\mathcal{F}_{K} ,




 \Pr\left[D(\mathcal{A}(X_{1},\dots,X_{S}),f(q))\leq\varepsilon\right]\geq 1-2\delta, 



where  X_{1},\dots,X_{S}  are i.i.d. from  q .




Now let  p\in\Delta_{m}  be arbitrary, and set  q:=r(p) . Run  \mathcal{A}  on  S  i.i.d. samples from  p . By coupling the two one-sample distributions and taking a union bound over the  S  samples,




 d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(p^{\times S},q^{\times S}\right)\leq S\,d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\leq\delta. 



Hence the success probability of  \mathcal{A}  for estimating  f(q)  with samples from  p  is at least




 (1-2\delta)-\delta=1-3\delta. 



Furthermore, whenever  \mathcal{A} ’s output  v  satisfies  D(v,f(q))\leq\varepsilon , by the triangle inequality we have




 D(v,f(p))\leq D(v,f(q))+D(f(q),f(p))\leq\varepsilon+L\cdot d_{\mathrm{TV}}(p,q)\leq 2\varepsilon. 



So we would obtain an  S -sample algorithm that estimates  f(p)  to accuracy  2\varepsilon  with confidence  1-3\delta , contradicting the assumed lower bound. This proves the claim about  \mathcal{F}_{K} .




We now pass to the subset-sampling model. Fix  q\in\mathcal{F}_{K} . Since each  Kq_{i}  is an integer, we can realize  q  by a subset  I\subseteq\{0,1\}^{n}  of size  K  satisfying




 |I\cap B_{i}|=Kq_{i}\qquad\text{for all }i\in[m]. 



Assume, toward contradiction, that there is an algorithm  \mathcal{A}  which uses at most  S  samples from uniformly random elements of  I , and outputs an  \varepsilon -accurate estimate of  f(q)  with probability at least  1-\delta  when  \mathcal{A}  is run for a uniformly random such  I .




We compare two transcript distributions of length  S .




(1) True subset-sampling transcript.
We sample  Y_{1},\dots,Y_{S}  i.i.d. uniformly from the  K  elements of  I .




(2) Simulated transcript from sample access to  q .
We sample  J_{1},\dots,J_{S}  i.i.d. from  q . Each time  J_{t}=i , we output a fresh uniformly random string from bin  B_{i} , never reusing a previously output string from that bin.




The second procedure only needs sample access to  q . Moreover, we can couple the two transcripts such that they only differ if the subset-sampling returns some element of  I  twice. Thus




 d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\left(\mathcal{L}(Y_{1},\dots,Y_{S}),\mathcal{L}(\text{simulated transcript})\right)\leq\Pr[\exists\,s<t:\ Y_{s}=Y_{t}]. 



By the union bound,




 \Pr[\exists\,s<t:\ Y_{s}=Y_{t}]\leq\binom{S}{2}\frac{1}{K}\leq\frac{S^{2}}{2K}\leq\delta. 



Therefore, the simulated procedure also succeeds with probability at least




 (1-\delta)-\delta=1-2\delta. 






But this yields an algorithm using at most  S  ordinary samples from  q\in\mathcal{F}_{K}  that estimates  f(q)  to accuracy  \varepsilon  with confidence at least  1-2\delta , contradicting the lower bound on  \mathcal{F}_{K}  proved above.




Hence, no algorithm using  S  uniform subset samples can estimate  f(p^{\prime})  to accuracy  \varepsilon  with confidence  1-\delta .
∎

Now we turn to proving our general lower bound result based on the hybrid method, which also considers the low probability regime. This is a standard technique, but we could not find a readily applicable low-probability variant in the literature, so for completeness, we present a proof here. This technique was originally introduced for proving a lower bound for quantum search by Bennett et al. [BBBV97], and can be viewed as a simplified version of the adversary method [Amb00, HŠ05, LMR+11, Bel15].
Our proof closely follows the presentation of the hybrid method in Montanaro’s lecture notes [Mon11, Section 1], and that of [GAW19].

Proof.



According to the church of the larger Hilbert space, more precisely due to the triangle and data processing inequalities, we can assume without loss of generality that the algorithm’s starting state is pure  \left|\bm{0}\right\rangle  and the algorithm is unitary (i.e., does not involve measurements).




Suppose that  \mathcal{F}=\{f_{j}\colon j\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\} . Let  \mathcal{A}_{j}  denote the algorithm which uses phase oracle  \mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}  and let  \left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle:=\mathcal{A}_{j}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle  denote the final state of the algorithm. Since  \frac{1}{2}\left\||\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|-|\psi_{0}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{0}|\right\|_{1}\leq\left\|\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\right\| , due to the triangle inequality it suffices to bound




 \displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left\|\bm{\rho}_{\mathcal{F}}-\bm{\rho}_{f_{0}}\right\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbb{E}[|\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|]-|\psi_{0}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{0}|\right\|_{1}\leq\mathbb{E}[\frac{1}{2}\left\||\psi_{j}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{j}|-|\psi_{0}\rangle\!\langle\psi_{0}|\right\|_{1}]\leq\mathbb{E}[\left\|\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\right\|]. 

(8)





We can assume without loss of generality that  \mathrm{O}_{f}  is a controlled oracle, i.e., there is a known element  b_{0}\in B  such that  f(b_{0})=0  for all  j\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\} .
In general the quantum algorithm might use some workspace  \mathcal{W}=\underset{w\in W}{\mathrm{Span}}\{\left|w\right\rangle:w\in W\}  along with the Hilbert space  \mathcal{H} . With a slight abuse of notation, from now on we account for the presence of this additional Hilbert space by replacing  \mathrm{O}_{f}  by  \mathrm{O}_{f}\otimes I_{\mathcal{W}}  and  B  by  B\times W , so that the elements of  \mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{W}  can be labeled by the elements of  B .




Let us define for  t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\} 




 \left|\psi_{j}^{(t)}\right\rangle:=\left(\prod_{\tau=1}^{t}\bm{U}_{\tau}\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}\right)\bm{U}_{0}\left|\bm{0}\right\rangle, 



the state of algorithm  \mathcal{A}_{j}  after making  t  queries. We now prove by induction that for all  t\in\{0,1,\ldots,T\} 




 \displaystyle\left\|\left|\psi_{j}^{(t)}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}^{(t)}\right\rangle\right\|\leq\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|. 

(9)


For  t=0  the left-hand side is  0 , so the base case holds. Let us assume that (9) holds for  t-1 , we prove the inductive step as follows:




 \displaystyle\left\|\left|\psi_{j}^{(t)}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}^{(t)}\right\rangle\right\| 
 \displaystyle=\left\|\bm{U}_{t}\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}\left|\psi_{j}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle-\bm{U}_{t}\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}}\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\| 




 \displaystyle=\left\|\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}\left|\psi_{j}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}}\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\| 

(since norms are unitarily invariant)



 \displaystyle=\left\|\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}\left(\left|\psi_{j}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle+\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right)-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}}\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\| 




 \displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}\left(\left|\psi_{j}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right)\right\|+\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\| 

(triangle inequality)



 \displaystyle=\left\|\left|\psi_{j}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\|+\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(t-1)}\right\rangle\right\| 




 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{\tau=0}^{t-1}\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|. 

(by the induction hypothesis)





Since  \left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{j}^{(T)}\right\rangle , we additionally have that




 \left\|\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|\right)^{\!\!2}\leq T\sum_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}, 



where the last inequality uses the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
By Jensen’s inequality, we also have




 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\left\|\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\right\|]=\mathbb{E}[\sqrt{\left\|\left|\psi_{j}\right\rangle-\left|\psi_{0}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}}] 
 \displaystyle\leq\sqrt{T\sum_{\tau=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right]} 




 \displaystyle\leq T\sqrt{\max_{\tau\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi_{0}^{(\tau)}\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right]}. 

(10)


We now upper bound the right-hand side of Eq. (10) for an arbitrary pure state  \left|\psi\right\rangle  to conclude the proof.




 \displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|\psi\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right] 
 \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\left(\sum_{b\in B}|b\rangle\!\langle b|\right)(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left(\sum_{b^{\prime}\in B}|b^{\prime}\rangle\!\langle b^{\prime}|\right)\left|\psi\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right] 




 \displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{b\in B}|b\rangle\!\langle b|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})|b\rangle\!\langle b|\left|\psi\right\rangle\right\|^{2}\right] 

(since  \langle b|\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}|b^{\prime}\rangle=0  for  b\neq b^{\prime} )



 \displaystyle=\sum_{b\in B}\left|\langle b|\psi\rangle\right|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle b\right|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|b\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right] 




 \displaystyle\leq\max_{b\in B}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\left\langle b\right|(\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{j}}-\mathrm{O}_{\!f_{0}})\left|b\right\rangle\right|^{2}\right] 




 \displaystyle=\max_{b\in B}\mathbb{E}\left[|\exp(\pi\mathrm{i}f(b))-\exp(\pi\mathrm{i}f_{0}(b))|^{2}\right] 




 \displaystyle\leq\max_{b\in B}4\Pr[f(b)\neq f_{0}(b)]. 



Combining this upper bound with Eqs. (8) and (10), we obtain the desired inequality.
∎
