CYCLE 3 — PASSIVE PREDICTION DOES NOT CERTIFY COUNTERFACTUAL CONTROL
Date: 2026-10-08
Status: Exact two-model passive nonidentifiability theorem and a fully explicit probe calculation proved. One charged intervention improves, but does not eliminate, finite-sample control risk. No rank-free universal control certificate is obtained. No external review or novelty claim.

1. SCOPE AND ANSWER

The interface is one noisy raw trajectory from a stationary controlled-HMM environment under a fixed behavior policy. The learner receives no counterfactual/action oracle, finite-rank cap, test dictionary, or singular-value gap. The result separates two targets. Prediction of the observed behavior law can be easy and rank-free: in the example below, all outputs are iid fair bits. Prediction or control under an unobserved intervention is different: two stationary controlled HMMs give exactly the same passive trajectory law for every trajectory length, yet recommend opposite actions. Thus a rank-one exact predictive state for the behavior distribution supplies no uniform guarantee for counterfactual/control risk. A single charged intervention breaks exact observational equivalence and reduces the example's minimax decision risk from 1/16 to 3/64; a sample bound quantifies how many such probes give high confidence.

Here “counterfactual/control target” means an interventional predictive law under an action not taken by the behavior policy, conditioned on a recorded pre-action observation; it is not an individual-level cross-world counterfactual. This is a target-risk obstruction, not a claim that a finite-HMM law is impossible to learn from raw data. It also does not repeat a Hankel-basis identification argument: the proof is a two-point indistinguishability and decision-risk calculation.

2. TWO-MODEL THEOREM

Let S={0,1}, A={a,b}, O={0,1}, and fix any noise rate 0<eta<1/2. The emission kernel is a binary symmetric channel (BSC): O_t=S_t XOR N_t, with N_t iid Bernoulli(eta), independent of the state transitions. The action a transition resamples the latent state uniformly:

    T_a(s'|s)=1/2 for every s,s'.

There are two possible controlled environments, theta+ and theta-:

    T_b^+(s'|s)=1{s'=s},       T_b^-(s'|s)=1{s'=1-s}.

At the start of the observed trajectory S_0 is uniform. The fixed behavior policy always selects a. Under this policy the latent states are iid uniform, the observations are iid Bernoulli(1/2), and the joint action-observation process is stationary. Let lambda=1-2eta and Delta=lambda^2.

Theorem. For every passive trajectory length T, the laws of D_T=(A_1,O_1,...,A_T,O_T) under theta+ and theta- are identical. Nevertheless:

(a) After observing O_t=y, under intervention do(A_(t+1)=b),

    Pr_+(O_(t+1)=y | O_t=y, do(b)) = (1+Delta)/2,
    Pr_-(O_(t+1)=y | O_t=y, do(b)) = (1-Delta)/2.

The two conditional predictive distributions have total variation distance Delta. Any predictor qhat(D_T,y) of the next-observation law under do(b) therefore has worst-case expected TV risk at least Delta/2, no matter how large T is. This lower bound is attained by the fair Bernoulli predictor.

(b) Consider a one-step control task with reward 1{O_(t+1)=O_t}. Under action a the reward probability is 1/2 in both models. Under b it is (1+Delta)/2 in theta+ and (1-Delta)/2 in theta-. The optimal action is b in theta+ and a in theta-, with a regret gap Delta/2. With equal prior on the two models, the minimax passive-data policy has worst-case expected regret exactly Delta/4: choose b with probability 1/2 after any passive dataset. No passive-data policy has smaller worst-case regret.

(c) Under the fixed behavior policy a, the process has observable predictive rank one and the next-observation law is Bernoulli(1/2), independent of the entire past. From T observations the retained-data empirical mean has expected squared error 1/(4T) for the one-step behavior probability. This behavior-law prediction target is common to both environments and is not subject to the lower bound in (a), which concerns do(b).

Proof. Under a, each transition draws a fresh uniform state, so theta+ and theta- induce the same iid uniform hidden sequence and hence the same iid fair observations. Thus TV(P_+^T,P_-^T)=0 for every T. Under b in theta+, the state stays fixed for one transition; the two independent BSC noise bits disagree with probability 2eta(1-eta), so the outputs match with probability (1-eta)^2+eta^2=(1+lambda^2)/2. In theta-, the state flips, so the outputs match exactly when the two noise bits differ, with probability 2eta(1-eta)=(1-lambda^2)/2. The same formulas hold conditional on O_t=y by Bayes' rule and BSC symmetry.

For any fixed prediction g, the triangle inequality gives TV(g,q_+)+TV(g,q_-) >= TV(q_+,q_-)=Delta. Because the passive data and O_t have the same law under both models, averaging the two model risks gives at least Delta/2; choosing the midpoint Bernoulli(1/2) attains Delta/2. For control, let r(D_T,O_t) be the probability that a possibly randomized learner selects b. Its two model regrets are (Delta/2) E_+(1-r) and (Delta/2) E_-(r). The distributions of (D_T,O_t) agree, so their equal-prior average is Delta/4. Hence the worst of the two risks is at least Delta/4. Taking r=1/2 makes both risks Delta/4. Finally, under a the next state is an independent uniform draw, so every next observation is fair and independent; the rank-one and empirical-mean claims follow.

No rank, state labels, transition matrices, separation gap, or target basis is supplied to the learner in the passive experiment. The theorem remains valid even if the learner knows the two-model class: the passive data distributions coincide exactly.

3. A SINGLE CHARGED INTERVENTION

Insert one b step after an a step, and observe the resulting pair (Y,Z): Y is emitted after the a resampling, and Z after b. This requires no state observation or reset between probes. Repeating the pattern (a,b) yields independent pairs because each a transition resamples S uniformly, independent of the preceding pair.

For general eta, the pair probabilities are

    theta+:  P(00)=P(11)=(1+Delta)/4,  P(01)=P(10)=(1-Delta)/4,
    theta-:  P(00)=P(11)=(1-Delta)/4,  P(01)=P(10)=(1+Delta)/4.

Thus one pair has TV distance Delta between the two model laws. Under equal prior, the minimum error probability for deciding which model generated one pair is (1-Delta)/2. The rule “choose theta+ if Y=Z, otherwise theta-” attains it and has equal error under both models. Choosing the model's optimal control action after this test has worst-case expected regret

    (Delta/2) * (1-Delta)/2 = Delta(1-Delta)/4,

strictly below passive minimax regret Delta/4 for every 0<eta<1/2. This is the exact one-probe minimax regret: the equal-prior Bayes error gives a lower bound on worst-case risk, and the symmetric test attains that bound with equal risk under both models. One intervention does not identify the model perfectly; it changes exact nonidentifiability into a quantified, nonzero statistical risk.

Concrete noise level eta=1/4. Then lambda=1/2 and Delta=1/4. The behavior law remains iid fair and has rank one. Under do(b), the conditional match probabilities are 5/8 and 3/8; the next-observation TV separation is 1/4; passive minimax TV risk is exactly 1/8. Passive minimax control regret is exactly 1/16. With one b probe, the pair laws have cells

    theta+: (P00,P01,P10,P11)=(5,3,3,5)/16,
    theta-: (P00,P01,P10,P11)=(3,5,5,3)/16.

Their TV is 1/4, so equal-prior Bayes error is 3/8. The rule above therefore lowers worst-case control regret to 3/64, an exact reduction of 1/64 from the passive minimax value 1/16. For a predictor that outputs the selected model's do(b) law, worst-case expected TV risk is at most (1/4)(3/8)=3/32, a reduction of at least 1/32 from the passive lower bound 1/8.

For n independent (a,b) probe pairs, let Z_i=1{Y_i=Z_i}. Under theta+ it is Bernoulli((1+Delta)/2); under theta- it is Bernoulli((1-Delta)/2). Thresholding the sample mean at 1/2 has each model's error at most

    exp(-n Delta^2/2).

Consequently control regret is at most (Delta/2)exp(-n Delta^2/2). The one-pair KL divergence is

    KL(Pair_+ || Pair_-) = Delta log((1+Delta)/(1-Delta)).

Any test with both type-I and type-II errors at most epsilon<1/2 must, by data processing for KL, satisfy

    n Delta log((1+Delta)/(1-Delta))
       >= (1-2epsilon) log((1-epsilon)/epsilon).

This gives an information lower bound as well as the Hoeffding upper bound. For small Delta the KL is 2 Delta^2+O(Delta^4), so the number of probes scales at least on the order of Delta^(-2) log(1/epsilon); weak emission signal makes interventions expensive even though they are structurally informative.

4. FULL INTERVENTION AND MEASUREMENT COSTS

Charge c_R for the one initial ordinary reset, c_sel(a),c_sel(b) for selected actions, c_int(b) for the extra intervention operation, and c_O per observation. A fresh n-pair probe run uses

    C_probe(n)=c_R+n[c_sel(a)+c_sel(b)+c_int(b)+2c_O].

There are n b interventions, 2n selected actions, and 2n measured outputs. If the probes are inserted into an already-running stationary passive trajectory, no new reset is required. Relative to the passive continuation that would use a instead of b at each probe position, the incremental cost is

    DeltaC(n)=n[c_int(b)+c_sel(b)-c_sel(a)];

the observation at that position is charged in both arms, so it cancels only in this incremental comparison, not in C_probe. When action selection costs are equal, the added budget is exactly n c_int(b). If a protocol uses separately reset independent probe episodes instead, add n c_R and charge the two actions and two observations per episode. No intervention is treated as free.

The passive comparator with the same raw-data budget retains the entire length-T trajectory. Since the two passive laws are identical, retention cannot improve counterfactual risk; every rank-free truncation or model fit is a postprocessing of data with zero TV separation. Under the behavior-law target, the retained trajectory supplies the empirical frequency baseline with MSE 1/(4T). After interventions, the strongest same-data baseline retains all probe pairs and applies the likelihood-ratio test above; any compressed method is a postprocessing and has no information advantage over it. The example establishes the value of adding causal data, not an advantage for a particular truncation algorithm.

5. FAILURE BOUNDARY AND UNKNOWN

• If the target is only prediction under the fixed behavior policy, this counterexample gives no obstruction: both systems have the same rank-one iid behavior law, and ordinary frequency prediction suffices.
• If the target is a causal action effect or policy decision involving b, passive observations under pi_b(a)=1 do not identify it, even at infinite data. More passive samples cannot repair zero action support.
• The intervention result is for a named two-point model class and a one-step reward/predictive target. It does not prove general finite-state controlled-HMM identification from a small number of interventions, identify hidden labels, or guarantee long-horizon control.
• If eta is unknown, if the common behavior kernel is not known, or if the target reward differs, more parameters and a larger experiment design may be needed. The current bound is conditional on the example's known common emission and behavior mechanisms for analysis; the learner need not know which b-kernel is true.
• For a general class without an action-support condition, finite-rank cap, task definition, or margin, return UNKNOWN for counterfactual/control guarantees. This is not a claim that all observational off-policy evaluation is impossible: causal graphs, sequential ignorability, bridge functions, negative controls, or other structural assumptions can identify some targets.

6. PRIMARY-SOURCE RECONSTRUCTION

• Shpitser and Pearl, “Identification of Joint Interventional Distributions in Recursive Semi-Markovian Causal Models,” AAAI 2006 (technical report R-327), formulate identification from a causal graph plus an observational distribution and give necessary/sufficient graphical criteria in their model class. Our witness supplies the missing point for this interface: without a causal graph or equivalent structural restriction, two hidden-state controlled models can agree on all passive data and disagree under do(b). https://ftp.cs.ucla.edu/pub/stat_ser/r327.pdf
• Bennett and Kallus, “Proximal Reinforcement Learning: Efficient Off-Policy Evaluation in Partially Observed Markov Decision Processes,” arXiv:2110.15332, study off-policy value identification in POMDPs with observational trajectories, explicitly flag latent action confounding, and obtain identification under bridge-function conditions. Those assumptions are absent from the present no-oracle/no-dictionary interface; their result is not contradicted by this two-model example. https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.15332
• Eberhardt, Glymour, and Scheines, “On the Number of Experiments Sufficient and in the Worst Case Necessary to Identify All Causal Relations Among N Variables,” UAI 2005, derive intervention counts for causal DAG structure under assumptions including no latent common causes and no feedback. These counts are not transferable to a controlled HMM with hidden states and noisy outputs. https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/philosophy/docs/glymour/number%20of%20experiments%20for%20causal%20identification.pdf
• Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang, “A Spectral Algorithm for Learning Hidden Markov Models,” JCSS 2012 / arXiv:0811.4413, establish passive HMM learning under rank/singular-value separation. Their result concerns predictive sequence learning from observations, not action effects for actions absent from the observed support. https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.4413
