Automated conjecturing in mathematics with TxGraffiti

Abstract

TxGraffiti is a data-driven, heuristic-based computer program developed to automate the process of generating conjectures across various mathematical domains. Since its creation in 2017, TxGraffiti has contributed to numerous mathematical publications, particularly in graph theory. In this paper, we present the design and core principles of TxGraffiti, including its roots in the original Graffiti program, which pioneered the automation of mathematical conjecturing. We describe the data collection process, the generation of plausible conjectures, and methods such as the Dalmatian heuristic for filtering out redundant or transitive conjectures. Additionally, we highlight its contributions to the mathematical literature and introduce a new web-based interface that allows users to explore conjectures interactively. While we focus on graph theory, the techniques demonstrated extend to other areas of mathematics.

Keywords: Automated conjecturing; machine learned conjecturing; Graffiti; TxGraffiti.

1 Introduction

In 1948, Turing proposed that intelligent machines could play a significant role in mathematical research, particularly in tasks that require substantial intelligence but involve minimal interaction with the external world Turing (2004). This vision laid the foundation for early developments in computer-assisted mathematics. One of the first notable efforts in this area was Newell and Simon’s Logic Theorist, developed in the 1950s. Capable of proving theorems in first-order logic, Logic Theorist laid the groundwork for future automated reasoning systems, even though its early achievements were modest. Newell and Simon boldly predicted that computers would eventually discover and prove crucial mathematical theorems Simon and Newell (1958). Along with others of its era, this program focused heavily on automated theorem proving—a domain that reached a significant milestone in 1996 with the computer-assisted proof of the Robbins Conjecture McCune (1997).

While theorem proving became a well-established field, the complementary area of computer-assisted conjecturing (referred to as automated conjecturing) emerged with Wang’s work in the late 1950s Wang (1990). His Program II generated numerous statements in propositional logic that could be interpreted as conjectures or potential theorems. However, one of the key challenges in early automated conjecture-making systems, such as Wang’s, was filtering through vast numbers of generated statements to identify those of genuine mathematical interest.

The first computer program to effectively address this challenge was Fajtlowicz’s Graffiti, which generated meaningful mathematical conjectures by formulating inequalities between functions applied to mathematical objects. To further refine these conjectures and remove redundant or transitive results, Fajtlowicz introduced the Dalmatian heuristic, which systematically discarded possible new conjectures based on mathematical strength. The program was aptly named Graffiti because its conjectures were metaphorically written on the wall for mathematicians to explore. The conjectures of Graffiti have led to numerous publications, primarily in graph theory (see Fajtlowicz and Waller (1986); Chung (1988); Alon and Seymour (1989); Fajtlowicz (1989); Beezer et al. (1989); Favaron et al. (1990); Favaron et al. (1991a); Favaron et al. (1991b); Favaron et al. (1993); Griggs and Kleitman (1994); Fajtlowicz et al. (1995); Wang (1997); Firby and Haviland (1997); Bollobas and Erdos (1998); Bollobas and Riordan (1998); Caro (1998); Dankelmann et al. (1998); Jelen (1999); Codenotti et al. (2000); Beezer et al. (2001); Favaron et al. (2003); Zhang (2004); Dankelmann et al. (2005); Hansen et al. (2009); Cygan et al. (2012); Yue et al. (2019)) and mathematical chemistry (see Fowler (1997); Fowler et al. (1998); Fowler et al. (1999); Fajtlowicz and Larson (2003); Stevanovic and Caporossi (2005); Fajtlowicz (2005); Fajtlowicz et al. (2005); Doslic and Reti (2011)).

This paper describes TxGraffiti, a modern automated conjecturing program written and maintained by the author since 2017. TxGraffiti is named in homage to Graffiti and its successor, Graffiti.pc, developed by DeLaViña. Like its predecessors, TxGraffiti is primarily used for conjecturing in graph theory but features several distinct design elements. Moreover, TxGraffiti is now available as an interactive website, which demonstrates its conjecturing capabilities. While TxGraffiti has thus far focused on graph theory, the underlying principles apply to other areas of mathematics, and we mention such conjecturing in Section 4.

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: In Section 2, we recall the original Graffiti program and describe its process. In Section 3, we briefly discuss other notable conjecturing systems. In Section 4, we detail TxGraffiti’s implementation. Section 5 presents conjectures generated by TxGraffiti, which have led to publications. Finally, Section 6 offers concluding remarks.

2 Fajtlowicz’s Graffiti

As mentioned in the previous section, Fajtlowicz’s Graffiti program was the first system to circumvent the problem of filtering through many instances of mathematical conjectures. Indeed, Graffiti was able to generate conjectures that resulted in published mathematical results, particularly in graph theory (see Fajtlowicz and Waller (1986); Chung (1988); Alon and Seymour (1989); Fajtlowicz (1989); Beezer et al. (1989); Favaron et al. (1990); Favaron et al. (1991a); Favaron et al. (1991b); Favaron et al. (1993); Griggs and Kleitman (1994); Fajtlowicz et al. The Dalmatian heuristic applies two crucial tests to determine whether a proposed conjecture should be added to the list of conjectures:

Truth Test: The conjectured inequality must hold for all objects currently in the database. For example, if the conjecture proposes an upper bound for an invariant across a set of graphs, it must hold for every graph in the database. 

Significance Test: The conjecture must provide a better bound for at least one object than any previously proposed conjecture. This ensures that each conjecture added by Graffiti is meaningful, offering new insight by improving upon earlier bounds. 

By applying these tests, the Dalmatian heuristic ensures that each new conjecture is correct (with no known counterexample) and meaningful, advancing mathematical understanding by strengthening bounds for particular objects in the database. Fajtlowicz himself described this process in his own words, explaining how the heuristic ensures that only the most informative conjectures are retained by the program (see Fajtlowicz (1995)):

“The program keeps track of conjectures made in the past and when it runs across a new candidate for a conjecture then first of all it verifies if there is an example (in the database) demonstrating that the conjecture does not follow from the previous conjectures. If there is no such example then the conjecture is rejected as non-informative. If there is one, then the program proceeds with testing the correctness of the conjecture, and finally it verifies whether the conjecture should be rejected by one of its other heuristics. If the conjecture is accepted by the program then the list of conjectures is revised and those conjectures which are less informative than the new one are removed from the list and stored separately in the case the new conjecture will be refuted in the future.”

For an exceptionally detailed description of the Dalmatian heuristic, see Larson’s paper revisiting this heuristic Larson and Van Cleemput (2016).

The program works iteratively, constructing more complex expressions for an upper bound that satisfies the Dalmatian heuristic’s Truth Test. Once an expression is true for all objects in the database, Graffiti applies the Significance Test, determining whether the newly discovered bound offers additional insight beyond existing conjectures. However, many expressions generated during this process fail to pass the Truth Test, leading to numerous unnecessary computations.

2.2 Graffiti.pc, Counterexamples, and Data Quality

Graffiti.pc is another automated conjecturing program developed by DeLaViña under the supervision of Fajtlowicz during PhD studies. Like its predecessor, Graffiti.pc also implements the Dalmatian heuristic and primarily focuses on graph theory conjectures. However, while Graffiti was written in Pascal and operated on a dataset of a few hundred objects, Graffiti.pc was developed in C++ and worked with a significantly larger dataset—comprising millions of graphs. This expansion in scope sometimes required clusters of nodes to compute conjectures efficiently. Though Graffiti.pc was primarily designed for educational purposes, it also made notable contributions to domination theory in graph theory, as evidenced in works like Henning and Yeo (2013); Henning and Yeo (2014); Henning and Wash (2017). A list of open and resolved conjectures of Graffiti.pc can be found on this website.

Despite the dramatic increase in graphs available for analysis, this larger dataset did not substantially improve the quality of conjectures generated by Graffiti.pc when using the same methods as Graffiti. While the expanded dataset allowed Graffiti.pc to rule out some conjectures that Graffiti might have proposed, most counterexamples to conjectures from both programs still fell outside the datasets they used. Many of the smallest counterexamples to conjectures generated by Graffiti and Graffiti.pc are special graphs on a number of vertices not included in their datasets33

In graph theory, and likely in many other areas of mathematics, the dataset used for conjecture formulation often needs to focus on special objects—those well-studied in the literature or exhibit unique relationships among invariants. Including other, more generic objects can sometimes add unnecessary complexity or noise to the conjecture-generation process, obscuring meaningful insights. Larson also emphasized this notion of filtering out irrelevant data in discussions of his Conjecturing program, where selecting the right set of mathematical objects was crucial for generating insightful conjectures.

3 Other Notable Systems

Automated conjecturing systems have developed independently over the years, each contributing to the growing field of computer-assisted mathematical discovery in unique ways. One of the earliest systems, Lenat’s AM Lenat (1977); Lenat (1979); Lenat (1982), developed in the late 1970s, focused on exploring elementary mathematical concepts. Using heuristic search techniques, AM generated and refined concepts based on predefined rules, scoring them for interestingness. Although AM did not produce significant mathematical results like some later systems, it set a precedent for AI’s role in exploring new mathematical concepts and relationships, particularly in theory formation. While AM required human intervention and was limited by its knowledge base, it demonstrated the potential of AI in mathematical discovery.

Another early system, Epstein’s GT, or Graph Theorist, was developed in the 1980s and focused specifically on graph theory Epstein (1988). GT used symbolic computation to construct examples of graphs and explore their properties, generating conjectures and proofs. It was one of the first systems dedicated to discovering new conjectures in graph theory and played a key role in advancing the use of AI in this domain. However, Graffiti was the first to lead to publishable results in mathematics.

Colton’s HR program Colton et al. (1999); Colton (1999); Colton (2002), developed in the late 1990s, was a more general-purpose system for concept formation and conjecture generation across multiple mathematical fields, including algebra, graph theory, and number theory. HR applied production rules to foundational domain information the user provides, such as axioms or objects (e.g., Cayley tables in group theory or divisors in number theory), to form new concepts. The system evaluated these concepts for interestingness and generated conjectures by identifying common properties between concepts derived through different methods.

Hansen and Caporossi’s AGX, short for AutoGraphiX, was developed in the late 1990s and focused on solving combinatorial optimization problems related to graph invariants Caporossi and Hansen (2000); Caporossi and Hansen (2004). AGX used a neighborhood search strategy to explore possible graphs by adding or removing edges to optimize or satisfy certain constraints on invariants such as diameter, chromatic number, or eigenvalues. Equipped with heuristics to escape local optima, AGX could find global or near-optimal solutions. In addition to optimization, the system could refute conjectures by finding counterexamples and propose new conjectures by identifying patterns in extremal graphs. This system has been instrumental in generating important results in extremal graph theory.

Mélot’s GraPHedron, developed in the early 2000s, introduced a polyhedral approach to exploring relationships between graph invariants Mélot (2008). In GraPHedron, graphs are represented as points in a multidimensional space defined by their invariants, such as diameter, number of edges, or maximum degree. By analyzing the facets of polytopes associated with these graphs, GraPHedron generates conjectures through linear inequalities between invariants. These conjectures often arise from extremal graphs located at the vertices of the polytope, making GraPHedron particularly adept at identifying minimal systems of inequalities that describe optimal relationships between graph invariants. Recently, a successor to GraPHedron has been introduced called PHOEG Devillez et al. (2019).

Larson’s Conjecturing program, developed in the mid-2010s and heavily inspired by Graffiti, provides a heuristic-based approach to generating conjectures in various mathematical domains, particularly combinatorics and graph theory Larson and Van Cleemput (2016). The program operates by searching for inequalities between mathematical invariants, such as the number of vertices, edges, or chromatic numbers in graph theory, and also uses the Dalmatian heuristic. Integrated with SageMath, the Conjecturing program utilizes Sage’s libraries to compute the necessary invariants and then formulates conjectures based on patterns observed in the data. Once a conjecture is generated, it can be validated, refined, or refuted using Sage’s extensive computational tools, offering a dynamic and interactive environment for hypothesis generation and testing. The program emphasizes discovering non-trivial inequalities and patterns that have not yet been explored in the mathematical literature. Notably, Larson has been instrumental in popularizing automated conjecturing with the independence number project (see the independence number project website).

Each of these systems—from Lenat’s pioneering AM to Mélot’s GraPHedron and Larson’s Conjecturing—has contributed uniquely to the development of automated conjecturing. While they were developed independently, their collective impact demonstrates the potential of AI to explore mathematical structures, generate conjectures, and advance mathematical knowledge through various approaches.

4.3 Sorting Conjectures

4.4 The Static-Dalmatian Heuristic

After sorting the conjectures by their touch numbers, we may still be left with a substantial list of conjectured upper bounds on 
\alpha
α\alpha – in some cases, hundreds of conjectures. Furthermore, many of these conjectures are redundant, as they can be derived by transitivity from conjectures higher in the list. To address this redundancy, we adapt Fajtlowicz’s Dalmatian heuristic for conjecture acceptance into a new method, which we refer to as the static-Dalmatian heuristic.

Fajtlowicz’s original Dalmatian heuristic was dynamic, continuously refining the conjecture set by interacting with new data or revisiting previously generated conjectures to ensure only the most valuable conjectures were accepted. In contrast, the static-Dalmatian heuristic operates on a fixed, finite list of conjectures that have already been sorted based on touch number. The key distinction lies in this static property – static-Dalmatian works on a snapshot of conjectures generated from a single dataset without further refinement from new data, making it more appropriate for a precomputed list of conjectures.

The static-Dalmatian heuristic filters the sorted conjecture list (sorted by touch number in non-increasing order) to remove redundant conjectures that follow transitivity from others. The goal is to return a smaller, more meaningful set of conjectures representing the most informative and independent bounds based on their sharp instances.

Given a dataset and a list of conjectures sorted by touch number, the static-Dalmatian algorithm proceeds as follows:

1. 

Initialize: Start with the conjecture that has the highest touch number (the first conjecture in the sorted list) and keep track of the objects where this conjecture holds with equality (i.e., the sharp graphs).

2. 

Iterate Over Remaining Conjectures: For each subsequent conjecture in the sorted list, determine the set of sharp objects for that conjecture.

• 

If the set of sharp objects for the current conjecture matches the sharp objects of any previously selected conjecture, the conjecture is added to the list of selected conjectures.

• 

If the current conjecture introduces new sharp objects (i.e., objects where the inequality holds with equality but was not sharp for any previous conjecture), the conjecture is added to the list, and the set of sharp objects is updated to include the new sharp instances.

3. 

Return the Filtered List: The resulting list contains only the most independent conjectures, eliminating those that follow by transitivity or redundancy from higher-ranked conjectures.

The list of conjectures passed into the static-Dalmatian function must be sorted in non-increasing order by touch number. This is because conjectures with higher touch numbers are more informative, holding with equality on a maximum number of objects in the dataset. By starting with the conjecture that has the highest touch number, we maximize the initial set of sharp graphs (i.e., objects for which the conjecture holds with equality). Subsequent conjectures are then evaluated relative to this initial set, and only those that introduce new sharp objects—i.e., instances not already covered by previous conjectures—are retained.

Listing 2: The static_dalmatian function.

⬇

def static_dalmatian(df, conjectures):

    conj = conjectures[0]

    strong_conjectures = [conj]

    sharp_graphs = set(conj.get_sharp_graphs(df).index)

    for conj in conjectures[1:]:

        conj_sharp_graphs = set(conj.get_sharp_graphs(df).index)

        if any(conj_sharp_graphs == set(known.get_sharp_graphs(df).index) for known in strong_conjectures):

            strong_conjectures.append(conj)

        elif conj_sharp_graphs - sharp_graphs != set():

            strong_conjectures.append(conj)

            sharp_graphs = sharp_graphs.union(conj_sharp_graphs)

    return strong_conjectures

This static approach ensures that the conjectures remain valid and provide unique insights into the dataset’s structure, reducing unnecessary overlap and emphasizing conjectures that offer distinct mathematical value. Unlike Fajtlowicz’s original Dalmatian heuristic, which continuously refined conjectures based on new data, the static-Dalmatian heuristic offers a one-time pass over a fixed conjecture set. 4.6 Evaluating and Refining Conjectures in TxGraffiti

The final conjectures generated by TxGraffiti aim not merely to discover new theorems but rather to identify interesting and novel mathematical relationships. Even when a conjecture is disproven, generating and analyzing these conjectures often points to deeper patterns or connections that may have gone unnoticed. Each conjecture is built upon the data used to generate it, meaning the bounds presented reflect meaningful relationships that warrant further investigation. TxGraffiti is thus designed to stimulate new mathematical questions, explore uncharted territories, and reveal hidden structures in mathematical objects.

Notably, the full version of TxGraffiti maintains a database of known theorems when applied to graphs. This allows it to filter out conjectures corresponding to established results, ensuring that users are presented with only novel conjectures. This filtering mechanism prevents reinventing the wheel by discarding conjectures that have already been proven and focusing on new, unproven relationships. However, whether true or false, these conjectures require formal mathematical proof or counterexample to confirm their correctness and general applicability. Exploring even false conjectures often leads to discovering new mathematical relationships and insights that enrich the broader understanding of the subject.

4.6.2 Pepper’s Rule

Once a list of conjectures is presented to the user, the next crucial step is analyzing and evaluating them. A key approach, drawn from our experience, is what we call Pepper’s Rule. Named after mathematician Ryan Pepper—who has likely disproven more conjectures from Graffiti, Graffiti.pc, and TxGraffiti than anyone else—this rule advises that users should initially assume any conjecture they encounter is false. A conjecture should only be seriously considered if, after a thorough search, no counterexamples can be found.

Further, if many graph instances satisfy the conjecture with equality during the search, the conjecture is likely to be strong. For inequalities, the more instances of sharpness (i.e., when the inequality holds as equality), the more promising the conjecture becomes. This heuristic emphasizes the importance of sharpness in validating conjectures and encourages a healthy skepticism until the conjecture has been tested rigorously for counterexamples.

This focus on sharpness is particularly important when dealing with inequalities because the natural strategy in testing them is to try and construct a counterexample by maximizing one side of the inequality while minimizing the other. For example, when dealing with an upper bound conjecture, the aim is to make the left-hand side of the inequality as large as possible and the right-hand side as small as possible to break the conjecture. The opposite applies to lower bound conjectures. This notion is akin to how inequalities are formulated within TxGraffiti as discussed in Section 4.1. If, despite these efforts, the best result achieved is equality—meaning the left-hand and right-hand sides balance perfectly—this suggests that the conjecture may hold under much broader conditions than initially expected.

In such cases, sharpness acts as a litmus test: the more instances of equality found during counterexample searches, the stronger the conjecture appears to be. This process adds a layer of confidence when considering the conjecture’s validity, as it resists intentional attempts to invalidate it. Ultimately, sharpness not only signals the strength of the conjecture but also hints at deeper relationships between the involved graph invariants, making it a crucial criterion in assessing the conjecture’s robustness. Notably, there have been recent attempts at automating the process of finding counterexamples of conjectures of automated systems using deep reinforcement learning techniques Wagner (2021); Ghebleh et al. (2024).

4.6.4 Red Burton

Even when a conjecture is disproven, it does not render the process a failure. This is where Fajtlowicz’s Red Burton heuristic comes into play. After finding a counterexample to a conjecture, the next logical step is to identify the smallest counterexample and then prove that it is indeed minimal.

The Red Burton heuristic serves two important purposes: First, finding the smallest counterexample provides significant mathematical insight, as these minimal examples often reveal the fundamental structural properties of the studied objects. Second, the smallest counterexample is crucial because adding it to the dataset requires recomputing all the graph invariants and properties, many of which are 
{\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}}
𝒩​𝒫{\mathcal{N}\mathcal{P}}-complete problems and computationally expensive. By ensuring that the smallest counterexample is added, we minimize the computational burden associated with this process, making it more efficient to update the dataset.

Once the smallest counterexample is added to the dataset, it ensures that TxGraffiti will no longer generate the disproven conjecture in future iterations. Since all conjectures produced by TxGraffiti are valid on the data they are derived, the former conjecture will no longer appear. This approach prevents redundant conjectures and optimizes the system’s computational efficiency by focusing on minimal examples.

Thus, the Red Burton heuristic transforms a failed conjecture into an opportunity for deeper understanding and ensures that the system’s learning process remains computationally manageable. By incorporating the smallest counterexamples into the dataset, TxGraffiti becomes more robust and adaptive, producing stronger and more reliable conjectures without unnecessary computational overhead.

6 Conclusion

In this paper, we have introduced and detailed the workings of the artificial intelligence program TxGraffiti, demonstrating its effectiveness in generating meaningful conjectures in mathematical research, particularly within graph theory. We have shown that many of the conjectures produced by TxGraffiti have led to significant publications, underscoring the program’s potential to contribute valuable insights across various areas of mathematics.

Additionally, we have presented a new web-based interface for TxGraffiti, offering an accessible and interactive platform for researchers to explore conjectures. This platform facilitates further mathematical discoveries and collaborations, opening up new avenues for AI-assisted mathematics.
