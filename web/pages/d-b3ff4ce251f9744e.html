Abstract
    

Scientific discovery via symbolic regression is often viewed as statistically and computationally intractable because the hypothesis space of expressions grows combinatorially with depth.
This paper revisits the statistical side through the lens of PAC learning, focusing on compositional function trees built from a finite vocabulary of smooth operators (e.g., {+,×,sin,exp}\{+,\times,\sin,\exp\} and affine maps).
We prove that the relevant generalization quantity—Rademacher complexity, hence the excess risk—does not necessarily blow up exponentially with the number of distinct symbolic structures, but is controlled by (i) the depth dd and (ii) the Lipschitz constants of the base operators along the composed computation graph.
Concretely, under mild Lipschitz conditions on operators and bounded affine leaves, a finite-union bound over a vocabulary of size K=|ℋbase|K{=}|\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{base}}| together with Maurer-type vector contraction yields ℜn​(ℋcompd)≤(K​b​2​L)d−1​ℜn​(ℋcomp1)\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d})\leq(Kb\sqrt{2}\,L)^{d-1}\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{1}) with arity bound bb; corresponding high-probability risk bounds scale as 𝒪⁡(Ld/n)\mathcal{O}(L^{d}/\sqrt{n}) when K,b=O⁡(1)K,b{=}O(1) and ℜn(ℋcomp1)=O(n−1/2)\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{1}){=}O(n^{-1/2}).
We complement the theory with a modular codebase that trains differentiable operator trees (not MLPs) on synthetic “physics-like” targets of controlled depth and shows that the empirical generalization gap correlates positively with the predicted complexity term (L^d)/n(\widehat{L}^{d})/\sqrt{n}.

1 Introduction




Scientific machine learning frequently requires interpretable functional relationships from small datasets, not only accurate predictions.
While deep neural networks can approximate complex functions, they typically do not yield concise symbolic laws, raising concerns in the sciences where interpretability, extrapolation, and falsifiability are central.
Symbolic regression (SR) addresses this by searching over expressions built from a vocabulary of primitives, and has led to influential systems and benchmarks (Koza, 1992; Schmidt & Lipson, 2009a; Schmidt & Lipson, 2009b; Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020; Cranmer, 2023; La Cava et al., 2021).




However, SR is often described as “unlearnable” because the number of candidate expressions grows exponentially with depth, and finding the best expression is NP-hard in many formulations.
This view conflates computational hardness with statistical learnability.
PAC learning theory (Valiant, 1984) separates these issues: a hypothesis class can be PAC learnable (small sample complexity) even if optimizing over it is computationally challenging.




We formalize this separation for a compositional hypothesis space motivated by scientific discovery.
Our core message is that statistical generalization of compositional operator trees is governed by compositional stability (Lipschitzness) rather than by the raw count of symbolic structures.
Technically, we control generalization via Rademacher complexity (Bartlett & Mendelson, 2003; Mohri et al., 2018), extending classical contraction arguments (Ledoux & Talagrand, 1991) to tree-structured compositions.




Discrete search procedures incur computational costs proportional to the number of structures explored (Koza, 1992; Petersen et al., 2021), whereas uniform convergence controls how reliably empirical risk ranks hypotheses once attention is restricted to a class ℋcompd\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d} and its continuous parameters.
In practice SR couples both: a learner proposes syntax trees and fits coefficients (often nonlinearly); our bounds clarify what statistical scaling one should expect from that nonlinear estimation step when stability holds across primitives.

The experiments substitute dd by the fixed structural depth of the fitted tree and LL by a finite-batch estimator L^\widehat{L}.
Thus alignment between observed gaps and (L^d)/n(\widehat{L}^{d})/\sqrt{n} should be read as evidence that compositional stability predicts qualitative scaling rather than as identification of sharp numerical constants.






2 Related Work




Symbolic regression and scientific discovery.




Classic SR is commonly instantiated via genetic programming (Koza, 1992) or program synthesis-style search, with modern toolchains emphasizing physics heuristics (Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020), large-scale benchmarking (La Cava et al., 2021), and differentiable relaxations (Cranmer et al., 2020; Cranmer, 2023).
Recent work has also explored deep reinforcement learning and risk-seeking objectives for expression discovery (Petersen et al., 2021), as well as broader surveys of SR methodology and applications (Makke & Chawla, 2024).
In parallel, sparse discovery methods such as SINDy (Brunton et al., 2016) target dynamical systems by searching over a pre-specified library with sparsity penalties.
Our focus differs: we do not propose a new SR search algorithm; instead, we give a statistical learnability analysis of compositional operator classes and validate the predicted scaling empirically.





Generalization via complexity and stability.




Rademacher complexity and related uniform convergence tools are standard in learning theory (Vapnik, 1998; Bartlett & Mendelson, 2003; Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, 2014; Mohri et al., 2018).
For deep neural networks, stability and Lipschitz-like quantities (e.g., spectral norms) play central roles in generalization bounds (Bartlett et al., 2017).
Our analysis is closest in spirit to compositional complexity controls (e.g., chain rules for Gaussian/Rademacher processes and vector contraction inequalities) (Ledoux & Talagrand, 1991; Talagrand, 2014; van der Vaart & Wellner, 1996; Maurer, 2016a; Maurer, 2016b), specialized here to operator trees commonly used in scientific modeling.





Depth versus width.




Much neural-network theory emphasizes architectural depth jointly with width, norms, or margin quantities (Bartlett et al., 2017; Neyshabur et al., 2017).
Here depth enters explicitly through repeated composition of a fixed vocabulary of interpretable operators and produces multiplicative Lipschitz accumulation along trees rather than implicit coupling through parameters.
This distinction clarifies why symbolic/compositional models admit bounds stated directly in terms of (d,L)(d,L) without appealing to implicit layers or infinite-width limits.





Refined contraction and localization.




Recent refinements of Talagrand-type contraction for structured vector-valued maps sharpen dependence on norms and layer geometry beyond the textbook lemma (Truong, 2022).
Parallel localization tools—covering-number-based local Rademacher complexities (Lei et al., 2016) and offset/local frameworks that yield estimator-aware excess-risk bounds (Kanade et al., 2024)—provide routes toward sharper or hypothesis-dependent guarantees than the uniform bounds emphasized here.





Discrete syntax graphs versus continuous estimation.




Much SR methodology emphasizes discrete structure discovery combined with numerical tuning (Koza, 1992; Udrescu & Tegmark, 2020; Cranmer, 2023).
From a learning-theoretic viewpoint it is helpful to separate (i) the outer discrete optimization problem—often akin to program synthesis (Petersen et al., 2021)—from (ii) the inner regression problem induced once an architecture family is fixed.
Our complexity estimates speak primarily to (ii): depth enters compositionally through gradients/Lipschitz maps rather than through enumeration over exponentially many graphs.

3.2 Trees, depth, and branching




To make the role of depth explicit, it is useful to describe a hypothesis f∈ℋcompdf\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d} as a rooted operator tree whose internal nodes are operators from ℋbase\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{base}} and whose leaves are either input coordinates or constants.

4 Main Theoretical Results




Our bounds formalize the intuition that compositional trees generalize if each layer/operator is stable.
The key technical tool is a tree-structured contraction inequality, conceptually extending Ledoux–Talagrand contraction (Ledoux & Talagrand, 1991) and compositional Rademacher results (Maurer, 2016a) to our setting.




4.1 Lipschitz assumptions




Assumption 4.1 (Lipschitz operators).



Each base operator h∈ℋbaseh\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{base}} is (locally) LhL_{h}-Lipschitz on the relevant range of its inputs with respect to the Euclidean norm.
That is, for all admissible inputs u,vu,v,
|h⁡(u)−h⁡(v)|≤Lh​‖u−v‖2.|h(u)-h(v)|\leq L_{h}\left\lVert u-v\right\rVert_{2}.
Let L=maxh∈ℋbase⁡LhL=\max_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{base}}}L_{h}.





Remark 4.2.



For operators such as exp\exp, global Lipschitzness fails on ℝ\mathbb{R}. In scientific discovery, inputs are typically bounded by measurement ranges; our analysis is local to the operator ranges induced by the data distribution and the learned parameters. The experiments estimate L^\widehat{L} data-dependently on the fitted map rather than certifying a sharp uniform Lipschitz constant over all of ℋcompd\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d}.
When LL is measured relative to ranges induced by a specific hypothesis, contraction inequalities read hypothesis-dependent; uniform guarantees require either an envelope on admissible ranges (here enforced jointly by bounded outputs |f|≤B|f|\leq B, bounded covariates, and Assumption 3.2) or localization along the data-dependent slice relevant to empirical risk minimization (Koltchinskii & Panchenko, 2002; Lei et al., 2016; Kanade et al., 2024).

Interpretation.




The key point is that depth affects sample complexity through multiplicative stability growth—equivalently (K​b​2​L)2​(d−1)(Kb\sqrt{2}\,L)^{2(d-1)} when arity and vocabulary are tracked explicitly (Equation 14)—rather than through the (typically much larger) number of distinct symbolic trees of depth dd.

Cardinality versus complexity.




Let 𝒯d\mathcal{T}_{d} denote the finite collection of distinct shapes of binary trees of depth dd.
Uniform convergence requires controlling complexity over the union of realizable functions associated with each shape after accounting for continuous parameterizations at leaves.

Thus statistical complexity need not track worst-case enumeration when stability constants remain moderate—precisely the regime motivated by physics modeling where operators act locally smoothly on bounded domains.






4.5 A PAC-Bayes view: learning with structure priors




Rademacher bounds are uniform over ℋcompd\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d} and do not exploit a priori preferences for simpler trees.
An alternative is PAC-Bayes analysis, where one posits a prior π\pi over trees (e.g., favoring smaller depth/size via MDL) and controls the risk of a posterior ρ\rho learned from data (McAllester, 1999; Seeger, 2002; Catoni, 2007; Alquier, 2024).
For bounded losses, a representative bound has the form





𝔼f∼ρ​[R⁡(f)]≤𝔼f∼ρ​[R^S​(f)]+KL(ρ∥π)+log2​nδ2​(n−1).\mathbb{E}_{f\sim\rho}[R(f)]\leq\mathbb{E}_{f\sim\rho}[\widehat{R}_{S}(f)]+\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\rho\|\pi)+\log\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{\delta}}{2(n-1)}}.

(18)


Combining such KL penalties with the contraction certificates above yields guarantees trading description length (via π\pi) against multiplicative stability accumulation (K​b​2​L)d−1(Kb\sqrt{2}\,L)^{d-1} on top of ℜn​(ℋcomp1)\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{1}) (McAllester, 1999; Seeger, 2002).




Worked PAC-Bayes + stability sketch.




Suppose π\pi is a fixed MDL-style prior over tree shapes TT (e.g., π⁡(T)∝exp⁡(−code⁡(T))\pi(T)\propto\exp(-\mathrm{code}(T))) together with a Gaussian factor over leaf weights truncated to satisfy Assumption 3.2.
Let ρT\rho_{T} be any data-dependent posterior supported on trees of shape TT.
Combining Equation 18 with a union bound over shapes weighted by π⁡(T)\pi(T) yields an expectation bound under ρT\rho_{T} whose slack adds KL(ρT∥π)/n\sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\rho_{T}\|\pi)/n} to the empirical risk; coupling this with the deterministic stability multiplier (K​b​2​L)d−1(Kb\sqrt{2}\,L)^{d-1} from Equation 14 gives a qualitative trade-off between Occam penalties on syntax and Lipschitz-depth amplification along the chosen architecture.





Norm-based nets versus compositional primitives.




Deep-network bounds often scale with parameter norms or Jacobians while treating activation composition implicitly (Bartlett et al., 2017; Neyshabur et al., 2017).
Here each activation is an interpretable primitive with an explicit calculus (Proposition 4.3), which makes it natural to express guarantees directly through operator-level Lipschitz constants accumulated along tree edges rather than through aggregated weight matrices.

Limitations.




Our empirical study is deliberately controlled: inputs are one-dimensional and uniformly bounded, targets lie in a realizability-aligned architecture family (correct depth and operator set), and distribution shift is absent.
The estimator L^\widehat{L} measures stability of the trained map on in-distribution inputs rather than certifying a worst-case global Lipschitz constant over all structured hypotheses at depth dd; for compositions involving exp⁡(⋅)\exp(\cdot), local slopes can grow rapidly with learned ranges, so L^\widehat{L} need not reflect uniform worst-case operator stability without additional envelope constraints or interval arithmetic.
Finally, uniform bounds such as Equation 15 can be loose compared with hypothesis-dependent bounds when stronger structural priors are available (McAllester, 1999; Maurer & Pontil, 2011).







6 Conclusion




We provided a PAC learning perspective on scientific discovery with compositional function trees.
Despite the combinatorial explosion of symbolic structures, the statistical generalization of depth-dd compositional hypotheses is controlled by stability: Lipschitz constants accumulate multiplicatively along paths with an explicit (K​b​2​L)d−1(Kb\sqrt{2}\,L)^{d-1} prefactor on ℜn​(ℋcomp1)\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{1}) (Equation 14), yielding excess-risk scalings of order 𝒪⁡(Ld/n)\mathcal{O}(L^{d}/\sqrt{n}) when K,bK,b are treated as O⁡(1)O(1).
Our experiments with differentiable operator trees confirm that the generalization gap tracks the predicted complexity term.
These results support the view that, for stable operator vocabularies and modest depth, scientific discovery from small datasets is statistically feasible; the remaining challenge is computational search and inductive bias design.




Natural extensions include multi-dimensional covariates x∈ℝpx\in\mathbb{R}^{p} with coordinate-wise or norm-based Lipschitz bookkeeping, agnostic risk decompositions when the truth lies outside ℋcompd\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{comp}}^{d}, and combining PAC-Bayes priors over discrete syntax with the contraction estimates presented here.
Closing that loop would align statistical certificates more tightly with end-to-end SR systems that alternate discrete proposals with continuous refitting.

Impact Statement




This work gives learning-theoretic foundations for compositional symbolic models: stability and depth shape statistical complexity in ways that complement purely combinatorial views of expression search.
The emphasis on contraction along fixed trees clarifies that bounded-depth estimation with stable primitives need not inherit worst-case syntax-counting intuitions often cited against symbolic regression.
The perspective is complementary to algorithm design: it does not prescribe a particular symbolic search procedure, but clarifies the statistical scaling of nonlinear coefficient estimation once a vocabulary and depth budget are fixed.
Epistemic risks include trusting compact formulas under distribution shift, misspecification, or confounding; our guarantees do not address causal correctness or out-of-distribution validity.
Faster automated hypothesis discovery may also compress publication and decision timelines unless paired with scrutiny suited to the stakes.

Responsible deployment should pair such tools with domain theory, uncertainty quantification, replication, and appropriate governance in high-stakes settings.
