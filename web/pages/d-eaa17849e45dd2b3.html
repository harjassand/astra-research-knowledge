Simulating quantum computation by contracting tensor netw orks
Igor Markov1 and Yaoyun Shi 2
Department of Electrical Engineering and Computer Science
The University of Michigan
Ann Arbor, MI 48109-2122, USA
E-mail: {imarkov|shiyy}@eecs.umich.edu
Abstract
The treewidth of a graph is a useful combinatorial measure of how close the graph is to a tree. We prove
that a quantum circuit with T gates whose underlying graph has treewidth d can be simulated classically in
T O(1) exp(O(d)) time, which, in particular, is polynomial in T if d = O(log T ). Among many implications,
we show efﬁcient simulations for quantum formulas, deﬁned a nd studied by Yao (Proceedings of the 34th
Annual Symposium on F oundations of Computer Science, 352–361, 1993), and for log-depth circuits whose
gates apply to nearby qubits only, a natural constraint satisﬁed by most physical implementations. We also
extend the result to show that one-way quantum computation of Raussendorf and Briegel (Physical Review
Letters, 86:5188–5191, 2001), a universal quantum computation sch eme very promising for its physical
implementation, can be efﬁciently simulated by a randomize d algorithm if its quantum resource is derived
from a small-treewidth graph.
Keywords: Quantum computation, computational complexity, treewid th, tensor network, classical simula-
tion, one-way quantum computation.

1 Introduction
The recent interest in quantum circuits is motivated by seve ral complementary considerations. Quantum
information processing is rapidly becoming a reality as it a llows manipulating matter at a very ﬁne scale.
Such manipulations may create particular entangled statesor implement speciﬁc quantum evolutions — they
ﬁnd uses in atomic clocks, ultra-precise metrology, high-resolution lithography, optical communication, etc.
On the other hand, engineers traditionally simulate new designs before implementing them. Such simulation
may identify subtle design ﬂaws and save both costs and effor t. It typically uses well-understood host
hardware, e.g., one can simulate a quantum circuit on a commonly-used conventional computer.
More ambitiously, quantum circuits compete with conventional computing and communication. Quantum-
mechanical effects may potentially lead to computational speed-ups, more secure or more efﬁcient commu-
nication, better keeping of secrets, etc. To this end, one seeks new circuits and algorithms with revolutionary
behavior as in Shor’s work on number-factoring, or provable limits on possible behaviors. While proving
abstract limitations on the success of unknown algorithms a ppears more difﬁcult, a common line of rea-
soning for such results is based on simulation. For example, if the behavior of a quantum circuit can be
faithfully simulated on a conventional computer, then the possible speed-up achieved by the quantum circuit
is limited by the cost of simulation. Thus, aside from sanity-checking new designs for quantum information-
processing hardware, more efﬁcient simulation can lead to sharper bounds on all possible algorithms.
To simulate a quantum circuit, one may use a na¨ ıve brute-force calculation of quantum amplitudes that
has exponential overhead. Achieving signiﬁcantly smaller overhead in the generic case appears hopeless —
in fact, this very observation lead Feynman to suggest that quantum computers may outperform conventional
ones in some tasks. Therefore only certain restricted class es of quantum circuits were studied in existing
literature on simulation.
Classes of quantum circuits that admit efﬁcient simulation are often distinguished by a restricted “gate
library”, but do not impose additional restrictions on how gates are interconnected or sequenced. A case in
point is the seminal Gottesman-Knill Theorem [10] and its recent improvement by Aaronson and Gottesman
[1]. These results apply only to circuits with stabilizer ga tes — Controlled-NOT, Hadamard, Phase, and
single-qubit measurements. Another example is given by match gates deﬁned and studied by Valiant [22],
and extended by Terhal and DiVincenzo [20].
A different way to impose a restriction on a class of quantum c ircuits is to limit the amount of entan-
glement in intermediate states. Jozsa and Linden [13], as we ll as Vidal [23] demonstrate efﬁcient classical
simulation of such circuits and conclude that achieving qua ntum speed-ups requires more than a bounded
amount of entanglement.
In this work we pursue a different approach to efﬁcient simul ation and allow the use of arbitrary gates.
More speciﬁcally, we assume a general quantum circuit modelin which a gate is a general quantum operation
(so called physically realizable operators) on a constant number of qubits. This model, proposed and studied
by Aharonov, Kitaev and Nisan [2], generalizes the standard quantum circuit model, deﬁned by Yao [25],
where each gate is unitary and measurements are applied at th e end of the computation. We also assume
that (i) the computation starts with a ﬁxed unentangled stat e in the computational basis, and (ii) at the end
each qubit is either measured or traced-out.
We observe that the efﬁciency of simulating a quantum circuit C is closely related to the treewidth tw(GC),
where GC is the underlying circuit graph. This allows us to leverage the extensive graph-theoretical literature
dealing with the properties and computation of treewidth.
Theorem 1.1. Any quantum circuit C with T gates can be simulated by a classi cal algorithm in time
T O(1) exp(O(tw(GC)).
Therefore, given a function computable in polynomial time b y a quantum algorithm but not classically,

As a special case, we can simulate quantum formulas deﬁned an d studied by Yao [25], i.e., quantum

Yet another important application of our approach is to the simulation of one-way quantum computation.
In two inﬂuential papers [6, 16], Briegel and Raussendorf in troduced the concept of graph states, which
are quantum states derived from graphs, and show that an arbi trary quantum circuit can be simulated by
adaptive, single-qubit measurements on the graph state derived from the grid graph. Since a graph state can
be easily created and single-qubit measurements are much easier to implement than multi-qubit operations,
one-way quantum computation appears extremely promising for the physical realization of universal quan-
tum computation. An experimental demonstration of one-way quantum computation appeared in a recent
Nature article [24].
A natural question about one-way computation is to characte rize the class of graphs whose graph states
are universal for quantum computation. We show that having high treewidth is a necessary condition.

To model and reason about quantum circuits, we use the tensor network formalism. Being a direct
generalization of matrices, tensors capture a wide range of linear phenomena including vectors, operators,
multi-linear forms, etc. They facilitate convenient and fundamental mathematical tools in many branches of
physics such as ﬂuid and solid mechanics, and general relati vity [12]. A tensor network is a graph whose
every vertex v is labeled by a complex-valued tensor of size 2 degree(v), which is conceptually similar to
modeling a quantum gate by a unitary matrix. However, a tenso r network offers a variety of convolutions
(multiplication-like operations) — one for every edge. Our simulation contracts edges one by one and
performs respective convolutions, until there is only one v ertex left. Having degree 0, this vertex must be
labeled by a single number, which gives the desired measurem ent probability. In contrast with previous
simulation techniques, we do not necessarily simulate indi vidual gates in their original order — in fact, a
given gate may even be simulated partially at several stages of the simulation.
Our simulation algorithm is related to algorithms for other tasks in that its runtime depends on the
treewidth of a graph derived from the input. Bodlaender wrot e an excellent survey on this subject [7].
Particularly relevant are algorithms based on “vertex elim inations”, e.g., the Bucket Elimination algorithm
for Bayesian Inference [9]. Another parallel can be made with the work by Broering and Lokam [8], which
solves Circuit-SAT in time exponential in the treewidth of t he graph of the given circuit. However, to our
best knowledge, we are the ﬁrst to relate the treewidth of a quantum circuit to its classical simulation.
Our results are applicable to the simulation of classical pr obabilistic circuits, which can be modeled by
matrices, similarly to quantum circuits. Such simulation h as recently gained prominence in the literature
on the reliability of digital logic [14], and is particularly relevant to satellite-based and airborne electronics
which experience unpredictable particle strikes at higher rates.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After introduc ing notation, we describe how quantum
circuits and their simulation can be modeled by tensor netwo rks. The runtime of such simulation depends
on the graph parameter that we call the contraction complexi ty. To this end, we show in the following
section that contraction complexity can differ from treewi dth only by a constant factor. After completing
the proof for the main theorem, we discuss several concrete applications.

2 Notation and deﬁnitions
For integer n ≥ 1, let [n] def= {1,2, . . . ,n}. An ordering π of an n-element set is denoted by π(1), π(2), . . .,
π(n). In this paper, graphs are undirected and may have multiple e dges or loops. If G is a graph, its vertex
set is denoted by V (G) and its edge set by E(G). The degree of a vertex is the number of edges incident to
it. In particular, a loop counts as 1 edge. The maximum degree of a vertex is denoted by D(G).
Treewidth of a graph. Let G be a graph. A tree decomposition of G [17] is a treeT , together with a function
that maps each w ∈ V (T ) to a subset Bw ⊆ V (G), called a bag. In addition, the following conditions must
hold.
(T1) ⋃
v∈V (T ) Bv = V (G).
(T2) For any edge {u, v} ∈ E(G), ∃w ∈ V (T ), {u, v} ⊆ Bw.
(T3) For any u ∈ V (G), the set of vertices w ∈ V (T ) with u ∈ Bw form a connected subtree.
The width of a tree decomposition is maxw∈V (T ) |Bw| −1. The treewidth of G is the minimum width among
its tree decompositions. We may refer to a node in T by its bag when the context is clear.
Treewidth can be deﬁned in several seemingly unrelated ways, e.g., as the minimum k for which a given
graph is a partial k-tree, as the induced width (also called the dimension), or as the elimination width [19, 4].
An elimination ordering π of a graph G is an ordering of V (G). The induced width of a vertex v ∈ V (G)
in the ordering is the number of its neighbors at the time it is being removed in the following process: start
with π(1), connect all of its neighbors, remove π(1), then repeat this procedure with the next vertex in the
ordering. The width of π is the maximum induced width of a vertex, and the induced width of G is the
minimum width of an elimination ordering. It is known that th e induced width of a graph is precisely its
treewidth [4].
Quantum circuits. We ﬁx an orthonormal basis for the state space of a qubit and la bel the basis vectors
with |0⟩and |1⟩. The state space of one qubit is denoted by H def= C2. The space of operators on a vector
space V is denoted by L(V ). In this paper, a quantum gate with a input qubits and b output qubits is a
superoperator Q : L(H ⊗a) → L(H ⊗b). In existing applications one typically has a ≥ b and often a = b.
The ordering of inputs and outputs is in general signiﬁcant. If Q is a traced out operator, then b = 0, and
Q(|x⟩⟨y|) = ⟨x|y⟩, for all x, y ∈ {0,1}a.
A gate library B for quantum circuits is a set of quantum gates, which can be re plicated and connected
to each other (by “wire segments”). We assume that the maximu m number of qubits on which a quantum
gate can act is bounded by a constant (often two or three in app lications). A quantum circuit of size T on
n qubits with gates from B consists of the following: (1) A sequence of n wires, ordered 1, 2, . . ., n, each
of which represents one qubit. (2) A sequence of T quantum gates g1, g2, . . ., gT , each of which is drawn
from B, and is applied to some subset of the wires. (3) The last gate a cting on a qubit is either a traced-out
superoperator or a projective measurement in the computational basis (i.e., |0⟩⟨0| or |1⟩⟨0|).
The graph of a quantum circuit C, denoted by GC, is obtained from C by regarding each gate as a vertex,
wire segments as edges between vertices, adding a distinct n ew vertex to the open end of each input wire,
and similarly for output wires.

4 Contraction complexity and treewidth
Let G be a graph with vertex set V (G) and edge set E(G). For the convenience of the future discussion,
in our formal deﬁnition of contracting an edge, we allow a loo p to stay and count toward the degree of a
vertex. It can be veriﬁed that in the optimal contraction pro cess, this relaxed rule would at most double the
maximum degree of any new vertex resulted from contraction.
Formally, a contraction of an edge e removes of e and replaces its end vertices (or vertex) with a single
union vertex, whose tensor is computed as in Equation 1. A contract ion ordering π is an ordering of all
the edges of G, π(1), π(2), . . ., π(|E(G)|). Note that contracting all π(i), 1 ≤ i ≤ |E(G)|, sequentially,
reduces G to a single vertex. The complexity cc (π) of π is the maximum degree of a union vertex during
the contraction process. The contraction complexity of G, denoted by cc(G), is the minimum complexity of
a contraction ordering. Note that for any graph G, cc(G) ≤ |E(G)|.
The nature of cc(G) becomes clearer once we consider the line graph of G, denoted by G∗. That is, the
vertex set of G∗ is V (G∗) def= E(G), and the edge set is
E(G∗) def= {{e1, e2 ∈ E(G)} : ∃v ∈ V (G) such that e1 and e2 are both incident to v}.

Proposition 4.1. F or any graph G,cc(G) = tw(G∗) +1.
Proof. There is a one-to-one correspondence of the contraction of a n edge in G and the elimination of a

Computing the treewidth of a general graph is NP-hard [5]. We do not know if this is still true for

Theorem 4.2 (Robertson and Seymour [18]). There is a deterministic algorithm that given a graph G out-
puts a tree decomposition of G of width O (tw(G)) in time |V (G)|O(1) exp(O(tw(G))).
Applying a standard technique for using a tree decomposition, we obtain:
Theorem 4.3. Given a tree decomposition of G ∗ of width d, there is a deterministic algorithm that outputs
a contraction ordering π with cc(π) ≤ d + 1 in polynomial time.
Proof. Denote the tree decomposition by T . Repeat the following till the tree decomposition becomes
an empty graph. Choose a leaf ℓ in T . If ℓ is the single node of T , output vertices (of G∗) in Bℓ in
any order. Otherwise, let ℓ′ be its parent. If Bℓ ⊆ Bℓ′, remove ℓ and repeat this process. Otherwise, let
e ∈ Bℓ − Bℓ. Output e, remove it from the tree decomposition and continue the process, until all nodes of the
tree decomposition are removed.
Note that since each e output does not appear in any other bags in the tree decomposition, all its (current)
neighbors appear in the same bag. Hence its induced width is at most d. By the one-to-one correspondence
of the vertex elimination in G∗ and the contraction process in G, cc(π) ≤ d + 1. ⊓ ⊔

5 Treewidth and quantum circuits
Numerous published works on treewidth include precise boun ds for speciﬁc classes of graphs. Planar and
series-parallel graphs are such examples. Nevertheless, some classes of graphs arising from quantum circuits
have not been characterized from the perspective of treewid th. To this end, we prove below a slightly
stronger result than Corollary 1.5.
Proposition 5.1. Let C be a quantum circuit of size T , and r be the minimum intege r so that for any two

7 Discussions
Given a classical intractable problem that admits an efﬁcie nt quantum solution, if it exists, then our result
shows that there is an inherent tradeoff between the treewidth and the size of quantum circu its for solving
it. Therefore, it is a natural and fundamental problem to inv estigate this tradeoff for speciﬁc problems. It
would also be very interesting to investigate this type of qu estion for classical circuits. One may start with
simple functions like Parity.
The current knowledge on the limit of efﬁcient quantum compu tation is still far from satisfying. There
are natural directions opened up by our approach. For exampl e, is there a systematic method to reduce

the treewidth of a quantum circuit without affecting the com putation? One may expect that such a reduc-
tion would necessarily increase the size of the circuit. How ever, for a goal less ambitious than efﬁciently
simulating all quantum computation, it may still lead to signiﬁcant progress.
