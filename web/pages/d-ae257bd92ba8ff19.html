## 1. Exact interface and proposed conclusion

Let sigma be normalized area measure on S². For an integer T>=1 let e contain any real orthonormal basis of the nonconstant harmonics of degrees 1,...,T, with M=T(T+2). The acquisition target is a list of distinct rational unit vectors x_i with

    N=O(T²),   b=N^-1 sum_i e(x_i),
    ||b||_2 <= beta_T=1/[2048(T+1)^5],
    S=N^-1 sum_i e(x_i)e(x_i)^t > I/2.             (1)

The proposed deterministic algorithm below uses

    q=600(T+1),  N=2q²=720000(T+1)²,
    h=12/q=1/[50(T+1)],
    delta=2^-24(T+1)^-8,
    I=20+10 ceil(log_2(T+1)).                       (2)

It returns (1) in polynomial bit time. Every final direction has O(log(T+1)) coordinate bits. Polynomial time is in the explicit degree T/output scale; merely writing N vectors already costs Omega(T²). The coefficient 720000 is conservative and no numerical experiment at that constant is offered. The method optimizes the largest coordinate of a tangent correction rather than choosing the Euclidean pseudoinverse of the moment derivative. Its proof uses a gradient L1 dual norm; it never computes that norm.

## 2. An elementary quantitative sampling lemma

For a fixed vector a in R³ write L_a Q(x)=(a cross x) dot grad Q(x). The three coordinate rotations are L_1,L_2,L_3. All preserve spherical polynomial degree. For every unit fixed a and spherical polynomial Q of degree <=s,

    ||L_a Q||_1 <= s ||Q||_1.                       (3)

Appendix A proves the sharp trigonometric interpolation identity underlying (3), so no unknown constant from an MZ theorem is needed. Iteration gives ||L_{j_1}...L_{j_k}Q||_1 <=s^k||Q||_1.

Let R_i be an equal-area partition with sigma(R_i)=1/N. Suppose chosen z_i are at distance <=D from every y in R_i. The geodesic from y to z_i is the rotation of y through d<=D about some fixed unit axis a=a(y,z_i). Taylor expansion along that rotation is valid to all orders: its restriction is a trigonometric polynomial. Expanding each (sum_j a_j L_j)^k into its 3^k ordered words and using |a_j|<=1 gives

    |Q(z_i)-Q(y)|
      <= sum_(k>=1) D^k/k! sum_(j_1,...,j_k)
                         |L_{j_1}...L_{j_k}Q(y)|.

Integrate over cells, sum, and use (3). The nonnegative series has a finite integrable majorant. This proves both

    N^-1 sum_i |Q(z_i)| <= exp(3Ds)||Q||_1,
    |N^-1 sum_i |Q(z_i)| - integral |Q||
                       <=[exp(3Ds)-1]||Q||_1,      (4)

and the same last bound for signed Q rather than |Q|. The axis can depend on y and i; every word on the majorant has a globally fixed coordinate axis. The chosen point z_i may be a maximum in a neighborhood. No convexity, separation, density lemma, or fixed-axis assumption on the geodesics is implicit.

For P of degree <=T set G(P)=integral |grad_S P|. The identity

    sum_j (L_jP)^2=|grad_S P|²

and the Euclidean norm inequality applied before integration show that sampling the gradient at such z_i has error at most

    sqrt(3)[exp(3DT)-1]G(P).                       (5)

Now choose anchors c_i with R_i subset B_h(c_i), and consider arbitrary z_i in B_h(c_i). Then D<=2h. Since hT<=1/50,

    exp(6hT) <= 1/(1-3/25)=25/22,
    sqrt(3)<7/4,
    N^-1 sum_i |grad_S P(z_i)| >=G(P)/2,
    N^-1 sum_i sup_(B_h(c_i)) |grad_S P| <2G(P).   (6)

Indeed the loss in (5) is <21/88<1/2, and the upper bound is <175/88. For a tangent unit v at x, the great-circle second derivative is L_(x cross v)^2P(x)=Hess_S P(x)[v,v]. Therefore

    ||Hess_S P(x)||_op <=sum_(j,k)|L_jL_kP(x)|.

Use the first bound in (4), separately at a maximizer of each scalar summand in each B_h(c_i). Then (3) gives

    N^-1 sum_i sup_(B_h(c_i)) ||Hess_S P||_op
       <= exp(6hT) sum_(j,k)||L_jL_kP||_1
       <=3 sqrt(3) exp(6hT) T G(P) <6T G(P).      (7)

These are uniform neighborhood bounds, not just evaluations at the current nodes.

For P² of degree <=2T, the signed version of (4) gives at any such nodes

    |N^-1 sum_i P(z_i)²-||P||_2²|
      <=[exp(12hT)-1]||P||_2² <=(6/19)||P||_2².   (8)

Consequently the final Gram matrix is >=(13/19)I>I/2 as long as the neighborhood invariant holds.

## 3. Explicit equal-area cells and computable centroids

For j=1,...,q define the northern ring

    1-j²/q² <= z <= 1-(j-1)²/q².

Split it into 2j-1 equal longitude wedges. Reflect these cells across the equator for the south. Since normalized area is dz dphi/(4pi), each of the 2q² cells has area 1/(2q²).

The polar angle on ring boundary j is theta_j=2 asin[j/(sqrt(2)q)]. Its derivative in j is <=2/q. The radial span of a cell is therefore <=2/q. Its outer sin(theta) is <=sqrt(2)j/q, and its longitude span is 2pi/(2j-1). Moving first along a meridian and then around a parallel bounds any pairwise geodesic distance by

    2/q + 2pi sqrt(2)j/[q(2j-1)] <12/q=h.        (9)

The full first-ring wedge has no preferred longitude; the same conservative bound applies. Boundaries have measure zero.

Let m_i=N integral_(R_i) y d sigma and c_i=m_i/|m_i|. These are true real anchors used only in analysis/initial acquisition. For every y in R_i, y dot m_i >=cos h, while |m_i|<=1. Hence y dot c_i>=cos h, proving R_i subset B_h(c_i). Also |m_i|>=cos h>1/2. This argument uses all pairwise cell distances directly and does not require the centroid to lie in the cell or introduce a factor of two.

Their coordinates are explicitly computable. Put m=2j-1, r_0=(j-1)/q, r_1=j/q, alpha=pi/m, and phi_k=(2k+1)pi/m. For a northern cell,

    m_z=1-(r_0²+r_1²)/2,
    A_j=[2q²/m] integral_(r_0)^(r_1) r² sqrt(2-r²) dr,
    (m_x,m_y)=A_j [sin(alpha)/alpha]
                         (cos(phi_k),sin(phi_k)). (10)

For m=1 the transverse component is exactly zero. Southern m_z has the opposite sign. This is a normalized area average, not an arbitrary cell representative.

Certified polynomial-bit approximation of (10) needs no oracle for a geometric minimizer. Expand sqrt(2-r²)=sqrt(2) sum_(l>=0) binom(1/2,l)(-r²/2)^l, integrate its polynomial terms exactly over rational limits, and use the geometric tail (r²/2<=1/2). O(b+log q) terms suffice for b output bits, including the harmless factor q². Rational bisection computes sqrt(2) and |m_i|. Machin's identity pi=16 atan(1/5)-4 atan(1/239), with its alternating geometric tails, and Taylor series for sin/cos at bounded arguments compute the angular factors in polynomial time. Division by alpha needs only O(log q) extra guard bits. The lower bound |m_i|>1/2 makes normalization stable. Thus rational stereographic parameters within Euclidean delta/16 of c_i's true parameters are obtainable in poly(T,log(1/delta)) bit time. In the initial hemisphere chart the sign of m_z is known from (10), so no undecidable real sign comparison is required.

## 4. Centroid residual in the gradient dual norm

For mean-zero P define B_X(P)=N^-1 sum_i P(x_i) and

    epsilon(X)=sup_(P!=0) |B_X(P)|/G(P).          (11)

G is a norm on the finite-dimensional mean-zero polynomial space. Write xi=log_(c_i)y, theta=|xi|<=h and t=(I-c_ic_i^t)y. Then xi=(theta/sin theta)t. The ambient centroid identity gives N integral_(R_i) t=0. For theta<=h<1/2,

    theta/sin(theta)-1 <=theta²,
    |N integral_(R_i) log_(c_i)y| <=h³.          (12)

Taylor's formula on the cell geodesics, together with (6)-(7), therefore proves

    |B_C(P)| <=[2h³+3Th²]G(P) <=5Th²G(P).       (13)

This supplies the second-order residual even though the anchors are ambient normalized barycenters rather than Karcher centroids. Initial rational resetting within distance delta contributes at most 2delta G(P). Thus

    epsilon_0 <=5Th²+2delta <=E_0:=6Th².         (14)

## 5. Finite rational LP supplies the bounded tangent correction

Let the current nodes x_i lie in B_h(c_i), and write epsilon=epsilon(X). Define the moment derivative

    J_X(v)(P)=N^-1 sum_i grad_S P(x_i) dot v_i.

The image K_r of the product tangent Euclidean balls |v_i|<=r is compact, convex and symmetric, with support function

    h_(K_r)(P)=r N^-1 sum_i |grad_S P(x_i)|
                              >=r G(P)/2.       (15)

By the separating hyperplane theorem, (11) and (15) imply -B_X is in K_(2epsilon). This is an exact finite-dimensional linear correction result. In particular, J has full row rank: a nonzero mean-zero polynomial cannot have every sampled gradient zero under (6).

The algorithm does not evaluate epsilon. Use any rational harmonic basis f_1,...,f_M and solve the rational LP

    minimize d,
    N^-1 sum_i grad_S f_l(x_i) dot w_i=-B_X(f_l),
                                l=1,...,M,
    -d <=w_(i,a)<=d,  a=1,2,3;  0<=d<=1.       (16)

The Euclidean-ball witness makes (16) feasible with d<=2epsilon. Project the LP solution exactly,

    v_i=(I-x_ix_i^t)w_i.

Gradients are tangent, so all derivative equations remain exact, and

    max_i |v_i| <=sqrt(3)d <4epsilon.             (17)

This is the step that bypasses a possible logarithmic L-infinity bound for a specific pseudoinverse. It uses all tangent degrees of freedom, not a fixed inverse-gradient kernel.

## 6. Rational retraction, resets, and contraction

For rational unit x and rational tangent v set

    R_x(v)=[(1-|v|²/4)x+v]/[1+|v|²/4].          (18)

It is exactly rational and exactly unit. Its geodesic displacement is theta=2 atan(|v|/2)<=|v|, and |theta-|v||<=|v|³/12. Along the great circle, subtracting the exact linear equation in (16) from Taylor's formula gives, for R=max_i|v_i|<=1,

    |B_(R_X(v))(P)|
       <=[R³/6+3TR²]G(P)
       <=64T epsilon² G(P),                    (19)

using (6)-(7) and (17). This bound assumes the paths remain in B_h(c_i); the next paragraph proves that simultaneously by induction.

Reset the retracted nodes to distinct rational unit nodes within distance delta, using the deterministic procedure in Section 7. The reset error is <=2delta G(P) by (6). Therefore

    epsilon_(k+1) <=64T epsilon_k²+2delta.        (20)

While epsilon_k<=1/(256T), this is <=epsilon_k/4+2delta. With (14), the elementary rational bounds

    E_0 T <6/2500,
    6/2500+(8/3)2^-24 <1/256

give the uniform inductive estimate

    epsilon_k <=4^-k E_0+(8/3)delta.              (21)

The total distance from true anchor c_i through I corrections and resets is at most

    delta+sum_(k<I)(4epsilon_k+delta)
      <=(16/3)E_0+[1+(35/3)I]delta <0.65h<h.    (22)

For the last inequality, the first term is 32Th²<=0.64h. Also ceil(log_2(T+1))<=T gives I<=30(T+1), so the remaining ratio to h is at most 17550*2^-24(T+1)^-6<0.01. The same bound covers each partial path, since its actual displacement is no greater than its allocated length in (22). This closes the neighborhood induction used in (19)-(21). No a priori convergence, rank, or separation assumption is left unproved.

The specified I in (2) gives 4^-I<=2^-40(T+1)^-20. Each of the two terms in (21) is <=beta_T/[32(T+1)]. Thus

    epsilon_I <=beta_T/[16(T+1)],
    ||b||_2 <=(T+1)epsilon_I <=beta_T/16.        (23)

The second inequality follows from G(P)<=||grad P||_2<=sqrt(T(T+1))||P||_2 for harmonic degrees <=T. Equation (8) supplies the Gram test with fixed slack. ## 7. Distinct rational resets with short coordinates

Use one of the two stereographic maps

    F_+(s,t)=(2s,2t,1-s²-t²)/(1+s²+t²),
    F_-(s,t)=(2s,2t,-1+s²+t²)/(1+s²+t²).

For a unit input x choose F_+ if x_z>=0 and F_- otherwise. Its inverse parameter has norm<=1. The map differential has operator norm 2/(1+s²+t²)<=2, so a straight parameter segment proves d(F(p),F(p'))<=2|p-p'| globally.

Let lambda=delta/(16N). Round each exact rational parameter to the nearest lambda-grid; initially use a certified rational approximation of the true centroid parameter within Euclidean delta/16 first. For node i try parameters (s+j lambda,t), j=0,...,N, and select the first whose exact rational sphere image differs from every previously selected image. At most i-1 candidates can be excluded: the selected map is injective and its candidates are distinct. All candidate changes have Euclidean parameter size <=delta/16+lambda/sqrt(2)+N lambda<delta/4. Sphere movement is <delta/2, conservatively <=delta as charged throughout the proof.

This procedure is deterministic, works even if input nodes collide, and with naive comparison against all prior selected images needs at most O(N³) exact equality comparisons. A deterministic ordered dictionary can reduce this to O(N² log N) rational lexicographic comparisons, but that improvement is not needed for the polynomial bound. The grid parameters have numerator/denominator O(N/delta); their images have O(log(N/delta))=O(log(T+1)) coordinate bits. Rationality and the unit identity are exact. No lower bound on separation is inferred; it is unnecessary for (1). Resetting each iterate controls subsequent LP input height rather than allowing exact-retraction denominators to accumulate.

## 8. Rational basis, bit complexity, and algorithm

An explicit rational harmonic basis is supplied by the real and imaginary parts of

    H_(l,m)=(x+iy)^m sum_(j=0)^floor((l-m)/2)
      (-1)^j (2l-2j)! z^(l-m-2j)(x²+y²+z²)^j
                  /[2^l j!(l-j)!(l-m-2j)!],      (24)

with 1<=l<=T, 0<=m<=l, one real function for m=0 and both parts for m>0. These are homogeneous harmonic polynomials and give M=T(T+2) linearly independent restrictions. Their coefficient bits are O(T log(T+1)); powers/factorials, evaluations and ambient derivatives are computable with polynomial rational arithmetic. Tangential derivatives at rational unit nodes are (I-xx^t)grad H. The exact moment equations in this basis are the equations in every normalized basis.

Normalized squared norms are rational:

    nu_(l,0)=1/(2l+1),
    nu_(l,m,real)=nu_(l,m,imag)
              =(l+m)!/[2(2l+1)(l-m)!],  m>0.    (25)

These follow from Legendre/associated-Legendre orthogonality. If desired, the final verifier computes sum_l B(f_l)^2/nu_l exactly and tests the rational matrix C-diag(nu)/2 by exact LDL or principal minors. This is equivalent to (1); square roots never enter the LP.

With reset coordinate height B=O(log(T+1)), an individual evaluated coefficient has polynomial height, O(T(B+log(T+1))). Summing N rationals with differing denominators can cost O(NT(B+log(T+1))) bits; no common-denominator assumption is made. There are M=O(T²) equations and 3N+1=O(T²) variables, with a polynomial encoding length. Standard exact rational linear programming yields an optimal rational solution in polynomial bit time. Its solution height is polynomial by the determinant/basic-solution bound; the reset erases that potentially larger height before the next iteration. Linear dependence of equality constraints can be removed by exact Gaussian elimination, although (6) already proves full row rank. The d<=1 condition makes the feasible polytope bounded. This is a classical rational-LP dependency, not a floating-point solver guarantee. Its primary prerequisite is Khachiyan's *Polynomial algorithms in linear programming* (1980); only bibliographic/abstract access was used here, and the standard theorem must be treated as an explicit inherited algorithmic primitive.

Pseudocode, with all tolerance/cardinality choices made before acquiring nodes:

    acquire(T):
      compute q,N,h,delta,I from (2)
      enumerate the cells in Section 3
      compute their centroid chart parameters with certified delta/16 error
      X = distinct_stereographic_reset(parameters, delta)
      enumerate the rational harmonic basis (24)
      repeat exactly I times:
        evaluate moments and tangential derivative matrix exactly at X
        solve rational LP (16) exactly
        project w_i to v_i=(I-x_ix_i^t)w_i
        Y_i = rational_retraction(x_i,v_i) from (18)
        X = distinct_stereographic_reset(Y, delta)
      run the exact rational mean/Gram/unit/distinct verifier
      return X

Every called primitive has a polynomial bit implementation; I=O(log(T+1)). Thus the complete acquisition cost, including centroid computation, LP input assembly/solutions, fixed-height resets and final validation, is polynomial in T. The proof permits a standard exact rational LP implementation; a numerical linprog replay alone would not establish this cost or the theorem.

## Appendix A. Explicit proof of rotational L1 Bernstein

For a trigonometric polynomial f of degree <=s>=1, put alpha_k=(2k-1)pi/(2s), k=1,...,2s. The identity

    f'(t)=sum_(k=1)^(2s) w_k f(t+alpha_k),
    w_k=(-1)^(k+1)/[4s sin²(alpha_k/2)]           (26)

holds on all Fourier modes of degree <=s. Here is a finite algebraic verification. Let z_k=exp(i alpha_k), the roots of z^(2s)+1, and let A_p=sum_k z_k^p/(z_k-1)². The logarithmic derivative of z^(2s)+1 at 1 gives A_0=s-s² and sum 1/(z_k-1)=-s, hence A_1=-s². Also A_2-2A_1+A_0=2s, giving A_2=s-s². For 2<=p<=2s,

    A_(p+1)-2A_p+A_(p-1)=sum_k z_k^(p-1)=0.

Therefore A_p=s(p-s-1) for 1<=p<=2s+1. Since w_k=i z_k^(s+1)/[s(z_k-1)²], for -s<=m<=s their Fourier multiplier is i A_(s+m+1)/s=im. This proves (26). Finally sum_k csc²(alpha_k/2)=-4A_1=4s², so sum|w_k|=s. Integrating the absolute value of (26) yields ||f'||_1<=s||f||_1.

Apply (26) to the rotation restriction t->Q(rotation_a(t)x), then integrate over x using rotation invariance of sigma. This proves (3) directly on S². Every mixed coordinate rotation preserves degree, so the repeated bound used in (4) follows by induction. For degree zero both sides vanish.
