\begin{abstract}
We exhibit an explicit integer polynomial $\Psi$ in five variables, of total
degree $14$ and with constant Hessian determinant
$\det\Hess\Psi\equiv128$, whose gradient is not injective. Consequently
the formal Legendre transform of $\Psi$ is not a polynomial, and the
Hessian conjecture $\HC_5$ is false. The counterexample is obtained from the
six-variable doubling of Alp\"oge's 2026 Jacobian counterexample by a
one-variable \emph{Schur descent}---a partial Legendre transform in a
single variable---and all of its defining identities have been checked
in exact rational arithmetic. Combined with de~Bondt's theorem that
$\HC_n$ holds for $n\le3$, with the elementary doubling and
stabilization bridges relating the Jacobian conjectures $\JC_n$ to the
Hessian conjectures $\HC_n$, and with Alp\"oge's refutation of $\JC_3$,
this decides the Hessian conjecture in every dimension except $n=4$:
$\HC_n$ is true for $n\le3$, false for $n\ge5$, and open only at $n=4$.
Exactly two statements of the two families remain unsettled, $\JC_2$
and $\HC_4$, linked by $\HC_4 \Rightarrow \JC_2$. Along the way we
record, as a warm-up, an explicit six-variable counterexample to
$\HC_6$ with constant Hessian determinant $-4$ and gradient generically
three-to-one over $\C$. This paper extends and supersedes the first
author's earlier educational note \cite{MengRG2026}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==================================================================

Throughout, $\kk$ is a field of characteristic zero. For a polynomial
map $F=(F_1,\dots,F_n)\colon \kk^n\to\kk^n$ we write
$\Jac F=\det\bigl(\partial F_i/\partial x_j\bigr)$.

\begin{conjecture}[$\JC_n$, Keller \cite{Keller1939}]
If $F\colon \kk^n\to\kk^n$ is a polynomial map with
$\Jac F\in\kk^{\times}$, then $F$ is invertible, with polynomial
inverse.
\end{conjecture}

\noindent A polynomial map $F$ with $\Jac F\in\kk^{\times}$ is called a
\emph{Keller map}; thus the conjecture asserts that every Keller map has
a polynomial inverse.

The conjecture, open since 1939 in every dimension $n\ge2$ (see
\cite{BCW1982,vandenEssen2000} for surveys), was refuted in July 2026:
Alp\"oge \cite{Alpoge2026} announced an explicit polynomial map
$F\colon\C^3\to\C^3$ with $\Jac F=-2$ that is generically three-to-one.
With $u=1+x_1x_2$, the map is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Fmap}
F(x_1,x_2,x_3)=
\begin{pmatrix}
u^3x_3+x_2^2\,u\,(4+3x_1x_2)\\[2pt]
x_2+3x_1u^2x_3+3x_1x_2^2(4+3x_1x_2)\\[2pt]
2x_1-3x_1^2x_2-x_1^3x_3
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
of component degrees $7,6,4$. One checks that $\Jac F\equiv-2$, while
\begin{equation}\label{eq:collision}
F\Bigl(0,0,-\tfrac14\Bigr)
=F\Bigl(1,-\tfrac32,\tfrac{13}2\Bigr)
=F\Bigl(-1,\tfrac32,\tfrac{13}2\Bigr)
=\Bigl(-\tfrac14,\,0,\,0\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Since three distinct rational points share the same image, $\JC_3$
fails over every field of characteristic zero.

This paper has two purposes. First and foremost, we construct an
explicit counterexample to the Hessian conjecture $\HC_5$
(Section~\ref{sec:psi5}): a five-variable integer polynomial of total degree
$14$ with constant nonzero Hessian determinant and non-injective
gradient, obtained from Alp\"oge's map \eqref{eq:Fmap} by doubling
followed by a one-variable Schur descent. Second, we record the resulting status of
the two families $\JC_n$ and the closely related Hessian conjectures
$\HC_n$ introduced in \cite{Meng2006}. Together with de~Bondt's theorem
that $\HC_n$ holds for $n\le3$ \cite{deBondt2012} and two standard
bridges, the new counterexample decides $\HC_n$ in every dimension but
$n=4$, collapsing the infinite two-parameter family of open problems to
exactly two low-dimensional statements, $\JC_2$ and $\HC_4$
(Theorem~\ref{thm:status}). An earlier, single-author version recording
only the status portion, intended for educational purposes, is
available as \cite{MengRG2026}; the present paper adds the $\HC_5$
counterexample and updates the status to reflect the now-established
truth of $\HC_2$ and $\HC_3$.

\section{The Hessian conjecture and the two bridges}
%==================================================================

Let $\varphi\in\kk[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ satisfy
$\det\Hess\varphi\in\kk^{\times}$, where
$\Hess\varphi=\bigl(\partial^2\varphi/\partial x_i\partial
x_j\bigr)$. Then $\Hess\varphi(0)$ is invertible, so the gradient map
$\nabla\varphi$ admits a formal inverse at $\nabla\varphi(0)$ (after
harmless affine normalization), and one defines the \emph{formal
Legendre transform} $\varphi^{L}$ of $\varphi$ by the classical formula
$\varphi^{L}(p)=\langle p,x(p)\rangle-\varphi(x(p))$, where $x(p)$ is
the formal inverse of $p=\nabla\varphi(x)$; equivalently,
$\nabla\varphi^{L}=(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}$ as formal maps. In general
$\varphi^{L}$ is a formal power series; see \cite{Meng2006}, where a
tree formula for its coefficients is given.

\begin{conjecture}[$\HC_n$, \cite{Meng2006}]
If $\varphi\in\kk[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ has
$\det\Hess\varphi\in\kk^{\times}$, then $\varphi^{L}$ is a polynomial.
\end{conjecture}

The two conjectures are linked by two bridges. The first is immediate;
the second is the doubling construction of \cite{Meng2006} (see also
the symmetric reduction of de~Bondt--van den Essen
\cite{deBondtvandenEssen2005}, and \cite{Zhao2007} for related
formulations).

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:JCtoHC}
$\JC_n \Longrightarrow \HC_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\det\Hess\varphi\in\kk^{\times}$ then $\nabla\varphi$ is a
polynomial map with constant nonzero Jacobian determinant, so by
$\JC_n$ it has a polynomial inverse $G$. A polynomial inverse of a
gradient map is again a gradient map: $DG=(D\nabla\varphi)^{-1}\circ G$
is symmetric; since $\operatorname{char}\kk=0$, $G$ integrates to a
polynomial $\psi$ with $G=\nabla\psi$, which (up to an additive
constant) is $\varphi^{L}$. Hence $\varphi^{L}$ is a polynomial.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:HCtoJC}
$\HC_{2n} \Longrightarrow \JC_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof (sketch, following \cite{Meng2006}).]
Given $F\colon\kk^n\to\kk^n$ with $\Jac F\in\kk^{\times}$, set
$\varphi(x,y)=\langle y,F(x)\rangle\in\kk[x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n]$.
Since $\varphi$ is linear in $y$, the Hessian has the block form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:block}
\Hess\varphi=
\begin{pmatrix}
\sum_k y_k\,\Hess F_k(x) & \;(DF(x))^{T}\\[2pt]
DF(x) & 0
\end{pmatrix},
\end{equation}
and swapping the two block rows ($n$ transpositions) gives
$\det\Hess\varphi=(-1)^n(\Jac F)^2\in\kk^{\times}$. If $\HC_{2n}$
holds, $\varphi^{L}$ is a polynomial and
$\nabla\varphi^{L}=(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}$ as formal maps. Write the
arguments of $\varphi^{L}$ as $(p,q)$ dual to $(x,y)$, and set
$G(q)\coloneqq(\partial\varphi^{L}/\partial p)(0,q)$, the $x$-block of
$\nabla\varphi^{L}$ evaluated along $p=0$. Restricting the two-sided
formal identity $\nabla\varphi^{L}\circ\nabla\varphi=\mathrm{id}$ to
$y=0$ gives $G(F(x))=x$, while restricting
$\nabla\varphi\circ\nabla\varphi^{L}=\mathrm{id}$ gives $F(G(q))=q$;
thus $G$ is a two-sided polynomial inverse of $F$. Hence $\JC_n$ holds.
\end{proof}

\section{Two stabilization lemmas}
%==================================================================

Falsehood propagates upward in dimension, on both sides.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:JCstab}
If $\JC_n$ is false, then $\JC_{n+1}$ is false.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $F$ is a counterexample in dimension $n$, then
$\widetilde F=F\times\mathrm{id}$, i.e.
$\widetilde F(x,t)=(F(x),t)$, has
$\Jac\widetilde F=\Jac F\in\kk^{\times}$ and remains non-injective:
any two distinct points $P\ne Q$ with $F(P)=F(Q)$ give distinct points
$(P,0)\ne(Q,0)$ with $\widetilde F(P,0)=\widetilde F(Q,0)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:HCstab}
If $\HC_n$ is false, then $\HC_{n+1}$ is false.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\varphi\in\kk[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ be a counterexample:
$\det\Hess\varphi=c\in\kk^{\times}$ but $\varphi^{L}$ is not a
polynomial. Put
$\widetilde\varphi(x,t)=\varphi(x)+\tfrac12 t^2$. Then
$\Hess\widetilde\varphi=\Hess\varphi\oplus(1)$, so
$\det\Hess\widetilde\varphi=c$. The gradient map separates,
$\nabla\widetilde\varphi=(\nabla\varphi)\times\mathrm{id}$, hence so
does its formal inverse, and the Legendre transform separates
accordingly:
$\widetilde\varphi^{\,L}(p,s)=\varphi^{L}(p)+\tfrac12 s^2$. This is a
polynomial if and only if $\varphi^{L}$ is; by hypothesis it is not.
\end{proof}

\section{The status theorem}
%==================================================================

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:status}
Over any field of characteristic zero:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[\(\mathrm{(i)}\)] $\JC_1$ and $\HC_1$ are true; moreover $\HC_2$
and $\HC_3$ are true.
\item[\(\mathrm{(ii)}\)] $\JC_n$ is false for every $n\ge3$.
\item[\(\mathrm{(iii)}\)] $\HC_n$ is false for every $n\ge5$.
\item[\(\mathrm{(iv)}\)] Exactly two statements remain unsettled,
$\JC_2$ and $\HC_4$, linked by
\[
\HC_4 \;\Longrightarrow\; \JC_2 .
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) The one-dimensional cases are classical: $F'\in\kk^{\times}$ forces
$\deg F=1$, and $\varphi''\in\kk^{\times}$ forces $\varphi$ quadratic,
whose Legendre transform is quadratic. For the Hessian conjecture in
two and three variables, de~Bondt \cite{deBondt2012} proved that a
polynomial in at most three variables with constant nonzero Hessian
determinant has a polynomial Legendre inverse; hence $\HC_2$ and
$\HC_3$ are true.

(ii) $\JC_3$ is false by \eqref{eq:Fmap}--\eqref{eq:collision};
Lemma~\ref{lem:JCstab} propagates falsehood to all $n\ge3$.

(iii) $\HC_5$ is false by the explicit five-variable counterexample of
Section~\ref{sec:psi5} (Theorem~\ref{thm:psi5}); Lemma~\ref{lem:HCstab}
propagates falsehood to all $n\ge5$. Independently, the falsity of
$\JC_3$ gives the falsity of $\HC_6$ through
Proposition~\ref{prop:HCtoJC} (contrapositive), recovering the range
$n\ge6$.

(iv) The dimension-two Jacobian conjecture $\JC_2$ remains the
classical open case: the only implication reaching it is
$\HC_4\Rightarrow\JC_2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:HCtoJC} with $n=2$), and
$\HC_4$ is itself open, being reached neither by de~Bondt's range
$n\le3$ nor by the falsity range $n\ge5$.
\end{proof}

\medskip
\noindent
The resulting landscape:

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{c|cccc>{$}c<{$}}
$n$ & $1$ & $2$ & $3$ & $4$ & \ge 5\\
\hline
$\JC_n$ & true & \textbf{open} & false & false & \text{false}\\
$\HC_n$ & true & true & true & \textbf{open} & \text{false}\\
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{remark}
Thus exactly one Hessian statement, $\HC_4$, survives. It is the
strongest open problem of the cluster, since proving it would settle
$\JC_2$ affirmatively via $\HC_4\Rightarrow\JC_2$, whereas a
counterexample to $\JC_2$ would falsify $\HC_4$. The concentration of
the two remaining unknowns, $\JC_2$ and $\HC_4$, in the lowest
dimensions suggests a genuinely low-dimensional phenomenon.
\end{remark}

\section{An explicit counterexample to \texorpdfstring{$\HC_6$}{HC6}}
%==================================================================

Applying the doubling of Proposition~\ref{prop:HCtoJC} to the map
\eqref{eq:Fmap} yields a completely explicit counterexample to $\HC_6$
---the case $n=6$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:status}(iii), and hence every
$n\ge6$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:HCstab}; the remaining case $n=5$ is settled
in Section~\ref{sec:psi5}. Write $u=1+x_1x_2$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phi}
\begin{aligned}
\varphi(x_1,x_2,x_3,y_1,y_2,y_3)
={}& y_1\bigl(u^3x_3+x_2^2u(4+3x_1x_2)\bigr)\\
&+ y_2\bigl(x_2+3x_1u^2x_3+3x_1x_2^2(4+3x_1x_2)\bigr)\\
&+ y_3\bigl(2x_1-3x_1^2x_2-x_1^3x_3\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
a polynomial of degree $8$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:HC6}
The polynomial \eqref{eq:phi} satisfies
$\det\Hess\varphi\equiv-4$, while $\nabla\varphi$ is generically
three-to-one over $\C$; in particular $\nabla\varphi$ is not injective,
so $\varphi^{L}$ is not a polynomial and $\HC_6$ is false.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\varphi$ is linear in $(y_1,y_2,y_3)$, the lower-right
$3\times3$ block of $\Hess\varphi$ vanishes identically and
\eqref{eq:block} applies:
$\det\Hess\varphi=(-1)^3(\Jac F)^2=-(-2)^2=-4$.

For non-injectivity we exhibit three distinct points of $\kk^6$ with a
common gradient: their $x$-parts are the three colliding points of
\eqref{eq:collision}, and the $y$-parts are chosen so that the covectors
$(DF)^{T}y$ agree. With
\[
\begin{aligned}
Q_1&=\Bigl(0,\,0,\,-\tfrac14;\;1,\,0,\,0\Bigr),\\
Q_2&=\Bigl(1,\,-\tfrac32,\,\tfrac{13}2;\;-26,\,-\tfrac34,\,
\tfrac{27}{16}\Bigr),\\
Q_3&=\Bigl(-1,\,\tfrac32,\,\tfrac{13}2;\;-26,\,\tfrac34,\,
-\tfrac{27}{16}\Bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]
direct substitution gives
\[
\nabla\varphi(Q_1)=\nabla\varphi(Q_2)=\nabla\varphi(Q_3)
=\Bigl(0,\,0,\,1,\,-\tfrac14,\,0,\,0\Bigr),
\]
three distinct preimages of one point.
A non-injective polynomial map has no polynomial inverse; since
$\nabla\varphi^{L}$ would be such an inverse, $\varphi^{L}$ is not a
polynomial.

For the exact count, the block form
$\nabla\varphi(x,y)=\bigl((DF(x))^{T}y,\,F(x)\bigr)$ with
$\Jac F\in\kk^{\times}$ determines $y$ uniquely from $x$, so
$\nabla\varphi$ and $F$ share the same generic fiber cardinality over
$\C$. This cardinality is the generic three-to-one count reported for
$F$ in the announcement \cite{Alpoge2026}; taking that value as given,
$\nabla\varphi$ is generically three-to-one. We stress that only
non-injectivity---established above by three explicit preimages---is
needed for the refutation of $\HC_6$; the precise generic fiber count is
recorded for context, not used.
\end{proof}

All identities above---the constancy of $\Jac F$ and of
$\det\Hess\varphi$, the equal images in \eqref{eq:collision}, and the
three distinct points sharing one image under $\nabla\varphi$---have
been verified by exact rational arithmetic.

\section{An explicit counterexample to \texorpdfstring{$\HC_5$}{HC5}}\label{sec:psi5}
%==================================================================

The six-variable counterexample $\varphi$ of \eqref{eq:phi} has one variable
more than we would like. Every component of the map \eqref{eq:Fmap} is
of the first degree in $x_3$, so we may eliminate $x_3$ and obtain a
genuine five-variable counterexample. The elimination---a one-variable
\emph{Schur descent}, found by the second author---is
carried out, and shown to work in general, in
Appendix~\ref{app:schur}. In the main text we simply write down the
resulting polynomial and check the two properties that make it a
counterexample.

Collecting $\varphi=x_3A+B$ according to its dependence on $x_3$, and
writing $u=1+x_1x_2$ as before, gives the two polynomials
\begin{align*}
A&=y_1u^3+3x_1y_2u^2-x_1^3y_3,\\
B&=y_1x_2^2u(4+3x_1x_2)
+y_2\bigl(x_2+3x_1x_2^2(4+3x_1x_2)\bigr)
+y_3\bigl(2x_1-3x_1^2x_2\bigr)
\end{align*}
in the five variables $x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2,y_3$. The polynomial of the next
theorem is built from $A$ and $B$ alone; the appendix explains how it
arises and why the particular combination below is the right one.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:psi5}
The polynomial
\[
\Psi\;=\;A^2+11A+2B\;\in\;
\mathbb{Z}[x_1,x_2,y_1,y_2,y_3]
\]
has total degree $14$ and satisfies
\[
\det\Hess\Psi\;\equiv\;128 .
\]
Moreover the distinct rational points
\[
P_{\pm}=\Bigl(\pm1,\;\mp\tfrac32,\;-26,\;\mp\tfrac34,\;
\pm\tfrac{27}{16}\Bigr)
\]
satisfy
\[
\nabla\Psi(P_+)\;=\;\nabla\Psi(P_-)\;=\;
\Bigl(0,\,0,\,-\tfrac12,\,0,\,0\Bigr).
\]
Consequently $\Psi^{L}$ is not a polynomial, and $\HC_5$ is false.
\end{theorem}

Expanded in the monomial basis, $\Psi$ has $42$ terms. Both statements
of the theorem are plain polynomial identities in $\Psi$: one expands
$\det\Hess\Psi$ and finds the constant $128$, and one substitutes the
two points $P_\pm$ and finds the same gradient. We have checked them in
exact rational arithmetic, and the verification script is included as an
ancillary file. Because they refer to $\Psi$ only, and not to
the way $\Psi$ was found, the refutation of $\HC_5$ is unconditional.

The conclusion now follows exactly as in Proposition~\ref{prop:HC6}: the
nonzero constant $\det\Hess\Psi$ makes $\nabla\Psi$ formally invertible,
while the equal values at the distinct points $P_+$ and $P_-$ make it
non-injective, so it has no polynomial inverse; since the gradient of
$\Psi^{L}$ is that inverse, $\Psi^{L}$ is not a polynomial. Appendix~\ref{app:schur} shows
how $\Psi$ is produced from $\varphi$ and why the constants $11$ and $2$
above are the correct ones.

\section{The Schur-descent bridge in general odd dimensions}
\label{app:schur}
%==================================================================

The five-variable counterexample of Section~\ref{sec:psi5} was produced from the
six-variable doubling by a mechanism that is not special to the displayed
coefficients. We record here the two elementary lemmas underlying the
construction and the general statement they yield.

Throughout, $\kk$ is a field of characteristic zero. Suppose a potential
in the scalar variable $t$ and the block $w=(w_1,\dots,w_m)$ is
affine-linear in $t$,
\begin{equation}
\Phi(t,w)=t\,A(w)+B(w).
\label{eq:app-affine-t}
\end{equation}
Write $g=\nabla_w A$ and, for an auxiliary indeterminate $s$,
\[
M(s,w)\coloneqq\Hess_w B+s\,\Hess_w A,
\]
a matrix affine-linear in $s$ whose entries are polynomials in $s$ and
$w$; we may therefore substitute for $s$ any polynomial expression---below
the variable $t$, and later a function of $w$. With $s=t$ the lower-right
block is $\Hess_w\Phi=\Hess_w(B+tA)=M(t,w)$, so the full Hessian is the
bordered matrix
(the block $M(t,w)$ with the extra row $g^{T}$, column $g$, and corner
entry $0$)
\begin{equation}
\Hess_{t,w}\Phi=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & g^{T}\\
g & M(t,w)
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:app-bordered}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Schur descent]
\label{lem:schur}
Assume that in the situation \eqref{eq:app-affine-t}--\eqref{eq:app-bordered},
\[
\det\Hess_{t,w}\Phi=c\in\kk^{\times},
\qquad
\det M(s,w)\equiv0 ,
\]
the latter identically in $s$ and $w$.
For any $\lambda\in\kk^{\times}$ and $\mu\in\kk$ define the
$m$-variable polynomial
\begin{equation}
\psi_{\lambda,\mu}(w)=B(w)+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}A(w)^{2}+\mu A(w).
\label{eq:app-psi}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
\det\Hess\psi_{\lambda,\mu}=-\lambda c .
\label{eq:app-descent}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Expanding the bordered determinant \eqref{eq:app-bordered} along its first
row and column gives
\begin{equation}
c=-\,g^{T}\adj\!\bigl(M(s,w)\bigr)\,g,
\label{eq:app-border-adj}
\end{equation}
an identity whose right-hand side is independent of $s$ because the left-hand
side is. Differentiating \eqref{eq:app-psi} twice yields
\[
\Hess\psi_{\lambda,\mu}=M(\mu+\lambda A,\,w)+\lambda\,gg^{T}.
\]
For any square matrix $M$ the rank-one update identity
\[
\det\bigl(M+\lambda gg^{T}\bigr)=\det M+\lambda\,g^{T}\adj(M)\,g
\]
holds. Setting $s=\mu+\lambda A$ specializes the two hypotheses of the
lemma to
\[
\det M(\mu+\lambda A,w)=0,
\qquad
g^{T}\adj\!\bigl(M(\mu+\lambda A,w)\bigr)\,g=-c,
\]
the second by \eqref{eq:app-border-adj}. Applying the update identity to
$\Hess\psi_{\lambda,\mu}=M(\mu+\lambda A,w)+\lambda gg^{T}$ therefore gives
$\det\Hess\psi_{\lambda,\mu}=0+\lambda(-c)=-\lambda c$.
\end{proof}

The lemma is a one-variable partial Legendre transform in disguise. The
quadratically repaired potential
$\widehat\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}(t,w)=\Phi(t,w)-\tfrac{1}{2\lambda}(t-\mu)^{2}$
has critical equation $\partial_t\widehat\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}=0$, i.e.
$t=\mu+\lambda A(w)$, and its critical value is exactly
$\psi_{\lambda,\mu}$. Recall that the \emph{Schur complement} of an
invertible leading block $p$ in a symmetric block matrix
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p&q^{T}\\ q&N\end{smallmatrix}\right)$ is the
block $N-q\,p^{-1}q^{T}$ left on $N$ after the first row and column are
eliminated through the pivot $p$; it is the Hessian in the remaining
variables of the function obtained by solving $\partial=0$ in the
eliminated ones. Here the pivot is the scalar $(t,t)$-entry
$\partial_t^2\widehat\Phi_{\lambda,\mu}=-1/\lambda$, and the resulting
Schur complement is precisely the Hessian
$M(\mu+\lambda A,w)+\lambda gg^{T}$ of \eqref{eq:app-descent}.

\begin{lemma}[Transfer of a collision]
\label{lem:collision-transfer}
Suppose two distinct points $(t_0,w_+)\ne(t_0,w_-)$ sharing the same
$t$-coordinate satisfy
\[
\nabla\Phi(t_0,w_+)=\nabla\Phi(t_0,w_-),
\qquad
A(w_+)=A(w_-)=A_0 .
\]
(The second condition is automatic: $\partial_t\Phi=A(w)$ is a component
of $\nabla\Phi$, so equal full gradients at $(t_0,w_\pm)$ already force
$A(w_+)=A(w_-)$; we record it separately only to name the common value
$A_0$.) Choosing $\mu=t_0-\lambda A_0$, one has
\[
\nabla\psi_{\lambda,\mu}(w_+)=\nabla\psi_{\lambda,\mu}(w_-).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At either point, using $\mu+\lambda A=\mu+\lambda A_0=t_0$,
\[
\nabla_w\psi_{\lambda,\mu}
=\nabla_w B+(\mu+\lambda A)\,\nabla_w A
=\nabla_w B+t_0\,\nabla_w A
=\nabla_w\Phi(t_0,w),
\]
and the right-hand sides agree at $w_+$ and $w_-$ by hypothesis.
\end{proof}

Together the two lemmas turn any suitable doubled Keller map into a
constant-Hessian polynomial in one fewer variable with a surviving
gradient collision.

\begin{proposition}[Odd-dimensional Schur-descent bridge]
\label{prop:bridge}
Let $F\colon\kk^{n}\to\kk^{n}$ be a Keller map and
$\Phi(x,y)=\langle y,F(x)\rangle$ its doubled potential. Suppose
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $F$ is affine-linear in one source coordinate $t=x_n$; and
\item[(ii)] $\nabla\Phi$ has two distinct equal-gradient points sharing the
same $t$-coordinate.
\end{enumerate}
Then the Schur-descent construction produces a $(2n-1)$-variable polynomial
$\Psi$ with $\det\Hess\Psi\in\kk^{\times}$ constant and $\nabla\Psi$
non-injective; in particular $\Psi^{L}$ is not a polynomial.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
Write $\Phi=tA+B$ as in \eqref{eq:app-affine-t} with
$w=(x_1,\dots,x_{n-1},y_1,\dots,y_n)$. Since $F$ is a Keller map, its
doubling has $\det\Hess_{t,w}\Phi=(-1)^{n}(\Jac F)^{2}\in\kk^{\times}$
by the block form \eqref{eq:block} (Proposition~\ref{prop:HCtoJC}),
which is the constant $c$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:schur}. For every $s$
the polynomial $B+sA$ is linear in the $n$ dual variables $y_1,\dots,y_n$
while only the $n-1$ source variables $x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}$ remain; hence
in $M(s,w)=\Hess_w(B+sA)$ the $n$ rows indexed by $y_1,\dots,y_n$ are
supported entirely in the $n-1$ columns indexed by $x_1,\dots,x_{n-1}$
(their $y$--$y$ entries vanish, as $B+sA$ is linear in $y$). These $n$
rows therefore lie in an $(n-1)$-dimensional column space and are
dependent, so $M(s,w)$ is singular and $\det M(s,w)\equiv0$. Lemma~\ref{lem:schur} then yields a
$(2n-1)$-variable polynomial $\psi_{\lambda,\mu}$ with constant nonzero
Hessian determinant $-\lambda c$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:collision-transfer}
to the two equal-gradient points of hypothesis (ii), with the normalization
$\mu=t_0-\lambda A_0$, preserves the collision, so $\nabla\psi_{\lambda,\mu}$
is non-injective. A non-injective gradient map admits no polynomial inverse,
whence the formal Legendre transform $\psi_{\lambda,\mu}^{L}$ is not a
polynomial; clearing denominators gives the integral counterexample $\Psi$.
\end{proof}

For $n=3$ this is precisely the bridge of Section~\ref{sec:psi5}: taking
$F$ the doubled Jacobian counterexample \eqref{eq:Fmap}, $t=x_3$,
$\lambda=1$, and $\mu=\tfrac{11}2$, hypotheses (i)--(ii) hold by
\eqref{eq:collision}. Here $c=\det\Hess_{t,w}\Phi=-4$, so
Lemma~\ref{lem:schur} gives
$\psi_{1,11/2}=B+\tfrac12A^2+\tfrac{11}2A$ with
$\det\Hess\psi_{1,11/2}=-\lambda c=4$. Clearing the single denominator
means passing to $\Psi=2\psi_{1,11/2}=A^2+11A+2B$; since scaling a
potential in $m=5$ variables by $2$ scales its Hessian determinant by
$2^{5}$, we get $\det\Hess\Psi=2^{5}\cdot4=128$. This recovers the
five-variable counterexample $\Psi=A^2+11A+2B$ with $\det\Hess\Psi\equiv128$.
Here the value $\mu=\tfrac{11}2=\tfrac{13}2-1$ is the normalization of
Lemma~\ref{lem:collision-transfer}, dictated by the two six-variable
points $Q_2,Q_3$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:HC6}---the source of $P_\pm$
after $x_3$ is discarded---which share the eliminated coordinate
$x_3=\tfrac{13}2$ and the value $A=1$. It is the resulting
\emph{quadratic} dependence of $\Psi$ on the dual variables
$y_1,y_2,y_3$, through the term $A^2$, that supplies the nondegeneracy a
merely linear doubling such as \eqref{eq:phi} cannot.
