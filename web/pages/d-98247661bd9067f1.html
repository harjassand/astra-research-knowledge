# A junction-edit graph for evolvable allosteric designs

**Research note — 8 October 2026.** The most promising non-generic representation in this lane is a *state-conditioned junction-edit graph*: represent an allosteric chimera by its insertion site, both sensor–effector junctions, and the point/indel edits that can alter those junctions. Rank starting designs by how cheaply an input-state-dependent connection can be made from the moving sensor helix to the effector’s cooperative sector, then compute the probability of reaching a specified two-state output under a named mutation/selection protocol. This is a candidate algorithm, not a validated design method or a novelty claim.

## Mechanism and evidence

The candidate mechanism is **path access through a switchable junction**. A sensor can be folded into an effector and still fail to transmit its conformational change. Insertion tolerance is therefore a weaker design target than a mutational path to input-dependent function. A persistent, stabilizing interface can preserve the domains yet clamp the motion needed for coupling; alternatively, a junction edit can connect the sensor’s moving helix to a pre-existing cooperative network in the host.

A new bioRxiv preprint tests this distinction in LOV2–DHFR chimeras. LOV2 insertions at DHFR 115/116 (DL116) and 120/121 (DL121) are five residues apart and have similar dark-state catalytic activity. DL121 is sector-connected and already responds to light; DL116 is non-sector-connected and has no measurable starting allostery. Deep mutational scanning found nominal allostery effects for 47/1,253 viable DL116 mutants and 148/1,632 DL121 mutants. After the authors’ multiple-test correction, none of DL116’s mutants remained significant, while 52 DL121 mutants did; a label-permutation comparison gave p = 4 × 10⁻⁹. DL116 also showed little unfolding up to 80 °C. Its few nominal allostery-inducing mutations clustered near the end of LOV2’s Jα helix and the DHFR sector, which the authors interpret as possible “bridge” routes. These results support a local-accessibility hypothesis, but test only two sites in one fusion. DL116 and DL121 also start from different allosteric phenotypes, and weak bridge signals in DL116 remain below the paper’s corrected significance threshold. [Dinan et al., bioRxiv (2026)](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.735819) [preprint].

An independent 2026 primary study shows that junction **edit operators** matter. POGO-PANCE applied opposing light/dark selection, and RAMPhaGE enabled point changes plus in-frame insertions/deletions up to five residues. In a targeted AraC–LOV2 linker library, more than 90% of enriched variants changed at least one linker; a left-junction replacement of the native Ser-Gly motif by Lys made up over 40% of variants. AlphaFold3 suggested that the Lys extends a continuous helix across the junction. Transplanting the selected linker edit into WT-LOV increased dark-state activity about eight-fold and yielded more than 100-fold light control. The same edit produced constitutive activity in one already evolved background and impaired activity in another. Thus the junction is a useful search coordinate, but its effect is background-dependent; the paper does not establish a universal “helix extension” rule. [Southern et al., *Nature Communications* (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71717-0).

## Candidate algorithm and exact scope

For a fixed sensor, effector, assay, and input pair, define each genotype `g` to include the insertion cut, left/right junction sequences and lengths, and all edits in the chosen variable region. Measure the output vector `y(g)=(y_0(g),y_1(g),b(g))`, where `y_0,y_1` are outputs in the two input states and `b` is an abundance/folding control. Freeze acceptable output intervals `I_0,I_1` and a minimum `b_*`; the target set is

\[
F=\{g:y_0(g)\in I_0,\;y_1(g)\in I_1,\;b(g)\ge b_*\}.
\]

Use a two-layer representation:

1. **Structural edit prior.** From the sensor’s two input-state structures, mark its moving helix and the host’s active-site/cooperative-sector residues. Build contact graphs for each state and label contacts as persistent or state-changing. For every insertion/linker candidate, compute the minimum number of allowed junction edits needed to create a source-to-sector path that includes a state-changing contact; penalize persistent interface contacts that clamp the moving helix. Include a ProDomino-style insertion-tolerance score as a separate feature, not as the allostery score.
2. **Measured edit graph.** Build a finite genotype graph whose edges are the actual allowed coding events—point substitutions and the specified in-frame indels. On a training subset, measure the two-state outputs and estimate transition probabilities `Q(g,g')` under the declared mutation and selection protocol. Do not fill unmeasured combination scores by an additive model.

For a fully measured finite graph and a fixed lineage-level transition kernel `Q`, the chance of reaching `F` within `r` edit/selection steps is exactly computable by

\[
H_0(g)=\mathbf1[g\in F],\qquad
H_{r+1}(g)=\mathbf1[g\in F]+\mathbf1[g\notin F]\sum_{g'}Q(g,g')H_r(g'),
\]

with target nodes made absorbing. Include the current light/dark or other selection phase in the state if the protocol alternates conditions. Induction on `r` proves this recursion gives the finite-horizon hitting probability. This is a standard finite-state dynamic program, not a new theorem about protein physics. Its practical value would be to optimize **future reachability**, not only the current sequence score, while retaining measured epistasis and charging for the actual mutational operators. If population competition or recombination matters, the state must also encode the relevant population composition; the lineage-level version is then only an approximation. For unmeasured genotypes, the result is a model prediction; report `UNKNOWN` if the chosen assay graph does not cover the candidate path.

## Prior-art attack and boundary

- **Insertion tolerance is already a mature design target.** ProDomino uses ESM-2 sequence representations and was validated for insertion-site prediction and switch construction, including Cas9/Cas12a. Do not claim novelty for sequence-based insertion-site ranking or one-shot domain insertion. In the DHFR follow-up, however, ProDomino explained only 17–19% of growth-rate variance and 0.6% of allostery variance; high insertion score did not predict allosteric phenotype in that assay. [Wolf et al., *Nature Methods* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02741-z); [Dinan et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.735819) [preprint].
- **Sector-connected allosteric hotspots are prior art.** Reynolds et al. mapped surface hotspots connected to sectors in 2011. The 2026 DHFR preprint directly tests sector connection against single-mutant accessibility. “Sectors transmit allostery” and “DL121 is more evolvable than DL116” are not available novelty claims. [Reynolds et al., *Cell* (2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2011.10.024); [Dinan et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.735819) [preprint].
- **Indel-capable allosteric evolution is also prior art.** POGO-PANCE/RAMPhaGE already combines dual-state selection with targeted junction indels, tracks trajectories, and reports the AraC–LOV2 Lys/helix-extension result. A claim that indels or opposing selections improve switches would repeat that work. [Southern et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-71717-0).
- **Codon-neighborhood steering is a weaker alternative.** Cambray and Mazel’s Evolutionary Landscape Painter already recodes synonymous genes to expose different point-mutant amino-acid neighborhoods and experimentally recovered mutations unique to each encoding. A newer complete-landscape study evaluates six empirical sequence-function maps across hundreds of thousands of rewired codes. Do not present synonymous recoding alone as a new representation. [Cambray & Mazel, *PLoS Genetics* (2008)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1000256); [Rozhoňová et al., *PLOS Biology* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pbio.3002594).

The remaining candidate contribution is narrower: **prospectively rank insertion/linker backgrounds by measured-operator reachability using a state-dependent bridge/clamp representation**, then show it predicts functional paths on unseen insertion sites beyond insertion tolerance, baseline activity, stability, and simple sequence priors. The recent papers motivate this comparison but have not established that such a representation generalizes.

## Decisive experiment and prediction

Freeze at least four sensor-insertion backgrounds in one effector: two high and two low predicted bridge scores, matched as closely as possible on insertion tolerance, dark activity, and abundance. Generate equal-coverage junction libraries containing substitutions and in-frame ±1–5-aa edits. Before evolution, measure each genotype’s output in both input states, effector function, and abundance. Run replicated dual-state selections with barcodes/long reads, and compare the observed one-/two-edit target hit rate and number of viable improving paths against ProDomino-only, stability-only, PLM-only, and random-junction baselines. Keep the target criteria, mutation operator, and total library coverage equal.

**Prediction:** at matched insertion tolerance and baseline function, the bridge/clamp score predicts the fraction of one- or two-edit paths reaching the frozen two-state target better than ProDomino or stability. Low-score sites should be enriched for bridge-forming edits near the sensor helix/host sector, and persistent interface-clamp edits should reduce input-dependent output without necessarily reducing basal activity. The prediction fails if the score adds no held-out predictive value, if bridge edits do not enrich at low-score sites, or if a static sector/contact representation performs no better than insertion tolerance after matching. The POGO-PANCE LdelK background dependence makes leave-one-background-out evaluation essential.
