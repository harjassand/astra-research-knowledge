# All-input complex automorphisms and quadratic reflection count

Worker `metric_prior`, 2026-10-07. Independently reconstructed theorem, exchanged with `metric_acquisition` and independently checked by root. Historical novelty **UNKNOWN**. This is a structural result, not a polynomial-time acquisition algorithm.

## Theorem and exact scope

Let

\[
 f(y)=\sum_iq_{ii}y_i^4+2\sum_{i<j}q_{ij}y_i^2y_j^2,
 \qquad Q\in\operatorname{Sym}_n(\mathbb R),\quad Q\succ0.
\]

Every complex linear automorphism \(g\in GL_n(\mathbb C)\) with \(f(gy)=f(y)\) is monomial in these hidden axes. Thus the axis lines are preserved by the full complex stabilizer. The number of complex linear reflections preserving \(f\) is at most

\[
 n+4\binom n2=2n^2-n,
\]

and the number of real reflections is at most

\[
 n+2\binom n2=n^2.
\]

Rational reflections are a subset of the real ones. This bound is on distinct geometric reflections/axis points, not the algebraic multiplicity of any particular polar-system presentation or its intermediate elimination degrees.

For an expanded input \(f(x)=f_{hidden}(Lx)\), conjugate all groups and lines by \(L\). No metric or hidden frame is acquired during this proof; invariant metrics are used only as existence tools.

The proof needs only \(q_{ii}>0\) and \(|q_{ij}|^2<q_{ii}q_{jj}\) for every pair, rather than all of \(Q\succ0\). We state the theorem on the requested QPD subclass to avoid conflating it with existence/coercivity claims that may fail for an indefinite \(Q\).

## 1. The complex stabilizer is finite

The stabilizer \(G=\{g\in GL_n(\mathbb C):f\circ g=f\}\) is an affine algebraic group. Its Lie algebra consists of matrices \(A\) with

\[
 \nabla f(y)^TAy=0
\]

as a polynomial identity. The \(y_i^4\) coefficient gives \(q_{ii}A_{ii}=0\), hence every diagonal entry vanishes. The \(y_i^3y_j\) and \(y_iy_j^3\) coefficients give

\[
 \begin{pmatrix}q_{ii}&q_{ij}\\q_{ij}&q_{jj}\end{pmatrix}
 \binom{A_{ij}}{A_{ji}}=0.
\]

Each pair matrix is nonsingular. Thus \(A=0\). In characteristic zero the algebraic group has dimension equal to that of its Lie algebra, so \(G\) is zero dimensional and finite.

This finite-dimensional algebraic argument includes nonreal automorphisms. Real compactness alone would not establish finiteness of the complex stabilizer.

## 2. A common Hermitian metric is diagonal in the original axes

Average the standard Hermitian form over the finite group:

\[
 \langle u,v\rangle_G=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\langle gu,gv\rangle.
\]

This is positive definite and \(G\)-invariant. The hidden sign flips belong to \(G\). Invariance under each sign flip makes every off-diagonal Hermitian metric entry zero. Hence this metric has positive real diagonal entries in the **original hidden coordinates**.

Normalize those coordinates by their positive real diagonal lengths. The axes are now orthonormal, every \(g\in G\) is unitary, and \(f\) is still biquadratic with a real PD coefficient matrix: positive diagonal coordinate rescaling acts on \(Q\) by a positive diagonal congruence.

Invariant-Hermitian-form averaging is classical representation theory. Its full-group sum is a proof tool here; enumerating \(G\) is not asserted efficient, and the resulting diagonal entries need not be rational.

## 3. Complex maximum-modulus points identify the axes

For a complex unit vector \(z\), put \(m_i=|z_i|^2\), so \(m\) lies in the ordinary real probability simplex. Then

\[
 |f(z)|\le\sum_iq_{ii}m_i^2+2\sum_{i<j}|q_{ij}|m_im_j
 \le\left(\sum_i\sqrt{q_{ii}}m_i\right)^2
 \le\max_iq_{ii}.
\]

If \(m\) has at least two nonzero entries, the middle inequality is strict: at least one pair has \(m_im_j>0\), and PD gives \(|q_{ij}|<\sqrt{q_{ii}q_{jj}}\). Therefore the global maximum of \(|f|\) on the complex unit sphere is attained precisely on

\[
 \bigcup_{i:q_{ii}=\max_jq_{jj}}\{e^{it}e_i:t\in\mathbb R\}.
\]

A unitary automorphism preserves this complete maximal tier. A linear invertible map that preserves the union of these circles must permute their complex axis lines. The span of the tier is therefore invariant, and its Hermitian orthogonal complement is invariant.

Restrict \(f\) to that complement. It has the remaining axis coordinates and a PD principal coefficient matrix. The same maximum-modulus argument identifies its entire maximal tier. Induction on tiers proves that every automorphism permutes all axis lines. Hence every automorphism is monomial.

Deflating a **whole invariant tier** is essential when several diagonal values tie. Deflating one selected axis for an arbitrary automorphism would not ensure its complement is invariant.

## 4. Count and form of reflections

Let \(g\) be monomial and a linear reflection: \(g^2=I\) and \(\operatorname{rank}(I-g)=1\). Its underlying permutation is a product of fixed points and transpositions. Each transposition contributes one minus-one eigenvalue, and each diagonal minus sign contributes one. Thus exactly one of the following occurs:

1. One coordinate is negated, and all other coordinates are fixed. There are n possibilities.
2. A single pair \((i,j)\) is swapped with reciprocal scales, while all other coordinates are fixed:

\[
 y_i\mapsto t y_j,\qquad y_j\mapsto t^{-1}y_i.
\]

Preservation of the two diagonal quartic coefficients forces

\[
 t^4=q_{jj}/q_{ii}.
\]

There are at most four complex roots, and since the ratio is positive exactly two real roots. Couplings to other coordinates impose further equations, so these are upper bounds. In detail, for every \(k\notin\{i,j\}\), preservation requires

\[
 q_{jk}=t^2q_{ik},\qquad q_{ik}=t^{-2}q_{jk},
\]

which are the same equality. The \(q_{ij}\) coupling is already preserved. If t is rational the reflection is rational in hidden coordinates; after rational L conjugation it remains rational. For irrational or nonreal t it need not be rational in the input coordinates.

The bounds follow. The Fermat form \(\sum_i y_i^4\) attains both: every pair permits \(t\in\{1,-1,i,-i\}\). For n=4 this independently explains Miura–Taki2026's 28 complex quasi-Galois points and the 16 rational/real subset.

## Corollary: all promised real/rational frames identify the same axes

Suppose f has two real even representations with PD coefficient matrices. Their sign groups lie in its same finite complex stabilizer. An averaged Hermitian metric is diagonal in both real frames; since the frames are real, the metric becomes a real SPD form and both axes are orthogonal after normalization.

The maximum-modulus set described above is intrinsic to f and this common metric. Both representations must identify precisely its maximal tier of axis lines. Restrict to the common orthogonal complement and repeat. Thus the two unordered axis-line sets coincide. Scales and ordering remain unidentifiable. This gives an independent route to the sibling worker's `AXIS_IDENTIFIABILITY.md` conclusion.

The theorem proves identifiability, not acquisition cost or numerical conditioning. If an exact verifier returns some QPD even rational frame, its unordered axis lines are the promised ones. It still need not return the originally selected row scales or row order.

## Degeneracy and complexity gates

The strict pair inequalities are load-bearing. For \(Q=c\mathbf1\mathbf1^T\), \(f=c(\sum_i y_i^2)^2\), the stabilizer contains the complex orthogonal group; the Lie algebra and strict maximum argument fail. Under mere strict convexity of the simplex restriction, \((x^2-y^2)^2\) can have infinitely many rational Lorentz frames, as the sibling note exhibits. Neither boundary is a QPD counterexample.

The universal quadratic count sharpens the exhaustive polar-system baseline's **distinct final output size**. It does not prove a polynomial bit solver: cleared equations can retain unwanted components at excluded loci, nonradical scheme multiplicities can be large, and intermediate elimination varieties can be much larger than the final point set. The parent Hessian/extactic route avoids general multivariate solving but still needs its nonvanishing/bit guarantees. Its generic or exchangeable-coupling theorem is not automatically upgraded to all QPD by this result.
