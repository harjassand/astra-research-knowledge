Abstract
We show that high-accuracy guarantees for log-concave sampling—that is, iteration and query complexities which scale as  $\mathrm{poly}\log(1/\delta)$ , where  $\delta$  is the desired target accuracy—are achievable using stochastic gradients with subexponential tails.
Notably, this exhibits a separation with the problem of convex optimization, where stochasticity (even additive Gaussian noise) in the gradient oracle incurs  $\mathrm{poly}(1/\delta)$  queries.
We also give an information-theoretic argument that light-tailed stochastic gradients are necessary for high accuracy: for example, in the bounded variance case, we show that the minimax-optimal query complexity scales as  $\Theta(1/\delta)$ .
Our framework also provides similar high accuracy guarantees under stochastic zeroth order (value) queries.
1 Introduction
We study the problem of sampling from a log-concave density  $\mu\propto e^{-f}$  given access to a stochastic gradient oracle for  $f$ .
Our main result shows that if the stochastic gradients are unbiased and have light tails (e.g., subexponential), then it is possible to generate a  $\delta$ -accurate sample in total variation distance in  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  queries and time.
We refer to such a guarantee as a high-accuracy guarantee.
Recent works take inspiration from the close connections between log-concave sampling and the better-understood field of convex optimization. From that standpoint, the phenomenon we highlight here could be surprising.
Indeed, it is well-known that optimization in the presence of noisy gradients—even additive Gaussian noise—does not admit high-accuracy guarantees.
Information-theoretic lower bounds (Agarwal et al., 2012; Raginsky and Rakhlin, 2011) establish that the optimal bounds are  $1/\delta$  in the strongly convex case, and  $1/\delta^{2}$  in the weakly convex case.
On the other hand, in the literature on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), there are remarkable examples of “exact MCMC” methods in which various components of the algorithm are replaced by unbiased estimates, yet the resulting Markov chain remains geometrically ergodic toward the original stationary distribution.
For example, suppose that  $\mu$  is the marginal distribution over a parameter  $\theta$ , but there is an additional latent variable  $z$ .
In this case, the exact density can be difficult to compute, but unbiased estimators can be produced via importance sampling. Incorporating these estimators into Metropolis–Hastings algorithms leads to the class of pseudo-marginal MCMC methods (Andrieu and Roberts, 2009), some of which are exact.
When  $f$  is a finite sum of functions (e.g., negative log-likelihoods in a statistical context), there is a great need to develop samplers which make use of batched stochastic gradients, echoing the stochastic gradient revolution in machine learning.
This led to the widespread use of stochastic gradient Langevin dynamics (SGLD) (Welling and Teh, 2011); see Nemeth and Fearnhead (2021) for a survey of recent developments.
These methods are based on discretizations of diffusions and are therefore not exact, i.e., they do not admit high-accuracy guarantees.
Other works propose minibatch variants of Metropolis–Hastings methods (Seita et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2022b), leading to tailored algorithms but often without quantitative convergence guarantees.
A notable recent advance is the work of Lee et al. (2021), which developed a high-accuracy sampler for the finite sum setting.
Our interest lies in generalizing the above observations to the black-box setting, in which no particular structure for  $\mu$  is assumed except log-concavity, the starting point of most non-asymptotic analyses (Chewi, 2026), as well as generic properties of the stochastic gradient oracle.
In doing so, we aim to provide precise, non-asymptotic guarantees that incorporate recent state-of-the-art advances in sampling theory so that these guarantees are as sharp as possible.
1.1 Contributions
Our main contribution is the development of high-accuracy samplers in the presence of stochastic gradient noise, provided that the stochastic gradients have light tails (e.g., subexponential or sub-Gaussian).
As a preview of our results, suppose that the target distribution is  $\alpha$ -strongly log-concave and  $\beta$ -log-smooth.
Then, state-of-the-art guarantees for high-accuracy sampling (Chewi et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2022a; Fan et al., 2023; Altschuler and Chewi, 2024; Chen et al., 2026) have established that it is possible to draw a  $\delta$ -accurate sample in total variation distance from  $\mu\propto e^{-f}$  using
$\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\big(\kappa d^{1/2}\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)\big)\qquad\text{queries to exact oracles for}~f,~\nabla f\,,$
where  $\kappa\coloneqq\beta/\alpha$  is the condition number of  $\mu$ .
A consequence of our results is that it is in fact possible to draw a sample in
$\displaystyle\widetilde{O}\big((\kappa d^{1/2}+\sigma^{2}/\alpha)\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)\big)\qquad\text{queries to a \emph{stochastic} oracle for}~\nabla f\,,$
provided that the unbiased stochastic estimates  $g(x)$  of the gradient  $\nabla f(x)$  satisfy the subexponential tail bound  $\En\exp(\|g(x)-\nabla f(x)\|/\sigma)\leq 2$ .
We note that our main results are considerably more general, allowing for both log-concavity and log-smoothness to be relaxed, and covering noisy zeroth-order queries as well; see Section 3.3 for details.
This demonstrates a surprising robustness to gradient noise for sampling, in that the parameter  $\sigma$  appears additively in the final bound and does not significantly deteriorate the dependence on the target accuracy  $\delta$ .
As discussed above, this is a stark departure from the corresponding results in optimization, in which stochasticity quickly degrades the rates to  $\mathrm{poly}(1/\delta)$  regardless of the tail behavior.
We further remark that the work of Chatterji et al. (2022) established a lower bound of  $\Omega(\sigma^{2}/\delta^{2})$  in a certain regime, even under Gaussian additive noise.
In Section 4.2, we explain why this does not contradict our results: their lower bound example requires the strong log-concavity parameter  $\alpha$  to tend to zero with  $\delta$ ; in fact,  $\alpha\lesssim\delta^{2}$ .
Hence, our results imply that the  $\Omega(\delta^{-2})$ -scaling is in fact a consequence of the target distribution being ill-conditioned.
However, this raises the question of whether one can prove a lower bound which captures the dependence on  $\delta$ , even when  $\alpha$  remains bounded away from zero.
We resolve this question via a new lower bound that captures how the tail behavior of the stochastic gradients affects the complexity of sampling to high precision.
In particular, when we only assume that the stochastic gradients have bounded variance, our lower bound reads  $\Omega(1/\delta)$ .
This is actually attained by our upper bound algorithm in this setting, establishing that the optimal rate is  $\Theta(1/\delta)$  under a bounded variance constraint.
More generally, if we only assume that finitely many moments of the stochastic gradient are bounded, our lower bound shows that  $\Omega(1/\delta^{c})$  queries are necessary for some exponent  $c>0$ .
Taken together, our results show that light-tailed stochastic gradients are both necessary and sufficient for high-accuracy sampling.
2 Preliminaries
We first define the stochastic gradient/value oracle and its tail behavior.
Assumption 1 (Stochastic gradient oracle).
For any  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we can draw i.i.d. samples from a distribution  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(x)$  such that under  $g\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(x)$ , it holds that  $\En[g]=\nabla f(x)$ .
We assume that there is a parameter  $\mathfrak{m}_{1}>0$  such that  $\En_{g\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(x)}\|g-\nabla f(x)\|\leq\mathfrak{m}_{1}$  for any  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .
Assumption 2 (Stochastic value oracle).
For any  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we can draw i.i.d. samples from a distribution  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{eval}}(x)$  such that under  $v\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{eval}}(x)$ , it holds that  $\En[v]=f(x)$ .
For any stochastic oracle  $O$  and integer  $n\geq 1$ , we define  $O^{(n)}$  to be the batch oracle that, given input  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , returns  $y=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y^{i}$  by generating i.i.d. samples  $y^{1},\dotsc,y^{n}\sim O(x)$ .
Definition 1 (Oracle with  $\epsilon$ -tail).
Suppose that  $\epsilon=(\epsilon_{n})_{n\geq 1}$  is a sequence of decreasing functions. We say an oracle  $O$  is of  $\epsilon$ -tail if for any  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ ,  $M\geq 0$ ,  $n\geq 1$ , it holds that under  $g\sim O^{(n)}(x)$ ,
$\displaystyle\frac{1}{M}\En\big[\|g-\En[g]\|\,\mathbb{I}\{\|g-\En[g]\|>M\}\big]\leq\epsilon_{n}(M)\,.$
Some cases of interest are as follows.
Example 1 (Sub-polynomial tail).
Suppose that for some parameter  $\zeta>0$  and  $\sigma_{\sf g}>0$ , for any  $x$ , under  $g\sim O(x)$ ,  $\En\exp\big(\frac{\|g-\En[g]\|^{\zeta}}{\sigma_{\sf g}^{\zeta}}\big)\leq 2$ .
Then, we can choose  $\epsilon_{1}(M)\leq C_{\zeta}\exp(-c_{\zeta}(M/\sigma_{\sf g})^{\zeta})$  for  $M\geq\sigma_{\sf g}$ .
For  $\zeta=2$  (sub-Gaussian tail), we can choose  $\epsilon_{n}(M)\leq C_{2}\exp(-c_{2}n(M/\sigma_{\sf g})^{2})$ .
Example 2 (Polynomial tail).
Suppose that for some  $k\geq 1$  and any  $x$ ,  $\En\|g-\En[g]\|^{2k}\leq\sigma_{2k}^{2k}$ . Then we can choose  $\epsilon_{n}(M)\leq\frac{(2k)!\sigma_{2k}^{2k}}{n^{k}M^{2k}}$ .
We carry out our analysis under the following Hölder continuity assumption for  $\nabla f$ .
It interpolates between the Lipschitz case ( $s=0$ ) and the smooth case ( $s=1$ ).
Assumption 3 (Hölder continuous gradient).
There exists  $s\in[0,1]$  and  $\beta_{s}\geq 0$  such that  $\left\lVert\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\rVert\leq\beta_{s}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{s}$  for all  $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .
For technical convenience, we also state our results using an approximate proximal oracle.
Assumption 4 (Approximate proximal oracle).
Given input  $x_{0}$ , the oracle  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{prox},\eta}(x_{0})$  returns  $\widehat{x}$  such that
$\|\widehat{x}+\eta\nabla f(\widehat{x})-x_{0}\|\leq\eta\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}$ .
Alternatively, if we assume that the guarantee in the assumption holds with high probability, then our results remain unchanged up to another error term in total variation distance.
The following lemma shows that the approximate proximal oracle can be implemented using the stochastic gradient oracle.
Lemma 2.1.
Suppose that Assumption 3 holds with  $s\in[0,1]$  and denote  $m_{s}=\beta_{s}^{1/(1+s)}$ .
Suppose that  $\eta\leq\frac{1}{2m_{s}}$  and we are given access to a stochastic gradient oracle with  $\epsilon$ -tail, and that  $\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|\leq G$ .
Then, the approximate proximal oracle with  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}=10(m_{s}+M)$  can be implemented with probability at least  $1-\epsilon_{n}(M)$  using  $O(n\log(G/(m_{s}+M)))$  queries to the stochastic gradient oracle.
Notation
For  $B>0$ , we write  $\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\cdot)\coloneqq\max\{-B,\min\{B,\cdot\}\}$ . We use  $\lesssim$  and  $O(\cdot)$  to hide absolute constants, i.e.,  $f\lesssim g$  (and  $f=O(g)$ ) if there is an absolute constant such that  $f\leq Cg$ .
The notation  $\widetilde{O}(\cdot)$  hides logarithmic factors.
3 High-accuracy sampling with stochastic queries
We build up to our results in three steps.
Our methods build upon first-order rejection sampling (FORS), a meta-algorithm recently developed in Chen et al. (2026) which simulates rejection sampling given unbiased estimators of the log-density ratio between the proposal and target.
Therefore, we first review the FORS framework in Section 3.1.
Then, in Section 3.2, we instantiate FORS for the problem of sampling from a Gaussian tilt distribution, thereby showing that the results of Chen et al. (2026) are robust to stochastic gradient noise.
Finally, in Section 3.3, we combine the results of Section 3.2 with the proximal sampler algorithm (Lee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022) to establish our main results for log-concave sampling.
3.1 Background on first-order rejection sampling (FORS)
To motivate the FORS algorithm, we replicate the motivating example of Chen et al. (2026) here. Consider the simple problem of sampling from a density  $p\propto e^{-f}$ , where  $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ ,  $f(0)=0$ , and  $-1\leq f^{\prime}\leq 1$ .
In order to perform rejection sampling with the base measure  $\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ , we must generate randomness  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{-f(x)})$  for any given  $x\in[0,1]$ .
To do so, one typically assumes access to evaluations of  $f$  itself.
The novelty of FORS lies in recognizing that this is unnecessary—it suffices to produce unbiased estimators of  $f$ .
A more general version of this idea is known as the “Bernoulli factory” problem (Keane and O’Brien, 1994; Nacu and Peres, 2005), and variants of this idea can be found in multiple domains (Wagner, 1988; Papaspiliopoulos, 2011, e.g.,).
It can be stated as the following abstract task:
Task: Given i.i.d. random variables  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\dotsc$  in  $[-1,1]$ , generate a sample  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{\En W_{1}})$ .
To solve this, write the Taylor series as
$\displaystyle e^{\En W_{1}}=e^{-1}\cdot e^{\En[1+W_{1}]}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-1}}{j!}\,\bigl(\En[1+W_{1}]\bigr)^{j}\,.$
Suppose that  $J\sim\mathsf{Poisson}(2)$  is independent of the i.i.d. sequence  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\dotsc$ . Then we notice that
$\displaystyle e^{\En W_{1}}=e\En\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)\Big]\,,$
so we can set  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}\big(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)\big)$ .
Indeed,  $\mathbb{P}(b=1)=\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)=e^{-1+\En W_{1}}$ .
In summary, to generate a sample  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{-f(x)})$ , it suffices to have access to (a random number of) unbiased estimates of  $f(x)$ . In Chen et al. (2026), this was leveraged to produce high-accuracy samplers which only use queries to the derivative  $f^{\prime}$ , via the representation  $f(x)=\En_{y\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,x])}[xf^{\prime}(y)]$ .
In our work, our goal is to leverage this phenomenon in order to produce high-accuracy samplers that tolerate stochasticity in the gradient oracle.
We are now ready to state the general FORS meta-algorithm. Given a proposal distribution  $q$ , a tilt function  $w$ , and a tuneable parameter  $B=\Theta(1)$ , the goal of Algorithm 1 is to produce a sample from  $\widehat{p}(x)\propto q(x)\,e^{w(x)}$  without having access to the value  $w(x)$ . Instead, for each  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we can generate i.i.d. samples  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}\dotsc$  such that  $\En[W_{1}\mid x]=w(x)$ .
Let  $\mathcal{W}_{x}$  denote the conditional distribution of  $W_{1}$  given  $x$ .
Algorithm 1  First-order rejection sampling (FORS)
Input: Parameter  $B>0$ , proposal distribution  $q$  over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , estimator distributions  $(\mathcal{W}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}$  supported on  $[-B,B]$
for  $i=1,2,3,\dotsc$  do
Sample  $x\sim q$ .
Sample  $J\sim\mathsf{Poisson}(2B)$ .
Sample i.i.d.  $W_{1},\dotsc,W_{J}\sim\mathcal{W}_{x}$ .
Output  $x$  with probability  $\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{B+W_{j}}{2B}$ .
end for
Theorem 3.1 (FORS guarantee, Chen et al. (2026, Theorem 3.1)).
Algorithm 1 outputs a random point with density  $\widehat{p}(x)\propto q(x)\,e^{\En[W_{1}\mid x]}$ .
The number of sampled  $W_{j}$ ’s is bounded, with probability at least  $1-\delta$ , by  $3Be^{2B}\log(2/\delta)$ .
Moreover, if Algorithm 1 is called  $T$  times, then with probability at least  $1-\delta$ , the total number of sampled  $W_{j}$ ’s is  $O(Be^{2B}\,(T+\log(1/\delta)))$ .
3.2 Sampling from Gaussian tilts with stochastic queries
The goal of this section is to sample from the following Gaussian tilt distribution:
$\displaystyle\nu(x)\propto\exp\Big(-f(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\,\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}\Big)\,.$
(1)
In Section 3.3, this will be used as a subroutine for the proximal sampler algorithm (Lee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2023), leading to new guarantees for log-concave sampling.
Remark 1 (Diffusion models).
Leveraging the fact that the backward transition kernels along a diffusion model are also Gaussian tilts of the form (1), Chen et al. (2026) used FORS to provide the first high-accuracy sampling guarantees for diffusion models under minimal data assumptions.
Similarly, the results we present below could also be applied to that setting to show that diffusion sampling can be made robust to stochastic errors in the score evaluations.
For brevity, we do not pursue this application here.
We now consider instantiating FORS for the Gaussian tilt distribution (1).
Let  $\gamma_{z,r}(x)\vcentcolon=\gamma(x;z,r)$  be any path function such that  $\gamma_{z,1}(x)=x$  and  $\gamma_{z,0}(x)=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z)$  is independent of  $x$ ; here,  $z\sim P$  is an external source of randomness.
Then, by the fundamental theorem of calculus,
$\displaystyle f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P}f(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z))=\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1]),\,z\sim P}\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),\nabla f(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle\,.$
If we choose the proposal  $q=\mathsf{N}\left(\widehat{x},\eta I\right)$ , where  $\widehat{x}$  is a fixed base point, then
$\displaystyle q(x)\propto\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\lVert x-\widehat{x}\right\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\,.$
Thus, we can express
$\displaystyle\log\nu(x)-\log q(x)-\mathrm{const}$
$\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\eta}\En_{z\sim P}\langle x-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z),\widehat{x}-x_{0}\rangle-f(x)+\mathbb{E}_{z\sim P}f(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z))$
$\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1]),\,z\sim P}\Bigl\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),\,\frac{\widehat{x}-x_{0}}{\eta}-\nabla f(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\Bigr\rangle\,.$
By the guarantee of Theorem 3.1, it suggests that we use the unbiased estimator  $W_{r,z,x}\coloneqq\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),u-\nabla f(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle$ , with  $u\coloneqq(\widehat{x}-x_{0})/\eta$ .
Actually, since the  $W$ ’s in Algorithm 1 must lie in  $[-B,B]$ , we truncate the estimator to lie in this range.
Further, we replace the exact gradients by stochastic gradients, leading to
$\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{r,z,x}\coloneqq\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),u-g(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle)\,.$
Below, we choose the base point of the proposal  $\widehat{x}$ , the path function  $\gamma_{z,r}$ , and the noise distribution  $P$  in order to optimize the dimension dependence of our result.
Theorem 3.2 (Sampling from Gaussian tilts).
Suppose that Assumption 3 holds, and that  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(\cdot)$  has  $\epsilon$ -tail. Let  $M\geq\mathfrak{m}_{1}$ ,  $n\geq 1$ , and  $B=\Theta(1)$ .
Instantiate Algorithm 1 as follows:
•
$q=\mathsf{N}\left(\widehat{x},\eta I\right)$ , where  $\widehat{x}$  is drawn from  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{prox},\eta}(x_{0})$ . We write  $u\coloneqq\frac{x_{0}-\widehat{x}}{\eta}$ .
•
$\mathcal{W}_{x}$  is the law of  $\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{r,z,g,x})$ , where
$\displaystyle W_{r,z,g,x}=\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),u-g\rangle\,,\qquad r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])\,,\;z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right)\,,\;g\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}^{(n)}(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\,,$
(2)
and
$\displaystyle\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x)=a_{r}x+(1-a_{r})\widehat{x}+b_{r}z\,,\qquad a_{r}=\sin(\pi r/2)\,,~~b_{r}=\cos(\pi r/2)\,,$
(3)
so that  $\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x)=a_{r}^{\prime}(x-x_{0})+b_{r}^{\prime}z$ .
Then, conditioned on  $\|u-\nabla f(\widehat{x})\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}^{2}$ , the law  $\widehat{\nu}$  of Algorithm 1 satisfies  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\nu,\widehat{\nu}\right)\leq\delta+4\epsilon_{n}(M)$ , provided that
$\displaystyle\eta^{-1}\gg\Big(\beta_{s}^{2}d^{s}\log(1/\delta)+\frac{s\beta_{s}^{2}}{d^{1-s}}\log^{2}(1/\delta)\Big)^{1/(1+s)}+(M^{2}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}^{2})\log(1/\delta)\,.$
In our application to log-concave sampling,  $\eta$  will be interpreted as a step size, and hence the overall complexity of sampling will scale with  $\eta^{-1}$ , multiplied by the batch size  $n$  and other distribution-specific pre-factors.
We pause to give several remarks to elucidate the dependencies in this result.
Remark 2 (Dimension dependence).
The first term requires taking an inverse step size  $\eta^{-1}\gg\beta_{0}^{2}$  in the Lipschitz case ( $s=0$ ), and  $\eta^{-1}\gg\beta_{1}d^{1/2}$  in the smooth case ( $s=1$ ).
This matches state-of-the-art results for high-accuracy sampling (Fan et al., 2023; Altschuler and Chewi, 2024; Chen et al., 2026), except that we allow for stochastic gradient queries.
Remark 3 (Proximal tolerance).
Since the theorem already requires taking  $\eta^{-1}\gg\beta_{0}^{2}+M^{2}$  in the Lipschitz case, and  $\eta^{-1}\gg M^{2}$  in the smooth case, then Lemma 2.1 (with  $n=1$ ) implies that implementing the approximate proximal oracle with the stochastic gradient oracle only incurs a logarithmic overhead.
Remark 4 (Accuracy dependence).
To reach a final error of  $\delta$ , we need to take  $\eta^{-1}$  at least of order  $M^{2}=\epsilon_{n}^{-1}(\delta)^{2}$ .
We elucidate this in two cases of particular interest.
•
(Sub-Gaussian tails) Suppose that the stochastic gradients have sub-Gaussian tails, which corresponds to  $\zeta=2$  in the discussion following Definition 1.
Then, we can take  $n=1$  and  $M^{2}\asymp\sigma_{\sf g}^{2}\log(1/\delta)$ , thus the final term requires  $\eta^{-1}\gg\sigma_{\sf g}^{2}\log^{2}(1/\delta)$ .
Hence, this leads to a high-accuracy guarantee.
•
(Bounded variance) Suppose now that the stochastic gradients merely have variance bounded by  $\sigma^{2}$ .
Since  $\epsilon_{1}(M)\lesssim\sigma^{2}/M^{2}$ , we can choose  $M^{2}\asymp\sigma^{2}/\delta$ .
Thus, the dependence on  $\delta$  becomes  $\eta^{-1}\gg\delta^{-1}\log(1/\delta)$ .
Although it suffices to take  $n=1$  here, to avoid error accumulation in the next section we will eventually have to apply batching ( $n>1$ ). After doing so, the iteration complexity remains  $1/\delta$  (up to logarithmic factors).
In Section 4, we will show that the  $1/\delta$  rate is in fact optimal under the bounded variance assumption (Proposition 4.1). Thus, high-accuracy sampling requires light-tailed stochastic gradients.
Parallel to Theorem 3.2, we show that it is also possible to sample from the Gaussian tilt distribution with only stochastic value queries, provided that the error of the stochastic value oracle is sufficiently small.
Theorem 3.3.
Suppose that Assumption 3 holds, and  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{eval}}(\cdot)$  has  $\epsilon$ -tail. Suppose that  $n\geq 1$ ,  $B=\Theta(1)$  and  $\epsilon_{n}(B/3)\leq 1$ .
Instantiate Algorithm 1 as follows:
•
$q=\mathsf{N}\left(\widehat{x},\eta I\right)$ , where  $\widehat{x}$  is drawn from  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{prox},\eta}(x_{0})$ . We write  $u=\frac{x_{0}-\widehat{x}}{\eta}$ .
•
$\mathcal{W}_{x}$  is the law of  $\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{z,v,v^{\prime},x})$ , where
$\displaystyle W_{z,v,v^{\prime},x}=v^{\prime}-v-\langle u,x-z\rangle\,,\qquad z\sim q\,,\;v\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{eval}}^{(n)}(x)\,,\;v^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{eval}}^{(n)}(z)\,.$
(4)
Then, condition on  $\|u-\nabla f(\widehat{x})\|\leq\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}$ , the law  $\widehat{\nu}$  of Algorithm 1 satisfies  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\nu,\widehat{\nu}\right)\leq\delta+10\epsilon_{n}(B/3)$ , provided that
$\displaystyle\eta^{-1}\gg\Big(\beta_{s}^{2}d^{s}\log(1/\delta)+\frac{s\beta_{s}^{2}}{d^{1-s}}\log^{2}(1/\delta)\Big)^{1/(1+s)}+\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}^{2}\log(1/\delta).$
We note that in general, implementing the proximal oracle with only noisy queries will incur additional computational cost. However, with the choice of  $\widehat{x}=x_{0}$ , it is trivially guaranteed that  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}=\|\nabla f(x_{0})\|$ .
In this case, as we will see in Theorem 3.5 below, the term  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}^{2}$  in fact dominates the complexity.
3.3 Log-concave sampling
To apply our results to log-concave sampling (and beyond), we apply the results of the previous section to the proximal sampler algorithm (Lee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022).
Given a target distribution  $\mu\propto e^{-f}$ , the proximal sampler aims to sample from the augmented distribution
$\displaystyle\bar{\pi}(x,y)\propto\exp\Big(-f(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\,\|y-x\|^{2}\Big)\,.$
It does so by applying Gibbs sampling to  $\bar{\pi}$ .
Concretely, for  $n=0,1,2,\dotsc$  and an initial point  $X_{0}\sim\mu_{0}$ , repeat:
1.
Sample  $Y_{n}\sim\bar{\pi}^{Y|X=X_{n}}=\mathsf{N}\left(X_{n},\eta I\right)$ .
2.
Sample  $X_{n+1}\sim\bar{\pi}^{X|Y=Y_{n}}$ .
The distribution  $\bar{\pi}^{X|Y=y}$  is known as the restricted Gaussian oracle (RGO), and it is exactly the Gaussian tilt distribution (1) with  $x_{0}=y$ .
We therefore combine our result in Theorem 3.2 for implementing the RGO, together with existing results on the convergence of the proximal sampler itself, to deduce the following sampling corollaries.
We begin by recalling the definitions of functional inequalities.
Definition 2 (Poincaré).
A distribution  $\pi$  satisfies a Poincaré inequality (PI) with constant  $C$  if for all compactly supported and smooth test functions  $h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ ,
$\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{X\sim\pi}(h(X))\leq C\En_{X\sim\pi}[h(X)^{2}]\,.$
We let  $C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\pi)$  to be the smallest constant  $C$  such that  $\pi$  satisfies PI with constant  $C$ .
Definition 3 (Log-Sobolev).
A distribution  $\pi$  satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) with constant  $C$  if for all compactly supported and smooth test functions  $h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ ,
$\displaystyle\mathrm{Ent}_{X\sim\pi}(h^{2}(X))\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}\Big[h^{2}(X)\log\frac{h^{2}(X)}{\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\pi}[h^{2}(X)]}\Big]\leq 2C\En_{X\sim\pi}[h(X)^{2}]\,.$
We let  $C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\pi)$  to be the smallest constant  $C$  such that  $\pi$  satisfies LSI with constant  $C$ .
It is well-known (Bakry et al., 2014, e.g.,) that if  $\pi$  is  $\alpha$ -strongly log-concave (SLC), i.e.,  $-\log\pi$  is  $\alpha$ -strongly convex, then it satisfies LSI with constant  $1/\alpha$ , and if  $\pi$  satisfies LSI with constant  $1/\alpha$ , then it satisfies PI with constant  $1/\alpha$ .
These represent meaningful enlargements of the class of SLC measures which still allow for tractable sampling. For example, unlike SLC, LSI is robust to bounded perturbations of the log-density; and unlike LSI, PI allows for capturing measures without sub-Gaussian tails (e.g., the two-sided exponential).
See Chewi (2026) for further background in the context of sampling.
We now present a suite of results by combining Theorem 3.2 (Theorem 3.3) with the guarantees of the proximal sampler (Chen et al., 2022).
Let  $\phi_{M}(\delta)\coloneqq\inf\{n\geq 1:\epsilon_{n}(M)\leq\delta/10\}$ .

4 Lower bound: light tails are necessary for high accuracy sampling
4.1 A simple lower bound
We establish lower bounds for sampling with stochastic gradient queries under oracles with bounded  $\psi$ -moment.
Definition 4.
Let  $\psi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)$  be an increasing function such that  $\psi(0)=0$ . An oracle  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(\cdot)$  is a  $\psi$ -oracle for  $f$  if for any  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , under  $g\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(x)$ , it holds that  $\En[g]=\nabla f(x)$  and  $\En\psi(\|g-\nabla f(x)\|)\leq 1$ .
In the following, we present a simple information-theoretic argument based on the goal of sampling from a one-dimensional Gaussian with unit variance:  $f_{\theta}(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-\theta)^{2}$  and  $\nabla f_{\theta}(x)=x-\theta$ .
Proposition 4.1 (Lower bound).
Fix any  $\delta\in(0,1)$  and increasing function  $\psi:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)$  such that  $\psi(0)=0$ . Suppose that  $T\geq 1$ , and there is an algorithm Alg such that for any  $\theta\in\{0,\delta\}$ , given any  $\psi$ -oracle  $O$  for  $f_{\theta}$ , return a sample  $x\sim\texttt{Alg}(O)$  using  $T$  queries to  $O$  and  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{\theta},\texttt{Alg}(O)\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{10}$ . Then, it holds that
$\displaystyle T\geq\frac{1}{10}F_{\psi}(\delta)\,,\qquad F_{\psi}(\delta)\coloneqq\sup\{u\geq\delta:\delta\cdot\psi(u)\leq(1-\psi(\delta))\cdot u\}\,.$
Now, consider the case where the only assumption on  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(\cdot)$  is that the variance is bounded by 1. Then we can take  $\psi(m)=m^{2}$  and  $F_{\psi}(\delta)=\frac{1}{\delta}-\delta$ , i.e.,  $\Omega(1/\delta)$  queries are necessary for stochastic gradients with only bounded second moment.
Thus, in this case, our upper bounds are optimal (Remark 5), at least with respect to the dependence on  $\delta$ .
More generally, for  $\psi(m)=m^{s}$  with  $s>1$ , we have  $F_{\psi}(\delta)\asymp\frac{1}{\delta^{1/(s-1)}}$ . For  $\psi(m)=e^{m^{s}}-1$  with  $s>0$ , we have  $F_{\psi}(\delta)\geq\Omega(\log^{1/s}(1/\delta))$ .
4.2 Revisiting the lower bound of Chatterji et al. (2022)
Here, we discuss the lower bound of Chatterji et al. (2022), which also applies to sampling with stochastic gradients, in order to avoid potential misunderstandings.
Their main lower bound shows that there is a strongly log-concave and log-smooth distribution, with condition number  $\kappa=O(1)$ , such that it requires  $\Omega(\sigma^{2}/\delta^{2})$  queries to reach  $\delta$  error in TV distance.
Here,  $\sigma^{2}$  is the variance of the stochastic gradients.11
1
A variance bound of  $\sigma^{2}$  in our convention corresponds to a variance bound of  $\sigma^{2}d$  in theirs.
This appears to contradict our upper bound, which only requires  $O(1/\delta)$  queries in the bounded variance case.
Moreover, inspection of their lower bound reveals that it holds when the stochastic gradient oracle is produced by adding Gaussian noise; in particular, the stochastic gradients have sub-Gaussian tails.
In such a setting, we have produced algorithms whose complexity scales as  $O(\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta))$ .
To resolve this apparent contradiction, we remark that Chatterji et al. (2022, Theorem 4.1) therein requires taking the strong log-concavity parameter  $\alpha\lesssim\delta^{2}$ .
Our upper bounds, which generally incur a dependence of  $\sigma^{2}/\alpha$ , therefore match their  $\Omega(\sigma^{2}/\delta^{2})$  lower bound for their hard examples up to logarithmic terms.
However, their lower bounds do not address the question of what the best dependence on  $\delta$  is, provided that  $\alpha$  is bounded away from zero.
This is the reason why we proved Proposition 4.1.
We leave it as an open question to prove a more general lower bound which captures the dependence, not just on  $\delta$ , but on other problem parameters such as  $\kappa$  and  $d$ .
In the case of exact oracle access, proving lower bounds for sampling remains notoriously challenging, with existing results providing sharp characterizations only for Gaussians or in low dimension (Chewi et al., 2022; Chewi et al., 2023).
5 Conclusion
In this work, we have shown that high-accuracy guarantees— $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  rates—are achievable for sampling, provided that the stochastic gradients have light tails.
Moreover, via an information-theoretic argument, we have shown that light tails are necessary for such a result.
In fact, as a by-product of our analysis, we identified that the optimal dependence is  $\Theta(1/\delta)$  if the stochastic gradients are only assumed to have a bounded variance.
Several open questions remain, of which we list two: (1) Can the lower bound be extended to capture dependence on other problem parameters, such as the dimension  $d$ ? (2) Can our results yield improved results in the finite-sum setting?

Appendix A Implementation of the approximate proximal oracle
In this section, we show how to implement the approximate proximal oracle (Assumption 4) using a stochastic gradient oracle.
Proof of Lemma 2.1.
Our goal is to approximately compute
$\displaystyle\widehat{x}\approx\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\big(f(x)+\frac{1}{2\eta}\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}\big)\,.$
Let  $x^{\star}$  be an optimal solution to the minimization problem. We denote  $m_{s}=\beta_{s}^{1/(1+s)}$  assume that  $\eta\leq\frac{1}{2m_{s}}$ .
We consider the following linearized update rule: Let  $X_{0}=x_{0}$ , and
$\displaystyle X_{k+1}=\frac{X_{k}-\eta g_{k}+x_{0}}{2}\,,\quad g_{k}\sim\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}^{(n)}(X_{k})\,,\qquad\forall k\geq 0\,.$
Note that  $x^{\star}+\eta\nabla f(x^{\star})=x_{0}$ . Therefore,
$\displaystyle 2\|X_{k+1}-x^{\star}\|=$
$\displaystyle~\|X_{k}-\eta g_{k}-x^{\star}+\eta\nabla f(x^{\star})\|$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\|X_{k}-x^{\star}-\eta(\nabla f(X_{k})-\nabla f(x^{\star}))\|+\eta\|g_{k}-\nabla f(X_{k})\|\,.$
In the following we denote
$\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\coloneqq\|X_{k}-x^{\star}\|\,,\qquad E_{k}\coloneqq\|g_{k}-\nabla f(X_{k})\|\,.$
Note that by
Assumption 3,
$\displaystyle\|X_{k}-x^{\star}-\eta(\nabla f(X_{k})-\nabla f(x^{\star}))\|\leq$
$\displaystyle~\|X_{k}-x^{\star}\|+\eta\|\nabla f(X_{k})-\nabla f(x^{\star})\|$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\Delta_{k}+\eta\beta_{s}\Delta_{k}^{s}\,.$
When  $s\in(0,1)$ , we can use AM–GM inequality to derive  $\eta\beta_{s}\Delta^{s}\leq\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\eta m_{s}$ . It is also straightforward to verify this inequality holds for  $s\in\{0,1\}$ .
Then, it holds that
$\displaystyle\Delta_{k+1}\leq\frac{\frac{3}{2}\Delta_{k}+\eta(m_{s}+E_{k})}{2}\,,\qquad\forall k\geq 0\,.$
Applying this inequality recursively, we get
$\displaystyle\Delta_{k}\leq\Big(\frac{3}{4}\Big)^{k}\Delta_{0}+2\eta m_{s}+\frac{\eta}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Big(\frac{3}{4}\Big)^{i-1}E_{k-i}\,.$

$\displaystyle\En_{x,z\sim q}e^{\lambda\tau_{B}(\Delta_{x,z})}\leq\En_{Z_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right),Z_{1}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,(\pi/2)^{2}\eta I\right)}\exp\left(\lambda\tau_{B}(\lvert\langle Z_{1},\nabla f(\widehat{x}+Z_{0})-u\rangle\rvert)\right).$
Applying Lemma C.2 completes the proof.
∎
C.3 Proof of Theorem 3.4
Let  $\rho$  be the transition kernel on  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  induced by the proximal sampler, i.e.,  $X^{\prime}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid{}X)$  is generated by  $Y^{\prime}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(X,\eta I\right)$  and  $X^{\prime}\sim\pi^{X|Y=Y^{\prime}}$ . Then,  $\rho$  induces a Markov chain  $X_{0},X_{1},\cdots$  by  $X_{0}\sim\mu_{0},X_{n+1}\sim\rho(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})$  for  $n\geq 0$ . For  $n\geq 0$ , let  $\mu_{n}$  be the law of  $X_{n}$ .
Similarly, we let  $\widehat{\rho}$  be the transition with  $\pi^{X|Y=Y^{\prime}}$  implemented via Theorem 3.2, and the induced Markov chain  $X_{0},X_{1},\cdots$  is given by  $X_{0}\sim\mu_{0},X_{n+1}\sim\widehat{\rho}(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})$  for  $n\geq 0$ . For  $n\geq 0$ , let  $\widehat{\mu}_{n}$  be the law of  $X_{n}$ .
Then, by Lemma B.4 and data-processing inequality, it holds that
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{N},\mu_{N}\right)\leq D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mathbb{P}_{\widehat{\rho}},\mathbb{P}_{\rho}\right)\leq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\En_{X_{n}\sim\mu_{n}}D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\rho}(\cdot\mid{}X_{n}),\rho(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})\right).$
(11)
In all cases, we use the following error analysis. By Lemma C.4 and the fact that  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{n}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)$ , it holds that with  $G=O(\beta_{s}^{1/(1+s)}(d+\Delta+\log(N/\delta)))$ ,
$\displaystyle\max_{n\in[N]}\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu_{n}}(\|\nabla f(X_{n})\|\geq G_{\delta})\leq\frac{\delta}{10N}\,.$
Note that as long as  $\|\nabla f(X_{n})\|\leq G$ , we can implement the proximal oracle at  $X_{n}$  with  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{prox}}=10(\beta_{s}^{1/(1+s)}+M))$  and success probability at least  $1-\epsilon_{n}(M)$  by Lemma 2.1, using  $O(n\log(G/\beta_{s}^{1/(1+s)})=O(n\log A)$  queries to  $\mathsf{O}_{\mathsf{grad}}(\cdot)$ .
Therefore, by the choice of  $\eta$  (5), we can implement the RGO via Theorem 3.2 so that  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\rho}(\cdot\mid{}X_{n}),\rho(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{10N}+5\epsilon_{n}(M)$  as long as  $\|\nabla f(X_{n})\|\leq G$ , using  $O(n\log A)$  queries.
Then, by a conditioning argument, we see that
$\displaystyle\En_{X_{n}\sim\mu_{n}}D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\rho}(\cdot\mid{}X_{n}),\rho(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})\right)\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{n}}(\|f(X_{n})\|\geq G)+\frac{\delta}{10N}+5\epsilon_{n}(M)\leq\frac{\delta}{5N}+5\epsilon_{n}(M)\,.$
Therefore, taking summation over  $k=0,1,\cdots,N-1$  gives
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{N},\mu_{N}\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{5}+5N\epsilon_{n}(M).$
(12)
Finally, we can set  $n=\phi_{M}(\frac{\delta}{10N})$  so that  $\epsilon_{n}(M)\leq\frac{1}{10N}$ . Note that this implies
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\mu}_{N},\mu\right)\leq\frac{3}{4}\delta+D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mu_{N},\mu\right).$
Now, we apply results from Chen et al. (2022) to bound  $N$  such that  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mu_{N},\mu\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{4}$ :
•
LSI case. Here,  $N\asymp\frac{1}{\alpha\eta}\log\frac{D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\mu_{0}\,\|\,\pi\right)}{\delta^{2}}$ .
•
PI case. Here,  $N\asymp\frac{1}{\alpha\eta}\log\frac{D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\pi\right)}{\delta^{2}}$ .
•
LC case. Here,  $N\asymp\frac{W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\pi)}{\eta\delta^{2}}$ .
Plugging in the choice of  $\eta$  in (5) gives the desired results.
∎
Lemma C.4.
Suppose that Assumption 3 holds and  $\nu$  is a distribution such that  $\log(1+D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\nu\;\|\;{}\mu_{f}\right))\leq\Delta$ . Then it holds that for  $\delta\in(0,1)$ ,
$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\nu}\Big(\|\nabla f(X)\|^{2}\geq\frac{64\beta_{s}^{2/(1+s)}}{d^{(1-s)/(1+s)}}\,\left(\Delta+d+\log(1/\delta)\right)\Big)\leq\delta.$
Proof. We follow the proof of  Chewi (2026, Lemma 6.2.7). For any vector  $v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we bound
$\displaystyle f(x+v)-f(x)-\langle v,\nabla f(x)\rangle=\int_{0}^{1}\langle v,\nabla f(x+rv)-\nabla f(x)\rangle\,dr\leq\beta_{s}\,\|v\|^{1+s}\,,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.$
Then, we can bound
$\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f(x+v)}\,dx\geq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f(x)-\langle v,\nabla f(x)\rangle-\beta_{s}\|v\|^{1+s}}\,dx\,.$
Re-organizing gives
$\displaystyle\En_{X\sim\mu_{f}}[e^{\langle v,\nabla f(X)\rangle}]\leq e^{\beta_{s}\|v\|^{1+s}}\,,\qquad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,.$
For any  $m\geq 0$  such that  $\beta_{s}\leq\frac{d^{(1-s)/2}}{2(1+s)m^{(1+s)/2}}$ , we can take expectation over  $v\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,mI\right)$ , and then Lemma B.2 gives
$\displaystyle\En_{X\sim\mu_{f}}\exp\Big(\frac{m}{2}\|\nabla f(X)\|^{2}\Big)\leq\exp\big(2\beta_{s}(md)^{(1+s)/2}\big)\,.$
Therefore, we choose  $m>0$  such that  $m^{1+s}=\frac{d^{1-s}}{16\beta_{s}^{2}}$ , and then
$\displaystyle\En_{X\sim\nu}\exp\left(\frac{m}{4}\|\nabla f(X)\|^{2}\right)\leq$
$\displaystyle~\sqrt{(1+D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\nu\;\|\;{}\mu_{f}\right))\En_{X\sim\mu_{f}}\exp\left(\frac{m}{2}\|\nabla f(X)\|^{2}\right)}$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}\Delta+\beta_{s}(md)^{(1+s)/2}\Big)\leq\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2}(\Delta+d)\Big)\,.$
Applying Markov’s inequality gives
$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\nu}(\|\nabla f(X)\|\geq G)\leq e^{-mG^{2}/4}\En_{X\sim\nu}\exp\left(\frac{m}{4}\|\nabla f(X)\|^{2}\right)\leq\delta\,,$
as long as  $G^{2}\geq\frac{4}{m}\left(\Delta+d+\log(1/\delta)\right)$ . This the desired result.
∎

The proof is very similar to the proof of Theorem 3.4.
By Lemma C.4 and the fact that  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{n}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)$ , it holds that with  $G>0$  chosen as

it holds that
$\displaystyle\max_{n\in[N]}\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mu_{n}}(\|\nabla f(X_{n})\|\geq G_{\delta})\leq\frac{\delta}{10N}\,.$

Therefore, by the choice of  $\eta$  (6), we can implement the RGO via Theorem 3.3 so that  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\widehat{\rho}(\cdot\mid{}X_{n}),\rho(\cdot\mid{}X_{n})\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{10N}+10\epsilon_{n}(M)$  as long as  $\|\nabla f(X_{n})\|\leq G$ , using  $O(1)$  queries. D.1 Proof of Proposition 4.1
Fix any  $p\in[0,1]$  such that  $p\psi(\delta/p)+\psi(\delta)\leq 1$ . We denote  $M=\frac{\delta}{p}$ .
Consider  $\psi$ -oracle  $O_{0}$  and  $O_{\delta}$ : for any  $x\in\mathbb{R}$ ,  $O_{0}(x)$  returns  $x$  with probability 1, and  $O_{\delta}(x)$  returns  $x-M$  with probability  $p$  and returns  $x$  otherwise. Then,  $O_{0}$  is a  $\psi$ -oracle for  $f_{0}$ , and  $O_{\delta}$  is a  $\psi$ -oracle for  $f_{\delta}$ , because
$\displaystyle\En_{g\sim O_{\delta}(x)}\psi(\lvert g-\nabla f_{\delta}(x)\rvert)=p\psi(M-\delta)+(1-p)\psi(\delta)\leq 1\,.$
Note that
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(O_{0}(x),O_{\delta}(x)\right)=p\,,$
and hence by the sub-additivity of the TV distance, we have
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\texttt{Alg}(O_{0}),\texttt{Alg}(O_{\delta})\right)\leq Tp\,.$
On the other hand, by our assumption, it holds that
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{0},\texttt{Alg}(O_{0})\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{10}\,,\qquad D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{\delta},\texttt{Alg}(O_{\delta})\right)\leq\frac{\delta}{10}\,.$
An elementary calculation also yields
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{0},p_{\delta}\right)=D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mathsf{N}\left(0,1\right),\mathsf{N}\left(\delta,1\right)\right)\geq\frac{\delta}{3}\,.$
Therefore, by triangle inequality,
$\displaystyle\frac{\delta}{3}\leq D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{0},p_{\delta}\right)$
$\displaystyle\leq D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{0},\texttt{Alg}(O_{0})\right)+D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\texttt{Alg}(O_{0}),\texttt{Alg}(O_{\delta})\right)+D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{\delta},\texttt{Alg}(O_{\delta})\right)$
$\displaystyle\leq\frac{\delta}{5}+Tp\,,$
and this implies  $T\geq\frac{\delta}{10p}$ . Taking supremum over  $p\in(0,1]$  such that  $p\psi(\delta/p)\leq 1$  completes the proof.
