Abstract
    

We report a proof found by an artificial
intelligence system for the following statement: The hyperbolicity cone of every strictly hyperbolic
polynomial is spectrahedral. This establishes the generalized
Lax conjecture for the generic case, while the full conjecture remains open.

1  Introduction




1.1  Hyperbolic polynomials and the Lax conjectures




A real homogeneous polynomial hh is called
hyperbolic with respect to a vector ee if h⁡(e)>0h(e)>0 and, for
every real vector xx, the univariate polynomial t↦h⁡(x−t​e)t\mapsto h(x-te)
has only real roots.
The polynomial is strictly hyperbolic if these roots are distinct
whenever xx is not a multiple of ee.
The associated closed hyperbolicity cone is




Ce​(h)={x∈ℝn+1∣all roots of ​t↦h⁡(x−t​e)​ are nonnegative}.C_{e}(h)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid\text{all roots of }t\mapsto h(x-te)\text{ are nonnegative}\}.



Equivalently, it is the closure of the connected component of
{h>0}\{h>0\} containing ee. This cone is convex,
and every interior point can serve as a hyperbolicity direction
with the same cone [10].




For example, h⁡(x)=x02−x12−⋯−xn2h(x)=x_{0}^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-\cdots-x_{n}^{2} is hyperbolic with
respect to e=(1,0,…,0)e=(1,0,\ldots,0): the roots are
x0±x12+⋯+xn2x_{0}\pm\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}}, and its cone is the Lorentz cone
{x∣x0⩾x12+⋯+xn2}\{x\mid x_{0}\geqslant\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2}}\}.
Another fundamental example is the determinant on the space of real
symmetric or complex Hermitian matrices. The roots of det(Z−t​I)\det(Z-tI) are the eigenvalues of
ZZ, so its hyperbolicity cone is exactly the positive semidefinite cone.




A cone is spectrahedral if it can be written as




C={x∈ℝn+1∣M⁡(x)=x0​M0+⋯+xn​Mn⩾0},C=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\mid M(x)=x_{0}M_{0}+\cdots+x_{n}M_{n}\geqslant 0\},



where the MiM_{i} are fixed complex Hermitian matrices and ⩾0\geqslant 0
means positive semidefinite. Such a condition is a linear matrix
inequality, and MM is called a linear matrix pencil.
When M⁡(e)>0M(e)>0, the polynomial detM⁡(x)\det M(x) is hyperbolic with respect
to ee, and its hyperbolicity cone is precisely the set defined by this inequality.
This connection matters in optimization: minimizing a linear function
over an affine slice of a hyperbolicity cone is a hyperbolic program.
A spectrahedral representation makes it a semidefinite program,
for which matrix-based methods are available.




Lax’s original conjecture, posed in 1958 [20], concerns polynomials in
three variables. In coordinates with e=(1,0,0)e=(1,0,0) and h⁡(e)=1h(e)=1,
it asserts that every degree-dd hyperbolic form has a representation
h⁡(x)=det(x0​Id+x1​A+x2​B)h(x)=\det(x_{0}I_{d}+x_{1}A+x_{2}B) with real symmetric matrices A,BA,B.
This assertion follows from [15]; the connection with the original
formulation is explained in [21]. Alternative proofs of Lax’s original conjecture were given in [11, 12].
An elementary approach to Hermitian determinantal representations of
hyperbolic polynomials in three variables is given in [27].




For hyperbolic cubics in four variables, the existence of a definite
Hermitian determinantal representation in the smooth case is proved in
[6]. Approximation by smooth hyperbolic
cubics extends this to all hyperbolic cubics in four variables.




In more than three variables, a parameter count shows that general
hyperbolic forms of sufficiently large degree do not admit such
determinantal representations. A natural weaker question is whether
some power of the polynomial admits such a representation:




h​(x)r=detM⁡(x),M⁡(e)>0,r⩾1.h(x)^{r}=\det M(x),\qquad M(e)>0,\qquad r\geqslant 1.



Here MM is a linear matrix pencil of size r​drd if hh has degree dd.
Taking a power just repeats the roots on each line, so Ce​(hr)=Ce​(h)C_{e}(h^{r})=C_{e}(h). Thus a determinantal
representation of any positive power already gives a spectrahedral
representation of the original cone. It is nevertheless a stronger
requirement than asking only for a matrix inequality defining the cone.




There are several results concerning powers. In any number of
variables, every hyperbolic quadratic form has a positive power with
a determinantal representation
[26]. However, taking powers does not work in
general: hyperbolic polynomials for which no positive power admits a
definite determinantal representation were constructed in
[5]. The underlying matroid constructions were
extended in [7, 1], producing further examples with
this obstruction.




A complementary approach uses necessary sums-of-squares conditions.
Such conditions are derived from the parametrized Hermite matrix in
[24] and from products of directional
derivatives in [19].
These conditions are unified by the notion of sos-hyperbolicity,
which yields counterexamples in every degree
at least four and every number of variables at least four, as well as
the first cubic example, in 43 variables
[32].
A cubic example in six variables was subsequently constructed in
[2].
For these examples, failure of the necessary sums-of-squares condition
rules out definite determinantal representations of every power.




The generalized Lax conjecture now asks whether every hyperbolicity
cone is spectrahedral. It asks for a
representation of the cone, not necessarily of the given
polynomial or a power of it. The preceding counterexamples therefore
do not disprove this conjecture. Indeed, the
specialized Vámos quartic in four variables has a spectrahedral cone,
although none of its positive powers has a definite determinantal
representation [16].
One possible route is to find an additional hyperbolic
factor gg such that g​h=detMgh=\det M, M⁡(e)>0M(e)>0, and
Ce​(h)⊆Ce​(g)C_{e}(h)\subseteq C_{e}(g). The last condition is needed because
Ce​(g​h)=Ce​(h)∩Ce​(g)C_{e}(gh)=C_{e}(h)\cap C_{e}(g): the extra factor must not cut away any
part of the desired cone.
Without this last containment condition, the existence of such a
factor gg and a definite determinantal representation of g​hgh is
proved in arbitrary dimension in [17],
provided that hh is strictly hyperbolic.




The generalized Lax conjecture is known to be true for
certain families beyond the already mentioned cases. Spectrahedrality
was proved for all
elementary symmetric polynomials, using the matrix–tree theorem
[4].
These results are closely related to derivative cones [29]:
if hh is hyperbolic with respect to ee, then its directional
derivatives ∂ekh\partial_{e}^{k}h are also hyperbolic for
0⩽k<deg⁡h0\leqslant k<\deg h, and their cones form nested relaxations




Ce​(h)⊆Ce​(∂eh)⊆⋯⊆Ce​(∂ed−1h),d=deg⁡h.C_{e}(h)\subseteq C_{e}(\partial_{e}h)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq C_{e}(\partial_{e}^{d-1}h),\qquad d=\deg h.



Spectrahedrality of the first derivative cone of a product of linear
forms defining a polyhedral cone was proved in
[30]; the result extends to all higher
derivative cones of such products as well [4].
For the determinant on real symmetric matrices, an explicit
spectrahedral representation of the first derivative cone is given in
[31]. This was extended to every derivative
cone and, by restriction, to Ce​(∂ekh)C_{e}(\partial_{e}^{k}h) whenever
h=detMh=\det M for a real symmetric linear pencil with M⁡(e)>0M(e)>0
[18].
The generalized Lax conjecture was also proved for all hyperbolic cubics
in five variables in
[22]. On the other hand, even if the generalized Lax conjecture was true, we could not hope for small spectrahedral representations in general [28].




Let us also mention a weaker form of representing convex sets.
A spectrahedral shadow is a projection of a spectrahedron, so it allows for auxiliary variables.
If every nonzero boundary point of a hyperbolicity cone is a smooth point of the defining polynomial, then it is a spectrahedral shadow [25]. This proof uses results on lifted representations
of convex sets and convex hulls from
[13, 14].
This lifted conclusion was strengthened, even under the weaker
hypothesis of Nash-smooth boundary,
to a representation by second-order cones, equivalently a lifted
linear matrix inequality with blocks of size at most two
[33].




For broader introductions to convex algebraic geometry, spectrahedra,
and semidefinite representations, we refer to
[3, 23].




This paper presents a proof of the generalized Lax conjecture for all strictly hyperbolic polynomials:



Theorem 1.



The hyperbolicity cone of every strictly hyperbolic polynomial is spectrahedral.






1.2  Overview of the proof.




Our approach is closely related to the sums-of-squares approximations
in [19]. There, the hyperbolicity cone is realized as a linear slice of a cone of nonnegative polynomials and
nonnegativity is then replaced by a sum-of-squares condition.
Multiplication by powers of a fixed positive polynomial gives a sequence of inner approximations of the hyperbolicity cone by spectrahedral shadows. We use the same idea, but with a different family of
polynomials. There are two main differences. First, we work with Hermitian
biforms on the complex projective variety defined by the hyperbolic polynomial. Since the Gram matrices of Hermitian biforms are unique, the resulting inner approximations are spectrahedral rather than just spectrahedral shadows.
Furthermore, we obtain a sum-of-squares certificate of one fixed degree for every boundary point, which implies that the inner approximation becomes exact.




After the initial reductions, let hh be irreducible, write

the boundary of a compact base of CC. Note that KK can be identified with a subset of X⁡(ℝ)X(\mathbb{R}). We construct Hermitian
biforms

where the αi\alpha_{i} are real-valued and

quadratic term is positive definite on the tangent space of XX. Thus the
nonnegativity is required on the complex zero set, not just on its
real points.




In a fixed homogeneous basis
modulo hh, a Hermitian biform on X^\hat{X} has a unique coefficient
matrix, by polarization. The matrix therefore depends linearly on

the condition is that there exists a positive semidefinite Gram
matrix satisfying the coefficient equations. Projecting out these
additional variables gives a spectrahedral shadow. In our setting
there are no additional variables. Uniqueness here is an algebraic
fact; it does not follow from nonnegativity, nor does nonnegativity
alone imply that the coefficient matrix is positive semidefinite.




To construct the biforms, we first construct a continuous field of
linear functionals on XX. Near the real locus we use the Bézoutian
