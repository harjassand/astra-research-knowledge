Abstract
We address the computation of the degrees of minors of a noncommutative symbolic matrix of form
A[c]:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}t^{c_{k}}x_{k},
where A_{k} are matrices over a field \mathbb{K}, x_{i} are noncommutative variables, c_{k} are integer weights, and t is a commuting variable specifying the degree. This problem extends noncommutative Edmonds’ problem (Ivanyos et al. 2017), and can formulate various combinatorial optimization problems. Extending the study by Hirai 2018, and Hirai, Ikeda 2022, we provide novel duality theorems and polyhedral characterization for the maximum degrees of minors of A[c] of all sizes, and develop a strongly polynomial-time algorithm for computing them.
This algorithm is viewed as a unified algebraization of the classical Hungarian method for bipartite matching and the weight-splitting algorithm for linear matroid intersection.
As applications, we provide polynomial-time algorithms for weighted fractional linear matroid matching and linear optimization over rank-2 Brascamp-Lieb polytopes.
Keywords: Combinatorial optimization, noncommutative Edmonds’ problem, nc-rank, Dieudonné determinant, Euclidean building,
fractional linear matroid matching,
Brascamp-Lieb polytope.
MSC classifications: 90C27, 68Q25
1 Introduction
The theory of combinatorial optimization, particularly for well-solvable classes of combinatorial optimization problems, was founded by J. Edmonds through his groundbreaking work in the 1960s and 1970s.
In addition to the notion of a polynomial-time algorithm itself,
he invented several important concepts on it, such as good characterization
(\simeq polynomial-time verifiable min-max relation \simeq NP \cap co-NP characterization) via LP-duality and total dual integrality, and a powerful framework of polymatroids/submodular flows. With boosting of the separation-optimization equivalence [27] via the ellipsoid method,
these LP-based polyhedral methods (a.k.a. polyhedral combinatorics)
are now understood as a basic paradigm in combinatorial optimization,
and were culminated as the three-volume book [51] by Schrijver.
The present paper addresses a different direction in combinatorial optimization, which may go beyond the above paradigm of “Polyhedra and Efficiency.”
In his 1967 paper [15],
Edmonds also suggested an algebraic generalization of the bipartite matching problem,
which asks to compute the rank of a matrix A given as the following form
A=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}x_{k},
(1.1)
where A_{k} are matrices over a field \mathbb{K},
x_{k} are variables, and the rank of A is considered
in the rational function field \mathbb{K}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{k}).
This problem is sometimes called Edmonds’ problem.
A deterministic polynomial-time algorithm
for Edmonds’ problem is not known,
and is one of the major open problems in theoretical computer science.
The maximum cardinality bipartite matching problem is just the rank computation of (1.1) such that each A_{k} has exactly one nonzero entry. Other representatives of polynomially-solvable combinatorial optimization problems, such as linear matroid intersection, nonbipartite matching, and linear matroid matching, are also formulated as Edmonds’ problem for special matrices; see e.g., [25, 45].
Although min-max theorems in some of these problems were found/can be deduced via such algebraic formulation (see e.g., [51, Section 16.2b]),
it is not as well understood as the polyhedral methods.
Noncommutative Edmonds’ problem, introduced by Ivanyos, Qiao, and Subrahmanyam [34], is bringing about new algebraic understanding on combinatorial optimization.
In this setting,
x_{k} are regarded as noncommutative variables
(x_{i}x_{j}\neq x_{j}x_{i})
and A is regarded as a matrix over the noncommutative polynomial ring.
As the polynomial ring embeds into the rational function field,
the noncommutative polynomial ring
embeds into the most generic skew field of fractions,
called the free skew field [2, 11].
The rank of A over the free skew field
is called the noncommutative rank (nc-rank) of A,
which is denoted by \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A.
The nc-rank is an upper bound on the rank.
The Fortin-Rautenauer formula [16] says that nc-rank is obtained by an optimization problem over the family of vector subspaces.
For the matrices corresponding to bipartite matching, and more generally
linear matroid intersection,
the rank and the nc-rank are equal. Consequently,
the min-max formulas are deduced from the Fortin-Rautenauer formula.
This derivation is fundamentally different from the LP-based polyhedral methods. Further, the nc-rank can be computed in polynomial time [22, 28, 35]:
One is based on a vector-space generalization (the Wong sequences) of alternating paths [35], and the other two are based on geodesically convex optimization on nonpositively
curved manifolds [22] and non-manifolds [28] (symmetric spaces and Euclidean buildings).
They are beyond Euclidean convex optimization algorithms.
Edmonds’ problem for (1.1) naturally extends to a weighted version:
For an integer weight vector c=(c_{k}), we consider the following matrix
A[c]:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}t^{c_{k}}x_{k},
(1.2)
where t is a new variable.
Then, the computation of the degree \deg\det A[c] of the determinant \det of A[c] with respect to t
is an algebraic correspondent of weighted maximization in combinatorial optimization.
Actually, it captures the weighted maximization of the above-mentioned problems.
In response to this fact and the above development,
Hirai [30] formulated a noncommutative version of the deg-det computation, by using the Dieudonné determinant \mathop{\rm Det}, a determinant concept for matrices over a skew field [14].
He established a duality formula for the degree of the Dieudonné determinants, gave a simple pseudo-polynomial time algorithm (the Deg-Det algorithm)
to compute \deg\mathop{\rm Det}, and showed the (\deg\det=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}) relation
for linear matroid intersection.
Also, he noted that the Deg-Det algorithm can realize
the greedy algorithm for linear matroid
and the Hungarian method for bipartite matching.
Furue and Hirai [20] showed that it can also deduce the
weight-splitting algorithm [17] for linear matroid intersection, with a new matrix implementation.
Further, Hirai and Ikeda [31] showed that \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] can be computed in strongly polynomial time.
Their algorithm is based on cost-scaling of c and
simultaneous Diophantine approximation (the Frank-Tardos method) [18], both of which are standard techniques in combinatorial optimization.
They also gave a polyhedral interpretation of the deg-Det computation of A[c]:
It can be viewed as linear optimization over an integral polytope determined by A.
This provides a
broad class of polynomially optimizable integral polytopes,
including the perfect matching polytope of a bipartite graph and the common base polytope of two linear matroids. It also
suggests a polyhedral approach to Edmonds’ problem.
The present paper pursues this emerging direction of research,
which might be called algebraic combinatorial optimization.
The organization and results of this paper are outlined as follows.
In Section 2, we summarize previous results on nc-rank/deg-Det and their connections to combinatorial optimization.
Particularly, we introduce the Deg-Det algorithm,
which is a simple and flexible iterative algorithm using the nc-rank computation as a subroutine. It is an algorithmic as well as theoretical basis in the subsequent sections.
From Section 3,
we present the contributions of this paper.
Extending the previous study [30, 31] on the degree of determinants,
we consider the maximum degrees of subdeterminants for aiming to capture cardinality-(un)constraint maximization.
We first deal with linear symbolic rational matrices B=\sum_{k}B_{k}x_{k}, where B_{k} is a matrix over the rational function field \mathbb{K}(t).
Let \varDelta_{\ell}(B)
denote the maximum degree of Dieudonné subdeterminants of B of size \ell,
and let \varDelta_{\rm max}(B):=\max_{\ell}\varDelta_{\ell}(B).
We establish in Theorem 2.4 duality formulas for \varDelta_{\ell}(B) and \varDelta_{\rm max}(B).
The proof is based on the Deg-Det algorithm,
which yields an algorithm (Deg-SubDet)
to compute \varDelta_{\ell}(B) for all \ell at once.
Then, we focus on linear symbolic monomial matrices A[c] in (1.2), and establish sharpened duality formulas
for \varDelta_{\ell}(A[c]) and \varDelta_{\rm max}(A[c]), which link with good characterization and generalize Iwamasa’s duality theorem [36] for 2\times 2-partitioned matrices.
Extending the notion of nc-Newton polytopes in [31], we introduce
nc-independent set polytopes {\cal Q}(A) for A
and show that \varDelta_{\rm max}(A[c]) is interpreted as the optimal value of linear optimization over {\cal Q}(A) with respect to the weight vector c.
This class of polytopes includes the bipartite matching polytope, linear matroid independent set polytope, linear matroid intersection polytope, and
turns out to include the fractional matching and fractional matroid matching polytopes.
We provide a strongly polynomial-time specialization of
the Deg-Det algorithm (Hungarian Deg-Det).
This algorithm uses neither cost-scaling nor the Frank-Tardos method, and
is viewed as a dual-only-driven algebraic abstraction of the classical Hungarian method.
With an appropriate primal specification,
it can realize the original Hungarian method and the
weight-splitting algorithm for weighted linear matroid intersection.
Also, it brings a new strongly polynomial-time algorithm
for the deg-det computation on 2\times 2-partitioned matrices.
This algorithm is a concise modularized variant of
Iwamasa’s algorithm [36],
where
the unweighted algorithm in Hirai and Iwamasa [32]
is used as a subroutine.
In Section 4, we present applications.
For the linear matroid matching case,
the rank and nc-rank are not equal.
However, Oki and Soma [50] showed that the nc-rank
is equal to twice the maximum size of fractional matroid matchings.
We show its weighted extension: \varDelta_{\rm max}(A[c]) is equal to the maximum weight of fractional matroid matchings, with respect to the weight vector c.
As a consequence, the nc-independent set polytope {\cal Q}(A)
is the twice expansion of the fractional linear matroid matching polytope.
Also, our Hungarian Deg-Det algorithm brings
a new strongly polynomial-time algorithm, which is a variation
of Gijswijt and Pap [26], and can care about the bit complexity.
This result can be applied to the Brascamp-Lieb inequality [6].
It is known [4] that the parameter space of nontrivial BL-inequality forms an interesting rational polytope (BL-polytope). This polytope is defined by exponentially many inequalities and coincides with a matroid base polytope if the defining matrices are all rank-one [3].
The computational complexity on BL-polytopes
is an intriguing open problem in theoretical computer science [21].
Franks, Soma, and Goemans [19] found that the BL-polytope defined by rank-2 matrices
is equal to the perfect fractional matroid matching polytope associated with these matrices,
and showed that the membership problem for the corresponding BL-polytope is in
NP \cap co-NP. Our result implies that it is indeed in P.
A notable feature of the presented results is that they are described in a building theoretic nature.
Buildings (and Euclidean buildings) [23]
are a formalism of combinatorics on flags of vector subspaces, and are habitats of
linearly-representable matroids; see e.g., [5, Chapter 7].
Particularly, the presented min-max formulas
for \varDelta_{\ell} and \varDelta_{\rm max} are described by optimization over Euclidean buildings, and the Deg-Det algorithms
and its time complexity
are analyzed via arguments having building theory behind.
This will make waves on
the paradigm of matroid-based algorithm design
in combinatorial optimization so far.
Note that any pre-knowledge of the building theory is not required for reading this paper
as it is described in linear algebra language.
Related work and literature.
Edmonds’ problem is at a crossroad of various fields in mathematical science. In theoretical computer science, it is at the core of deterministic vs. randomization issue, particularly,
polynomial identity testing, which has been an active research field in theoretical computer science for decades.
If the underlying field is sufficiently large,
then Edmonds’ problem admits a simple randomized polynomial-time algorithm (Lovász [44]).
However, any deterministic polynomial-time algorithm is not known. Such an algorithm would imply a nontrivial circuit lower bound, which is another long-standing open problem in computational complexity theory [40].
Edmonds’ problem can also be solved by
matrix completion of finding a substitution of x_{k}
that attains the maximum rank.
The above randomized polynomial-time algorithm is based on random substitution. Deterministic polynomial-time matrix completions are known for a few classes of matrices; they are related to well-solved combinatorial optimization problems mentioned above; see e.g., [24].
In engineering, solvability and the degrees of freedom in
physical dynamical systems are analyzed
via the rank computation of symbolic matrices,
where x_{k} are physical parameters.
Motivated by this,
Murota [47]
developed the theory of mixed matrices,
a class of symbolic matrices enhanced with matroid algorithms, which is useful for such analysis on dynamical systems (DAEs), electrical networks, and related systems.
Rigidity of bar-joint frameworks in generic positions
also falls into the symbolic rank computation.
Several results (e.g., Laman’s theorem) as well as long-standing open problems (e.g., 3-dimensional generic rigidity) in rigidity theory are about combinatorial rank characterizations
of symbolic matrices arising as rigidity matrices; see [45].
Noncommutative Edmonds’ problem further broadens the literature.
As mentioned, the algorithm (IQS-algorithm) by [34, 35] for nc-rank is a vector-space generalization of the augmenting path algorithm for bipartite matching.
This suggests an algebraic generalization, or
algebraization, of combinatorial optimization.
It actually led to the study
of linear-algebraic analogues of several graph quantities by Li et al. [41, 42],
which may share a similar spirit of ours.
Noncommutative Edmonds’ problem
is also closely connected to invariant theory.
In \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C},
the nc-full-rank testing of A is equivalent to the null-cone membership (of asking whether the origin belongs to the orbit closure) of the left-right action SL_{n}(\mathbb{C})^{2}\ni(g,h)\mapsto(g^{\dagger}A_{k}h).
The first polynonial-time algorithm of nc-rank, due to Garg, Gurvits, Oliveira, Wigderson [22], is interpreted
as one minimizing the norm over an orbit closure of this action.
Subsequently, it turned out to be geodesically convex optimization on nonpositively curved manifolds, symmetric spaces of nonpositive curvature;
see e.g., [1, 8] for further developments. The algorithm by Hamada and Hirai [28] is based on
discrete convex optimization on a Euclidean building,
which is the boundary at infinity of the symmetric space;
see [29].
Its objective function has analogous properties (submodularity and L-convexity) of discrete convex functions [48], which were earlier developed on \mathbb{Z}^{n} for aiming unified understanding of well-solvable combinatorial optimization problems.
The min-max theorems presented in this paper are also described by such discrete convex functions,
L-convex functions on Euclidean buildings [30].

2 Noncommtative rank and determinant
2.1 Noncommutative rank
Here we summarize basic results of noncommutative rank.
Theorem 2.1 ([16]).
Let A be a linear symbolic matrix in (1.1).
Then \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A is equal to the optimal value of the following problem:
\displaystyle\mbox{{\rm(FR)}}\quad{\rm Min.}
\displaystyle 2n-r-s
\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}
SAT has an r\times s zero submatrix,
\displaystyle S,T\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}).
By \mathop{\rm rank}A=\mathop{\rm rank}SAT\leq 2n-r-s, nc-rank is an upper bound of rank:
\mathop{\rm rank}A\leq\mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A.
If \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A=n, we say that A is nc-nonsingular.
Theorem 2.2 ([19, 28, 34, 35]).
An optimal solution S,T in FR can be obtained in polynomial time.
For \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q},
the algorithm by Garg et al. [22] can compute the optimal value of FR
but cannot obtain an optimal solution S,T.
Recently, Franks, Soma, and Goemans [19]
modified this algorithm to obtain an optimal solution S,T.
The algorithm by Hamada and Hirai [28]
obtains optimal S,T even for a small finite field but
does not guarantee polynomial bit complexity in the intermediate computation when \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}.
Currently, the algorithm by Ivanyos, Qiao, and Subrahmanyam (the IQS-algorithm) [34, 35] is the only polynomial-time algorithm to compute an optimal solution S,T with no restriction on \mathbb{K}.
The problem FR is also written as the following vector-subspace optimization (called the Maximum Vanishing Subspace Problem in [28]):
\displaystyle\mbox{{\rm(MVSP)}}\quad{\rm Min.}
\displaystyle 2n-\dim U-\dim V
\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}
\displaystyle A_{k}(U,V)=\{0\}\quad(k\in[m]),
\displaystyle U,V\leqslant\mathbb{K}^{n},
where A_{k} is regarded as a bilinear form x,y\mapsto x^{\top}A_{k}y.
It should be noted that this problem already appeared in [45].
To see the equivalence between FR and MVSP,
consider nonsingular matrices S and T
including bases of U and V, respectively.
As observed in [28],
the problem MVSP can be viewed as submodular function minimization on
the modular lattice of vector subspaces.
Indeed, if U,V and U^{\prime},V^{\prime} are both feasible to MVSP,
then so are U\cap U^{\prime},V+V^{\prime} and U+U^{\prime},V\cap V^{\prime}.
From \dim U+\dim U^{\prime}=\dim(U\cap U^{\prime})+\dim(U+U^{\prime}),
if U,V and U^{\prime},V^{\prime} are both optimal, then
so are U\cap U^{\prime},V+V^{\prime} and U+U^{\prime},V\cap V^{\prime}.
In particular, there is a unique optimal solution U,V
such that \dim U is maximum (and \dim V is minimum).
Such an optimal solution is said to be dominant.
Accordingly, an optimal solution S,T in FR
is said to be dominant if r is maximum (and s is minimum).
The IQS-algorithm [34, 35] naturally obtains
a dominant optimal solution in (FR).
The algorithm by Franks, Soma, and Goemans [19]
also obtains a dominant optimal solution.
The nc-rank is represented as the ordinary rank of
an expanded matrix called a blow-up.
For a positive integer d,
the d-th blow-up A^{\{d\}} of A is a linear symbolic matrix defined by
A^{\{d\}}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}\otimes X_{k},
(2.1)
where \otimes denotes the Kronecker product and
X_{k}=(x_{k,ij}) is a d\times d matrix of variable entries x_{k,ij}.
We consider the (ordinary) rank of A^{\{d\}} over
the rational function field \mathbb{K}(\{x_{k,ij}\}_{k\in[m],i,j\in[d]}).
Theorem 2.3 ([34]).
\displaystyle\mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A=\max_{d=1,2,\ldots}\frac{1}{d}\mathop{\rm rank}A^{\{d\}}.
It is known [13] that the maximum in (1) is attained for any d\geq n-1.
2.2 Degree of Dieudonné determinant
Next we consider the degree of the determinant of the following matrix B=B(t):
B=\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{k}x_{k},
(2.2)
where x_{k} are variables as above and
B_{k}=B_{k}(t) are n\times n matrices
over rational function field \mathbb{K}(t) with indeterminate t.
In the commutative case, \deg\det B is the maximum degree, with respect to t,
of rational function \det B\in\mathbb{K}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})(t).
The noncommutative formulation is as follows; see [30, 31, 49] for details.
Regard the matrix B as a matrix over the rational function skew field
\mathbb{F}(t)=\{p/q\mid p\in\mathbb{F}[t],q\in\mathbb{F}[t]\setminus\{0\}\},
where \mathbb{F}:=\mathbb{K}(\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}\rangle) is the free skew field,
\mathbb{F}[t] is the skew polynomial ring over \mathbb{F},
and the degree of an element in \mathbb{F}(t) is defined similarly.
See [12, Chapter 7] for skew polynomial ring and their fractions.
Any (nonsingular) matrix B over \mathbb{F}(t) is
written as B=LDPU,
where L and U are lower-unitriangular and upper-unitriangular matrices, respectively,
P is a permutation matrix, and D is a diagonal matrix.
This decomposition is called the Bruhat normal form;
see [12, Theorem 9.2.2] and
also Lemma 3.3 below.
The Dieudonné determinant \mathop{\rm Det}B of B is defined
as the product of the sign of P and all diagonals of D modulo
the commutator subgroup of the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}(t)\setminus\{0\} [14]; see [12, Section 9.2].
We let \mathop{\rm Det}B:=0 if B is singular.
Although
\mathop{\rm Det}B is no longer an element of \mathbb{F}(t),
its degree \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B\in\mathbb{Z}\cup\{-\infty\} is well-defined
(as the degree of any commutator is zero).
We distinguish \det B and \mathop{\rm Det}B: The former stands
for the ordinary determinant of B when B is viewed as a matrix over \mathbb{K}(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n},t),
and the latter stands for the Dieudonné determinant of B
in the above formulation.
As in the ordinary determinant,
it holds \mathop{\rm Det}AB=\mathop{\rm Det}A\mathop{\rm Det}B; see [12, Chapter 11, Theorem 2.6]. Accordingly, it holds
\deg\mathop{\rm Det}AB=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}A+\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B.
(2.3)
The following property, obviously holding for \deg\det, also holds for \deg\mathop{\rm Det}; see [30].
\deg B_{ij}\leq 0\ (i,j\in[n])\ \Rightarrow\ \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B\leq 0.
(2.4)
2.2.1 Duality theorem
As an extension of the Fortin-Reutenauer theorem (Theorem 2.1),
Hirai [30] established the following duality theorem for \deg\mathop{\rm Det}:
Theorem 2.4 ([30]).
Let B=B(t) be a matrix in (2.2).
Then \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B is equal to the optimal value
of the following problem (Maximum Vanishing subModule Problem):
\displaystyle\mbox{{\rm(MVMP)}}\quad{\rm Min.}
\displaystyle-\deg\det P-\deg\det Q
\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}
\displaystyle\deg(PB_{k}Q)_{ij}\leq 0\quad(i,j\in[n],k\in[m]),
\displaystyle P,Q\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t)).
The weak duality is seen from (2.3) and (2.4) as
0\geq\deg\mathop{\rm Det}PBQ=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}P+\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B+\deg\mathop{\rm Det}Q,
(2.5)
where \mathop{\rm Det}P=\det P and \mathop{\rm Det}Q=\det Q.
The same weak duality holds for \deg\det.
Hence the theorem implies that \deg\mathop{\rm Det} is an upper bound of \deg\det:
\deg\det B\leq\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B.
We remark that this upper bounding of \deg\det already appeared in [46].
The feasibility condition of MVMP is rephrased
as the condition that PBQ is expanded
as PBQ=(PBQ)^{(0)}+(PBQ)^{(-1)}t^{-1}+\cdots.
The leading term (PBQ)^{(0)}=\sum_{k}(PB_{k}Q)^{(0)}x_{k}, viewed as a linear symbolic matrix over \mathbb{K},
plays particularly important roles.
Lemma 2.5 ([30]).
Let P,Q be a feasible solution for MVMP.
(1)
P,Q is optimal if and only
if \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}(PBQ)^{(0)}=n.
(2)
If \mathop{\rm rank}(PBQ)^{(0)}=n, then \deg\det B=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B=-\deg\det P-\deg\det Q.
The second property (2) is useful for showing \deg\det=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}
from \mathop{\rm rank}=\mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}.
As an analogue of Theorem 2.3, it holds:
Proposition 2.6 ([30, Lemma 4.5]).
\displaystyle\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B=\frac{1}{d}\max_{d=1,2,\ldots}\deg\det B^{\{d\}}.
This property follows from combining Theorem 2.3, Lemma 2.5 (2), and \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B^{\{d\}}=d\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B. Again the maximum is attained by any d\geq n-1.
2.2.2 The Deg-Det algorithm
The Deg-Det algorithm [30] is a conceptually simple algorithm to solve MVMP.
This algorithm uses an algorithm of solving FR as a subroutine, and
is viewed as a simplified version of
Murota’s combinatorial relaxation algorithm [46]
for \deg\det; see also [47, Section 7.1].
We assume that the position of
the zero submatrix in FR is in the upper-left corner,
and recall our notation, such as {\bf 1}_{k}, {\bf 1},
and (t^{\alpha}) in (1.4).
Algorithm: Deg-Det [30]
Input:
B=\sum_{k=1}^{m}B_{k}x_{k}, and
a feasible solution P,Q for MVMP.
Output:
\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B.
1:
Solve the problem FR for linear symbolic matrix (PBQ)^{(0)} to
obtain optimal S,T, where \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}(PBQ)^{(0)}=2n-r-s and
S(PBQ)^{(0)}T has an r\times s zero submatrix in the upper-left corner.
2:
If (PBQ)^{(0)} is nc-nonsingular, i.e., r+s=n,
then output \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B=-\deg\det P-\deg\det Q. Otherwise,
let (P,Q)\leftarrow((t^{{\bf 1}_{r}})SP,QT(t^{{\bf 1}_{s}-{\bf 1}})),
and go to step 1.
The algorithm works as follows:
The matrix SPAQT after step 1 has a negative degree in each entry of
its upper-left r\times s submatrix.
Multiplying t for the first r rows and t^{-1}
for the last n-s columns produces no entry of positive degree.
Thus, the next solution (P,Q):=\left((t^{{\bf 1}_{r}})SP,QT(t^{{\bf 1}_{s}-{\bf 1}})\right)
is feasible to MVMP,
and decreases the objective value by r+s-n(>0).
If the algorithm terminates, then (P,Q) is optimal by Lemma 2.5.
If the algorithm does not terminate,
then \deg\mathop{\rm Det}B=-\infty by (2.5).
The Deg-Det algorithm can incorporate the following strategies in steps 1 and 2:
Dominant-MVS strategy:
In step 1 of Deg-Det, choose optimal S,T with maximum r (and minimum s).
Long-step strategy:
In step 2 of Deg-Det, replace ({\bf 1}_{r},{\bf 1}_{s}-{\bf 1}) by
(\kappa{\bf 1}_{r},\kappa({\bf 1}_{s}-{\bf 1}))
for some integer \kappa>0 such that the next solution is feasible.
We will show that these strategies bring polynomial iteration complexity
for linear monomial matrices A[c].
2.2.3 Euclidean Building
Analogously to FR viewed as a vector-subspaces optimization MVSP,
the problem MVMP is viewed as a submodules optimization problem.
Observe that the change P,Q\to SP,QT for any S,T\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t)^{-})
keeps the feasibility and the objective value.
Namely, rather than P,Q,
the \mathbb{K}(t)^{-}-modules generated by P,Q are true variables in MVMP.
Particularly, MVMP is an optimization
over (the product of) the families of full-rank free \mathbb{K}(t)^{-}-modules11
1
Such a module is called a lattice in the literature. in \mathbb{K}(t)^{n}.
These modules form an abstract simplicial complex (by inclusion relation),
which is known as the Euclidean building for GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t));
see e.g., [23, Chapter 19].
In this view,
the Deg-Det algorithm minimizes the objective function by tracing the 1-skeleton of the building, where an adjacent vertex is uniquely determined by
optimal solution U,V of MVSP
at the current vertex (the modules generated by P,Q); see [30].
Therefore, when adopting the dominant-MVS strategy,
the minimizing trajectory in the building produced by Deg-Det
is uniquely determined, though S,T in each step is not unique.
2.2.4 Linear symbolic monomial matrix
For a matrix A=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}x_{k} in (1.1)
and an integer vector c\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}, as in (1.2),
consider a linear symbolic monomial matrix
A[c]:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}A_{k}x_{k}t^{c_{k}}.
We see in Section 2.3 below that
this class of matrices captures the weighted maximization
of several combinatorial optimization problems,
where c plays the role of a cost vector.
A natural question from computational complexity viewpoint
is whether one can compute \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] in time polynomial dependence of c.
The Deg-Det algorithm above runs in time pseudo-polynomial in c.
Hirai and Ikeda [31] addressed this question.
They incorporated cost-scaling,
a standard technique in combinatorial optimization,
with the Deg-Det algorithm, and
obtained a polynomial time algorithm for \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c]:
Theorem 2.7 ([31]).
Let A be a matrix in (1.1) and let c\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}.
(1)
Suppose that arithmetic operations
over \mathbb{K} are performed in constant time.
Then \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] can be computed in time polynomial of
n,m, and \log C, where C:=\max_{k}|c_{k}|.
(2)
Suppose that \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q} and that each A_{k} consists of integer entries whose absolute values are at most D>0.
Then \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] can be computed in time polynomial of
n,m,\log C, and \log D.
The second result (2) is based on the modulo-p reduction method by Iwata and Kobayashi [37] devised for weighted linear matroid matching problem.
As will be mentioned in more detail in Section 3,
\deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] is viewed as a linear optimization
over the polytope of the nonzero terms of \det A^{\{d\}}\in\mathbb{Z}[x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{m}]
for d\geq n-1. This fact can be seen from Proposition 2.6.
Then, an optimal nonzero-term is also
an optimal nonzero-term \deg\det A^{\{d\}} modulo p for large prime p.
By using this fact, the problem is reduced to symbolic matrices A\bmod p
over the finite field GF(p)
for a polynomial number of primes p with polynomial bit-length.
Hirai and Ikeda [31] also showed that the \log C dependency can be removed
by the Frank-Tardos method [18],
a general procedure rounding a weight vector c to have a bit-complexity polynomial of
the problem dimension, which is also a standard technique
to obtain strongly polynomial time algorithms
in combinatorial optimization.
In Section 3,
we develop
a more natural and fast primal-dual type strongly polynomial-time
algorithm to compute \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c], and more generally,
the maximum degrees of minors of all sizes.
2.3 Combinatorial optimization
In this section, we explain the interactions of the above results with combinatorial optimization, which is the main theme of this paper.
2.3.1 Bipartite matching
Let G=(U\sqcup V,E) be a bipartite graph with two color classes U,V and edge set E.
Suppose (for simplicity) that U=V=[n].
Define a linear symbolic matrix A (Edmonds matrix by
A:=\sum_{ij\in E}e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}x_{ij},
(2.6)
where e_{i} denotes the i-th unit vector in \mathbb{K}^{n}.
As is well-known, \mathop{\rm rank}A is equal to the maximum size of a matching of G.
It is also equal to \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A.
To see this, observe that matrices S,T in FR
can be taken as permutation matrices
(i.e., vector subspaces U,V in MVSP can be taken as coordinate subspaces).
Then, \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A is equal to the maximum of 2n-|S| over all stable sets S in G.
By the Kőnig-Egerváry theorem, this quantity equals the maximum number of a matching.
This means \mathop{\rm rank}A=\mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A. In addition, the IQS-algorithm (with 0,1 substitution) can realize the classical augmenting path algorithm.
Suppose that G has an integer edge-weight c:E\to\mathbb{Z}.
Then \deg\det A[c] is nothing but the maximum weight
of a perfect matching of G.
From the unweighted case and Lemma 2.5 (2),
it holds \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c]=\deg\det A[c].
MVMP in Theorem 2.4 becomes the LP-dual
of the perfect matching polytope, which can be seen
from the (block-)diagonal property of a solution P,Q of MVMP; see Section 2.3.4 below.
Further, the Deg-Det algorithm with the long-step and dominant MVS-strategies can realize the Hungarian method; see [30, Section 5.1].
2.3.2 Linear matroid intersection
Let {\bf M}_{1} and {\bf M}_{2} be two linear matroids represented
by vectors a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{m} and b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m} in \mathbb{K}^{n}, respectively.
Define a linear symbolic matrix A by
A:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}x_{k}.
(2.7)
Then \mathop{\rm rank}A is equal to the maximum size of a common independent set of the two matroids {\bf M}_{1} and {\bf M}_{2}.
The feasibility condition of MVSP is: U^{\bot}\ni a_{k} or V^{\bot}\ni b_{k} for k\in[m].
(U^{\bot},V^{\bot}) can be chosen as
(\mathop{\rm span}\{a_{k}\}_{k\in I},\mathop{\rm span}\{b_{k}\}_{k\in[m]\setminus I}) for I\subseteq[m],
and the objective value is r_{1}(I)+r_{2}([m]\setminus{I}), where r_{i} is the rank function of {\bf M}_{i}.
Namely, \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A equals \min_{I\subseteq[m]}r_{1}(I)+r_{2}([m]\setminus{I}).
By the matroid intersection theorem (see [51, Theorem 41.1]),
it equals \mathop{\rm rank}A.
Thus, the rank and nc-rank are the same.
Again, the IQS-algorithm can realize the matroid intersection algorithm by Edmonds.
The weighted extension goes in a similar manner as bipartite matching.
Given a weight c:\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{Z}, \deg\det A[c] is equal to the maximum weight of a common base of the two matroids. Again, from the unweighted case and Lemma 2.5 (2), it holds \deg\det A[c]=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c]. Transforming MVMP to the LP-dual
of a linear optimization over the common base polytope is also possible but requires a little effort.
Furue and Hirai [20]
showed that the Deg-Det algorithm
with the two strategies realizes the weight-splitting algorithm [17] with
a new matrix implementation.
2.3.3 Nonbipartite matching and linear matroid matching
A representative example where the rank and nc-rank differ is the Tutte matrix of a nonbipartite graph G=([n],E). The Tutte matrix A_{G} of G is a linear symbolic matrix
A=\sum_{ij\in E}(e_{i}{e_{j}^{\top}}-e_{j}e_{i}^{\top})x_{ij}.
(2.8)
The maximum matching number of G equals (1/2)\mathop{\rm rank}A.
The rank of the Tutte matrix of K_{3} is 2,
whereas the nc-rank is 3. So the rank and nc-rank differ.
Interestingly, the nc-rank of A
still has a natural combinatorial interpretation: It equals
twice the fractional matching number of G.
This fact was recently revealed by Oki and Soma [50].
They further revealed that this relation is
generalized for matroid matching.
A matroid matching for
a collection {\cal H}=\{H_{k}\}_{k\in[m]} of 2-dimensional subspaces in \mathbb{K}^{n}
is a subset I\subseteq[m] with 2|I|=\dim\sum_{k\in I}H_{k}.
It is known [45] that
the maximum cardinality of a matroid matching is equal to twice the rank of
A_{\cal H}:=\sum_{k\in[m]}(a_{k}{b_{k}^{\top}}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})x_{k},
(2.9)
where a_{k},b_{k} is any basis of subspace H_{k}
for k\in[m].
Oki and Soma showed
that the nc-rank of A_{\cal H} is equal to twice the maximum fractional matroid matching number. In Section 4.1,
we study this linear symbolic matrix A_{\cal H} in more detail
and establish an analogous relation
for \deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c].
Note that
\deg\det A_{\cal H}[c]
is equal to twice the maximum weight of a matroid matching, and that
a polynomial-time algorithm
for weighted linear matroid matching, found recently
by Iwata and Kobayashi [37],
is based on a related deg-det formulation.
2.3.4 Mixed matrix and partitioned matrix
A mixed matrix (Murota [47])
is the sum Q+T of a matrix Q over \mathbb{K} and the Edmonds matrix T for a bipartite graph G,
which is also viewed as a linear symbolic matrix A=Qx_{0}+\sum_{ij\in E}E_{ij}x_{ij}, where E_{ij}=e_{i}e_{j}^{\top}.
From Murota’s formula [47, Section 4.2],
its rank is interpreted as the optimal value of MVSP.
Thus the rank and nc-rank are the same.
A mixed polynomial/rational matrix [47]
is Q(t)x_{0}+\sum_{ij\in E}E_{ij}x_{ij}t^{c_{ij}},
where Q(t) is a polynomial/rational over \mathbb{K}
and c:E\to\mathbb{Z} is a weight on E.
Via Lemma 2.5 (2), \deg\det
and \deg\mathop{\rm Det} are equal.
These classes of matrices and their rank/deg-det computation are motivated
by numeric-symbolic integrated analysis on dynamical systems,
enhanced with methods of combinatorial optimization.
A related engineering-motivated class of matrices is (generic) partitioned matrices (Ito, Iwata, and Murota [33]), which are
linear symbolic matrices of form:
A=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}A_{11}x_{11}&A_{12}x_{12}&\cdots&A_{1n}x_{1n}\\
A_{21}x_{21}&A_{22}x_{22}&\cdots&A_{2n}x_{2n}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
A_{n1}x_{n1}&A_{n2}x_{n2}&\cdots&A_{nn}x_{nn}\end{array}\right),
(2.10)
where A_{ij} is an n_{i}\times m_{j} matrix over \mathbb{K} for i,j\in[n].
In fact, (nc-)rank computation of any linear symbolic matrix reduces to a matrix of this form; see [30, Appendix].
An important feature of a partitioned matrix is:
There is an optimal solution S,T in FR that is block-diagonalized as
\displaystyle S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}S_{1}&&&\\
&S_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&S_{n}\end{array}\right),\quad T=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}T_{1}&&&\\
&T_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&T_{n}\end{array}\right),
where S_{i}\in GL_{n_{i}}(\mathbb{K}) and
T_{j}\in GL_{m_{j}}(\mathbb{K}) for i,j\in[n].
Observe that the Edmonds matrix in (2.6) is
the special case where each submatrix A_{ij} is 1\times 1.
Iwata and Murota [38] studied
the next case where
each submatrix A_{ij} is 2\times 2.
They proved a formula (good characterization) of the rank, which
turned out to be equal to the Fortin-Rautenauer formula (Theorem 2.1).
Hence the rank and nc-rank are the same for 2\times 2 partitioned matrices.
Along with the development of the nc-rank theory,
Iwamasa and Hirai [32]
took up the rank-computation of 2\times 2 partitioned matrices
as a 2-dimensional generalization of bipartite matching.
They showed that the rank equals
the maximum of certain algebraically-constraint 2-matchings
in the bipartite graph of the nonzero block pattern of A,
and sharpened the IQS-algorithm to develop
a combinatorial polynomial-time augmenting path algorithm
to compute a maximum matching.
The deg-det
computation for weighted 2\times 2 partitioned matrices A[c], the maximum weight matching problem in this setting, are addressed by Hirai and Ikeda [31] and Iwamasa [36],
where \deg\det A[c]=\deg\mathop{\rm Det}A[c] holds again from Lemma 2.5 (2).
The former [31] provided a cost-scaling polynomial-time algorithm using the unweighted algorithm of [32] as a subroutine, which is a direct application of Theorem 2.7.
The latter [36] provided a strongly polynomial-time primal-dual algorithm to
compute the maximum degrees of minors of all sizes, and obtained algorithmically
a new min-max theorem (good characterization) for the maximum degrees of minors.
In Section 3, we extend his min-max formula for general linear monomial matrices,
and develop a unified strongly polynomial-time primal-dual framework.
3 Maximum degrees of subdeterminants
In this section, we present an improved analysis and results
for the deg-Det computation.
In addition to \deg\det and \deg\mathop{\rm Det},
we consider the maximum degrees of subdeterminants.
Let B be a matrix in (2.2).
For \ell\in[n], define the maximum degrees over \ell\times\ell subdeterminants of B by
\displaystyle\delta_{\ell}(B)
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\max\{\deg\det B[I,J]\mid I,J\subseteq[n]:|I|=|J|=\ell\},
\displaystyle\varDelta_{\ell}(B)
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\max\{\deg\mathop{\rm Det}B[I,J]\mid I,J\subseteq[n]:|I|=|J|=\ell\},
where B[I,J] denote the submatrix of B having row set I and column set J.
It is basic (see e.g., [47, section 5.1.2 (5.5)]) that \delta_{\ell}(B)=\delta_{\ell}(PBQ) holds for any biproper P,Q.
This property holds for \varDelta_{\ell}:
\varDelta_{\ell}(B)=\varDelta_{\ell}(PBQ)\quad(P,Q\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t)^{-})).
(3.1)
See [30, Proposition 2.9].
For convention, we define the degree of the determinant of an empty matrix by \delta_{0}(B)=\varDelta_{0}(B):=0.
Let \delta_{\rm max}(B) and \varDelta_{\rm max}(B)
denote the maximum degrees of all possible subdeterminants:
\displaystyle\delta_{\rm max}(B)
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\max_{\ell\in[0,n]}\delta_{\ell}(B),
\displaystyle\varDelta_{\rm max}(B)
\displaystyle:=
\displaystyle\max_{\ell\in[0,n]}\varDelta_{\ell}(B).
The correspondents in combinatorial optimization
are clear: \delta_{\ell} and \Delta_{\ell}
represent maximum weights under the cardinality constraint,
whereas \delta_{\rm max} and \Delta_{\rm max} represent
unconstraint maximum weights.
We introduce additional notation.
For a subset D\subseteq\mathbb{R},
let D^{n}_{\downarrow} denote the set of vectors \alpha\in D^{n} with \alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{n}.
Similarly, D^{n}_{\uparrow} denotes the set of vectors \alpha\in D^{n} with \alpha_{1}\leq\alpha_{2}\leq\cdots\leq\alpha_{n}.
A zero matrix is denoted by O and by O_{n,m}
if it has n rows and m columns.
The arguments in this section are
adapted to rectangular matrices B
(by e.g., filling zeros to make B being a square matrix).

3.1.2 Analysis on the Deg-Det algorithm
We start with two preliminary lemmas.
The first one is a partial commutative property
between (t^{\alpha}) and triangular matrices.
The proof is straightforward.
Lemma 3.2.
Let U\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t)^{-}) be upper-unitriangular biproper, and let \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}_{\downarrow}. Then
U(t^{\alpha})=(t^{\alpha})\tilde{U},
where \tilde{U}:=(t^{-\alpha})U(t^{\alpha})\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}(t)^{-}) is upper-unitriangular biproper.
The second one is a version of LU-decomposition,
called the Bruhat decomposition.
We identify a permutation \pi:[n]\to[n] with a permutation matrix \sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}e^{\top}_{\pi(i)}.
Lemma 3.3 (Bruhat decomposition).
Any nonsingular matrix S\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}) can be decomposed as
S=L\pi_{S}U
for permutation matrix \pi_{S}, lower-triangular matrix L, and upper-triangular matrix U, where \pi_{S} is uniquely determined.
For completeness, we give a proof based on [12, Theorem 9.2.2], which works on a matrix over any skew field and can deduce the Bruhat normal form.
Proof.
For i=1,2,\ldots,n in order,
choose the smallest index \pi(i):=j\in[n] with S_{ij}\neq 0.
Add scalar multiples of the i-th row and j-th column to later rows and columns
to make S_{i^{\prime}j}=S_{ij^{\prime}}=0 for all (i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\neq(i,j).
By the nonsingularity of S, this is actually possible, and \pi is a permutation on [n].
This procedure
corresponds to multiplying to S a lower-triangular matrix L from the left
and an upper-triangular matrix U from the right.
The resulting matrix is \pi D, where D is a diagonal matrix. Namely LSU=\pi D,
and we obtain desired decomposition S=L^{-1}\pi(DU^{-1}).
To show the uniqueness, it suffices to show that
L\pi^{\prime}U=\pi implies \pi^{\prime}=\pi.
This is immediate from the following observation:
If \pi^{\prime}_{ij}=1, the multiplication
of a lower/upper-triangular matrix from left/right to \pi^{\prime}
does not affect the (i,j)-entry (since \pi^{\prime}_{i^{\prime}j}=\pi^{\prime}_{ij^{\prime}}=0 for i^{\prime}<i, j^{\prime}<j).
Namely, \pi_{ij}^{\prime}=\pi_{ij}=1, implying \pi^{\prime}=\pi.
∎
Lemma 3.4.
(1)
In step 1 of Deg-Det, optimal S,T can be chosen in the form
S=\pi_{S}U,\ T=M\pi_{T},
(3.4)
where U is upper-triangular and M is lower-triangular.
(2)
In addition, if \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}(PBQ)^{(0)}=\ell
and the lower-right \ell\times\ell submatrix of (PBQ)^{(0)} is nc-nonsingular,
then the above S,T can be chosen as
\pi_{S}[n-\ell]=[n-\ell],\ \pi_{T}[n-\ell]=[n-\ell],
(3.5)
and the lower-right \ell\times\ell submatrix of ((t^{{\bf 1}_{r}})SPBQT(t^{{\bf 1}_{s}-{\bf 1}}))^{(0)} is nc-nonsingular.
Proof.
Let S,T be an optimal solution in step 1.
Consider the Bruhat decomposition S=L\pi_{S}U and T=M\pi_{T}V, where L,M are lower-triangular and U,V are upper-trianglar.
Let A:=(PBQ)^{(0)}.
Then SAT has an r\times s zero submatrix in upper-left corner,

We claim:
Claim.
\mathop{\rm rank}S[[r],[n-\ell]]=\mathop{\rm rank}T[[n-\ell],[s]]=n-\ell.
Proof.
Let a:=\mathop{\rm rank}S[[r],[n-\ell]] and b:=T[[n-\ell],[s]]. By adding n-\ell\geq a and n-\ell\geq b (with \ell=2n-r-s), we have
2(r+s-n)\geq a+b.
By row elimination within the first r rows,
we may assume S to satisfy S[[r-a],[n-\ell]]=O.
Similarly, we may assume T[[n-\ell],[s-b]]=O.
Let S^{*}:=S[[r-a],[n]\setminus[n-\ell]], T^{*}:=T[[n]\setminus[n-\ell],[s-b]], and

which is nc-nonsingular by the assumption.
From S[[r],[n]]AT[[n],[s]]=O, we have
S^{*}A^{*}T^{*}=O_{r-a,s-b}.
Since A^{*} has nc-rank \ell, the sum (r-a)+(s-b)

Remark 3.11 (Spherical building).
Again, MVMP{}_{\ell}^{\rm F} and MVMP{}_{\ell}^{\rm A} have a natural interpretation in the theory of building.

for GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}).

The fractional matroid matching polytope FMP({\cal H}) for {\cal H} is the polytope consisting of all fractional matroid matchings.
Suppose that for each k\in[m], the subspace
H_{k} is spanned by vectors a_{k},b_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}.
As in (2.9),
define linear symbolic matrix A_{\cal H}:=\sum_{k=1}^{m}(a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})x_{k}.
As mentioned in Section 2.3.3,
rank and nc-rank differ for this class of matrices.
However, Oki and Soma [50]
showed that \mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A_{\cal H} still has an interesting interpretation—it is equal to twice the maximum of a fractional matroid matching:
Theorem 4.1 ([50]).
\mathop{\rm nc\mbox{-}rank}A_{\cal H}=2\max\{{\bf 1}^{\top}y\mid y\in FMP({\cal H})\}.
This also gives an interpretation to nc-rank
of matrix (2.8) as fractional matching number.
We here establish a weighted generalization of this relation as follows:
Theorem 4.2.
Let c\in\mathbb{Z}^{m} and let \ell\in[n]. Then it holds
\varDelta_{\ell}(A_{\cal H}[c])=2\max\{c^{\top}y\mid y\in FMP({\cal H}),2{\bf 1}^{\top}y=\ell\}.
In particular, it holds that {\cal Q}(A_{\cal H})=2FMP({\cal H}) and {\cal Q}_{n}(A_{\cal H})=2PFMP({\cal H}).
The proof is given in Section 4.1.2.
4.1.1 Algorithmic consequence
By Theorem 4.2,
the weighted fractional matching problem can be solved by the presented framework (Hungarian-Deg-Det) for \varDelta_{\ell}, which requires O(n^{2}) calls
of a dominant-MVS computation subroutine.
The resulting algorithm is viewed as a variant of
the algorithm by Gijswijt and Pap [26] (in the linear matroid setting).
Their algorithm calls, in each iteration,
the unweighted algorithm (CLV-algorithm)
by Chang, Llewellyn, and Vande Vate [9, 10]
to find a dominant 2-cover for the associated unweighted problem, and performs a dual update.
As shown in [50],
the dominant 2-cover for
a fractional matroid matching instance {\cal H}^{\prime}
coincides with
the dominant-MVS in FR for A_{{\cal H}^{\prime}}.
A naive adaptation of Deg-Det
does not keep
the leading term ((t^{\alpha})PA_{\cal H}Q(t^{\beta}))^{(0)} skew-symmetric, and is unable to use
the CLV-algorithm for finding the dominant-MVS.
This is possible by using, via Lemma 3.10,
a symmetric formulation of MVMP{}_{\ell}^{\mathbb{K}}:
\displaystyle{\rm Min.}
\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=n-\ell+1}^{n}\alpha_{i}
\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}
\displaystyle\deg((t^{\alpha})PA_{\cal H}[c]P^{\top}(t^{\alpha}))_{ij}\leq 0\quad(i,j\in[n]),
\displaystyle\alpha\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{\downarrow},P\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}).
Then the leading term ((t^{\alpha})PA_{\cal H}P^{\top}(t^{\alpha}))^{(0)} is skew-symmetric, and
the dominant-MVS (U,V) can be obtained by the CLV-algorithm.
It necessarily satisfies U\geqslant V.
Let S\in GL_{n}(\mathbb{K}) include bases of U and V
as row subsets X and Y, respectively, where X\supseteq Y.
Then S,S^{\top} is a
dominant optimal solution of FR, and is taken to be block-diagonal relative to \alpha.
Accordingly, the feasible solution (t^{\alpha})P is updated in a symmetric way:
(t^{\alpha})P\to(t^{\alpha+\kappa((1/2){\bf 1}_{X}+(1/2)({\bf 1}_{Y}-{\bf 1})})SP,
where step size \kappa can be rational.
The proof arguments for Hungarian Deg-Det
can be adapted for the symmetric version.
Actually, the proof relies on the partition structure induced by \alpha,\beta,
not the integrality of \alpha,\beta.
Further, one can verify that Lemmas 3.4 and 3.5 and the arguments of Section 3.1.3 can be adapted for this setting.
Then, the resulting algorithm solves the weighted linear matroid matching in O(n^{2}) calls of the CLV-algorithm,
and has the same complexity as and looks quite similar to [26] (although we could not see the complete coincidence).
It is an interesting future research
to incorporate the CLV-algorithm into
a primal-dual framework in Section 3.2.4, which will improve the time complexity greatly.
It is expected that an extreme fractional matching matching
will work as a primal solution and its characterization in [10] will help.
4.1.2 Proof of Theorem 4.2
By Proposition 3.13, it suffices to show {\cal Q}(A_{\cal H})=2FMP({\cal H}).
We show an equivalent relation \varDelta_{\rm max}(A[c])=2\max\{c^{\top}y\mid y\in FMP({\cal H})\}.
By Theorem 3.8 and Lemma 3.10, \varDelta_{\rm max}(A[c]) is given by
\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Min.}\quad&2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi_{i}\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&\xi_{i}+\xi_{j}\geq c_{k}\quad(i,j\in[n],k\in[m]:(a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})\neq\{0\}),\\
&\xi\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{+\uparrow},\ \{U_{i}\}:\text{complete flags in $\mathbb{K}^{n}$}.\end{split}
(4.1)
We show that this problem reduces to the LP-dual of \max\{2c^{\top}y\mid y\in FMP({\cal H})\}.
For a vector subspace U\subseteq\mathbb{K}^{n}, let U^{\bot}
denote the orthogonal complement of U with
respect to bilinear form (x,y)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}.
Lemma 4.3.
For i\leq j,
it holds (a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})\neq\{0\} if and only if \dim H_{k}\cap U_{i}^{\bot}\leq 1 and \dim H_{k}\cap U_{j}^{\bot}=0.
Proof.
From the expression a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top}=(a_{k}\ b_{k}){0\ 1\choose-1\ 0}(a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}, we observe that (a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})=\{0\} if
(a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}U_{i} and (a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}U_{j} are the same 1-dimensional space.
Then,
(a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})\neq\{0\} if and only if \dim\,(a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}U_{i}\geq 1
and \dim\,(a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}U_{j}=2.
From the relation \dim\,(a_{k}\ b_{k})^{\top}U=2-\dim H_{k}\cap U^{\bot}, we have the claim.
∎
For i\in[n], define \lambda_{i}\geq 0 by
\lambda_{i}:=\xi_{i}-\xi_{i-1},
where we let \xi_{0}:=0.
Lemma 4.4.
For k\in[m], the constraints \xi_{i}+\xi_{j}\geq c_{k} for i,j\in[n] with
(a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})\neq\{0\} are written as a single constraint
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\dim H_{k}\cap U_{i-1}^{\bot}\geq c_{k}.
Proof.
Let i^{*} and j^{*} denote the first indices with \dim H_{k}\cap U_{i}^{\bot}=1 and \dim H_{k}\cap U_{j}^{\bot}=0, respectively.
By the previous lemma, for i\leq j,
(a_{k}b_{k}^{\top}-b_{k}a_{k}^{\top})(U_{i},U_{j})\neq\{0\}
if and only if i^{*}\leq i and j^{*}\leq j.
As \xi\in\mathbb{Q}_{\uparrow}^{n},
the constraints in question are written as one constraint \xi_{i^{*}}+\xi_{j^{*}}\geq c_{k}.
Since \dim H_{k}\cap U_{i}^{\bot}=2 for i<i^{*},
\dim H_{k}\cap U_{i}^{\bot}=1 for i^{*}\leq i<j^{*},
and \dim H_{k}\cap U_{i}^{\bot}=0 for j^{*}<i.
we have \xi_{i^{*}}+\xi_{j^{*}}=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{*}}\lambda_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{j^{*}}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{i^{*}}2\lambda_{i}+\sum_{i=i^{*}+1}^{j^{*}}\lambda_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\dim H_{k}\cap U_{i-1}^{\bot}, as required.
∎
By using \lambda, the objective function is written as
2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{i})=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-i+1)\lambda_{i}=2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\dim U_{i-1}^{\bot}.
For i\in[n], define X_{i}:=U_{i-1}^{\bot}.
Then our problem is rewritten as
\displaystyle{\rm Min.}
\displaystyle 2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\dim X_{i}
\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}\dim X_{i}\cap H_{k}\geq c_{k}\quad(k\in[m]),
\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{n}=X_{1}>X_{2}>\cdots>X_{n}>\{0\},\ \lambda\in\mathbb{Q}^{n}_{+}.
Consider the following relaxation, which is an infinite linear program:
\displaystyle\begin{split}{\rm Min.}\quad&2\sum_{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}\lambda(X)\dim X\\
{\rm s.t.}\quad&\sum_{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}\lambda(X)\dim X\cap H_{k}\geq c_{k}\quad(k\in[m]),\\
\quad&\lambda:{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\to\mathbb{Q}_{+},|\{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\mid\lambda(X)>0\}|<\infty,\end{split}
(4.2)
where {\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}) denotes the family of all vector subspaces of \mathbb{K}^{n}.
This relaxation is tight. To see this, we use the standard argument of uncrossing.
A function f:{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\to\mathbb{Q} is called submodular if f(X)+f(Y)\geq f(X\cap Y)+f(X+Y) for every X,Y\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}). In addition, it is called strict if the inequality is strict for every X,Y with X\not\leqslant Y and Y\not\leqslant X.
Lemma 4.5.
(1)
The function X\mapsto-\dim X\cap H is submodular for any H\leqslant\mathbb{K}^{n}.
(2)
The function X\mapsto\dim X\dim X^{\bot} is strict submodular.
Proof.
(1) follows from \dim X\cap H+\dim Y\cap H=\dim X\cap Y\cap H+\dim(X\cap H)+(Y\cap H)\leq\dim(X\cap Y)\cap H+\dim(X+Y)\cap H.
(2) follows from:
For X,Y\leqslant\mathbb{K}^{n}, we can choose basis \{u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n}\} of \mathbb{K}^{n}
such that X=\mathop{\rm span}\{u_{i}\}_{i\in I},
Y=\mathop{\rm span}\{u_{i}\}_{i\in J},
X+Y=\mathop{\rm span}\{u_{i}\}_{i\in I\cup J},
and X\cap Y=\mathop{\rm span}\{u_{i}\}_{i\in I\cap J} for some I,J\subseteq[n].
Then the strict submodularity reduces to
the well-known fact that I\mapsto|I||[n]\setminus I| for I\subseteq[n] is (usual)
strict submodular.
∎
Now consider a solution \lambda of (4.2), where
K denotes the common denominator of nonzero rationals \lambda(X).
Suppose that there are X,Y\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}) such that
\lambda(Y)>0, \lambda(X)>0, X\not\leqslant Y, and Y\not\leqslant X.
Then, for \epsilon:=\min(\lambda(X),\lambda(Y)), replace \lambda(X):=\lambda(X)-\epsilon,
\lambda(Y):=\lambda(Y)-\epsilon,
\lambda(X+Y):=\lambda(X+Y)+\epsilon, and \lambda(X\cap Y):=\lambda(X\cap Y)+\epsilon.
By submodularity (1), the resulting \lambda is feasible (with common denominator K).
By modularity of X\mapsto\dim X, the objective value does not change.
By strict submodularity (2),
the (nonnegative) quantity \sum_{X}\lambda(X)\dim X\dim X^{\bot}
strictly decreases at least by 1/K.
By repeating this in finite many times,
we obtain a solution \lambda keeping the objective value so that the nonzero support of \lambda is a chain.
Then we obtain a solution of the original problem (by omitting \lambda(\{0\})).
Finally, consider the LP-dual of (4.2):
{\rm Max.}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}z_{k}\quad{\rm s.t.}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{m}z_{k}\dim X\cap H_{k}\leq 2\dim X\ (X\leqslant\mathbb{K}^{n}),\ z\in\mathbb{Q}_{+}^{m}.
The strong duality holds, since the number of inequalities appearing in this problem is finite.
For y=z/2, this is nothing but twice \max\{c^{\top}y\mid y\in FMP({\cal H})\}.
4.2 Brascamp-Lieb inequality
The celebrated Brascamp-Lieb inequality [6, 43] states that:
Given surjective linear maps B_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}} and nonnegative reals
p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+} for j=1,2,\ldots,m (BL-datum), the inequality
\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{m}f_{j}(B_{j}x)^{p_{j}}{\rm d}x\leq C_{\rm BL}\prod_{j=1}^{m}\left(\int_{x_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}}f_{j}(x_{j}){\rm d}x_{j}\right)^{p_{j}}
(4.3)
holds for any nonnegative-valued measurable funtions f_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}\to\mathbb{R}_{+} (j=1,2,\ldots,m).
The best constant (the BL-constant) C_{\rm BL} is given by
C_{\rm BL}:=\left(\sup_{X_{j}}\frac{\prod_{j=1}^{m}(\det X_{j})^{p_{j}}}{\det\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}B_{j}^{\top}X_{j}B_{j}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\in[0,\infty),
(4.4)
where X_{j} ranges over all positive definite matrices of size n_{j}.
Several important geometric inequalities, such as the Hölder inequality, can be deduced from the BL-inequality for special BL-data.
The most familiar one is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
which corresponds to p_{1}=p_{2}=1/2,B_{1}=B_{2}=I.
The interesting case of the BL-inequality is of C_{\rm BL}<\infty, which is characterized by Bennett et al. [4] as follows:
Theorem 4.6 ([4]).
Given BL-datum B_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n_{j}}, p_{j}\in\mathbb{R}_{+} (j=1,2,\ldots,m),
the BL-constant is finite if and only if
the BL-datum satisfies
n=\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}n_{j} and
\sum_{j=1}^{m}p_{j}\dim B_{j}V\geq\dim V for every V\leqslant\mathbb{R}^{n}.
In particular, the set of all vectors p=(p_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{+}
with finite BL-constant
forms a rational polytope,
which is called the BL-polytope associated with \{B_{j}\}_{j}
and is denoted by BLP(\{B_{j}\}_{j}).
The computational complexity of the BL-polytope
has recently attracted attention in theoretical computer science [21].
To the best of our knowledge, only a weak membership algorithm for the general BL-polytope is known [7].
More precisely, given a rational point p, the algorithm of [7] can distinguish whether p in the BL-polytope or p is \varepsilon-far from the BL-polytope in time \poly(N,\varepsilon), where N is the total bit complexity of the input BL datum.
It is open if one can improve the time complexity to \poly(N,\log(1/\varepsilon)).
BL-polytopes are interesting from the combinatorial optimization point of view: It is known [3] that
if each B_{j} is rank-1, the BL-polytope is equal to
the base polytope of the linear matroid associated
with the row vectors of B_{j}.
In this case, the (strong) membership of the BL-polytope
can be solved in polynomial time, via the separation-optimization equivalence [27]
with the matroid greedy algorithm.
A further interesting connection was discovered by
Franks, Soma, and Goemans [19].
They showed that
the BL-polytope for rank-2 matrices (the rank-2 BL-polytope) coincides with
the perfect fractional matroid matching polytope for the row spaces of these matrices.
Theorem 4.7 ([19]).
Suppose that each B_{j} is rank-2.
Then the BL-polytope BLP(\{B_{j}\}_{j}) coincides with
the perfect fractional matching polytope PFMP(\{H_{j}\}_{j}),
where H_{j} is the 2-dimensional subspace in \mathbb{R}^{n} spanned by the row vectors of B_{j}.
Therefore, a polynomial-time linear optimization algorithm for fractional matroid matching problem
implies a polynomial complexity of the membership algorithm for
rank-2 BL-polytopes.
As mentioned in Section 3.2.3, such an algorithm was
given by Gijswijt and Pap [26].
However, as pointed out by Franks, Soma, and Goemans [19],
a direct application of this algorithm
can yield exponential bit-complexities in intermediate numbers
when \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q} (the setting of the BL-inequality).
They utilized modified operator scaling to establish
a good characterization for the membership of rank-2 BL-polytopes:
Theorem 4.8 ([19]).
The membership problem of rank-2 BL polytopes is in {\rm NP}\cap{\rm coNP}.
The coincidence between {\cal Q}_{n}(A_{\cal H}) and PFMP({\cal H}) established in the previous subsection enables us to apply our framework, particularly the modulo-p reduction trick,
for linear optimization over rank-2 BL polytopes. Thus we have:
Theorem 4.9.
The membership problem of rank-2 BL-polytopes is in P.
