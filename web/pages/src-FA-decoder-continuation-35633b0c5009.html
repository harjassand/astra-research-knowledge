<div id="abstract1" class="ltx_abstract"><h6 class="ltx_title ltx_title_abstract">Abstract</h6>
    
<p id="abstract1.1" class="ltx_p">Metamaterials are composite structures whose properties arise from a mesoscale organization of their constituents. Provided this organization occurs on scales smaller than the characteristic lengths associated with their response, it is often possible to design such materials to have properties that are otherwise impossible to achieve with conventional materials– including negative indexes of refraction, perfect absorption of electromagnetic radiation, and negative Poisson ratios <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib1" title="" class="ltx_ref">1</a>, <a href="#bib.bib2" title="" class="ltx_ref">2</a>, <a href="#bib.bib3" title="" class="ltx_ref">3</a>, <a href="#bib.bib4" title="" class="ltx_ref">4</a>, <a href="#bib.bib5" title="" class="ltx_ref">5</a>, <a href="#bib.bib6" title="" class="ltx_ref">6</a>, <a href="#bib.bib7" title="" class="ltx_ref">7</a>, <a href="#bib.bib8" title="" class="ltx_ref">8</a>, <a href="#bib.bib9" title="" class="ltx_ref">9</a>]</cite>. Here, we introduce and demonstrate a new material class– viscosity metamaterials. Specifically, we show that we are able to rapidly drive large viscosity oscillations in a shear-thickened fluid using acoustic perturbations with kHz to MHz frequencies. Because the time scale for these oscillations can be orders of magnitude smaller than the timescales associated with the global material flow, we can construct metamaterials whose resulting viscosity is a composite of the thickened, high-viscosity and dethickened, low viscosity states. Such viscosity metamaterials can be used to engineer a variety of surprising properties including negative viscosities, a response that is inconceivable with conventional fluids. The high degree of control over the resulting viscosity, the ease with which they can be accessed, and the variety of exotic properties achievable by viscosity metamaterials make them attractive for uses in technologies for which control over fluid flows and their instabilities are critical, ranging from coatings to cloaking to 3D printing.</p>

<p id="p2.1" class="ltx_p">Our inspiration for designing viscosity metamaterials arises from the tunability of a shear-thickened suspension’s viscosity via acoustic perturbations <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite>. Under shear, nearly all dense suspensions undergo a thickening transition from a low-viscosity to a high-viscosity state <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib11" title="" class="ltx_ref">11</a>, <a href="#bib.bib12" title="" class="ltx_ref">12</a>, <a href="#bib.bib13" title="" class="ltx_ref">13</a>, <a href="#bib.bib14" title="" class="ltx_ref">14</a>]</cite>. This transition occurs when the applied shear stresses are large enough to drive particles into contact, forming force chains that are primarily aligned along the maximum compressive axis <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib15" title="" class="ltx_ref">15</a>, <a href="#bib.bib16" title="" class="ltx_ref">16</a>, <a href="#bib.bib17" title="" class="ltx_ref">17</a>, <a href="#bib.bib18" title="" class="ltx_ref">18</a>, <a href="#bib.bib19" title="" class="ltx_ref">19</a>, <a href="#bib.bib20" title="" class="ltx_ref">20</a>, <a href="#bib.bib21" title="" class="ltx_ref">21</a>, <a href="#bib.bib22" title="" class="ltx_ref">22</a>, <a href="#bib.bib23" title="" class="ltx_ref">23</a>, <a href="#bib.bib24" title="" class="ltx_ref">24</a>, <a href="#bib.bib25" title="" class="ltx_ref">25</a>, <a href="#bib.bib26" title="" class="ltx_ref">26</a>, <a href="#bib.bib27" title="" class="ltx_ref">27</a>, <a href="#bib.bib28" title="" class="ltx_ref">28</a>, <a href="#bib.bib29" title="" class="ltx_ref">29</a>]</cite>. The resulting increase in viscosity can span orders of magnitude and in sufficiently dense suspensions can result in a completely rigid, shear jammed state. Recently, we have shown that by applying acoustic perturbations the suspension viscosity can be tuned from the thickened to the fully dethickened state <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite>. The applied acoustic perturbations are presumed to dethicken the suspension by breaking up particle contacts important for force transduction. Importantly, since acoustic perturbations can be applied at kHz to MHz frequencies, there is an opportunity to generate new material properties via rapidly cycling between the thickened and dethickened states (Fig. <a href="#S0.F1" title="Figure 1 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>).</p>
</div>
<div id="p3" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p3.1" class="ltx_p">We pursue this strategy using charge stabilized monodispersed colloidal suspensions consisting of 2-<math id="p3.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\mu" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mi>μ</mi><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\mu</annotation></semantics></math>m silica particles suspended in dipropylene glycol (<math id="p3.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\eta_{0}=0.11\text{ Pa s}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>η</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mn>0.11</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mtext> Pa s</mtext></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\eta_{0}=0.11\text{ Pa s}</annotation></semantics></math>) at a volume fraction of <math id="p3.m3" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\phi\simeq 0.53" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>ϕ</mi><mo>≃</mo><mn>0.53</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\phi\simeq 0.53</annotation></semantics></math>. When we apply acoustic perturbations to a quiescent suspension (for apparatus and analysis, see methods), we find that particles form a disordered hyperuniform state, as shown by the behavior of the number density variance and structure factor in Fig. <a href="#S0.F1" title="Figure 1 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>(a). These results indicate that acoustic perturbations generate isotropic repulsion that maximizes the interparticle separations as shown in the schematic at the bottom of Fig. <a href="#S0.F1" title="Figure 1 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>(a). Conversely, as the suspension thickens under shear, particles form contacts, organized into anisotropic force chains that align with the maximum compressive axis. The increase in viscosity arises from the increase in average number of contacts between the particles <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib30" title="" class="ltx_ref">30</a>, <a href="#bib.bib16" title="" class="ltx_ref">16</a>, <a href="#bib.bib27" title="" class="ltx_ref">27</a>, <a href="#bib.bib25" title="" class="ltx_ref">25</a>]</cite> (Fig. <a href="#S0.F1" title="Figure 1 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>(b)). In simulations (see methods and SI for details), the force chains formed can be visualized by plotting the 3-dimensional microstructure as seen in the inset of Fig. <a href="#S0.F1" title="Figure 1 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">1</span></a>(b). These results illustrate that there is an opportunity for toggling between these two structural arrangements to form viscosity metamaterials.</p>
</div>
<div id="p4" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p4.1" class="ltx_p">To investigate whether such oscillations are indeed possible, we develop acoustic protocols and implement them using a previously described acoustic apparatus mounted on an Anton Paar MCR Rheometer <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite>. In particular, to generate the oscillations in the viscosity, we apply the acoustic fields intermittently. We anticipate that over the period where the acoustic perturbations are on <math id="p4.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\mathrm{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>on</mi></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\mathrm{on}}</annotation></semantics></math>, the viscosity will rapidly drop due to dissolution of force chains. Conversely, over the period where the perturbations are turned off <math id="p4.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\mathrm{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>off</mi></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\mathrm{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>, formation of force chains is unimpeded, and the viscosity increases.</p>

<div id="p7" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p7.1" class="ltx_p">To illustrate the broad range of parameters over which acoustically generated viscosity metamaterials can be accessed, we determine the threshold strain rates and periods <math id="p7.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\mathrm{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>off</mi></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\mathrm{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>, beyond which it is possible to attain metamaterial states. Crucially, to obtain metamaterial states, the strain rate must be sufficiently high for the suspension to thicken via formation of force chains during the period where the acoustics are off, <math id="p7.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\mathrm{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mi>off</mi></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\mathrm{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>. To determine the threshold strain rate for each <math id="p7.m5" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>off</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>, we note that in the metamaterial state the time-averaged viscosity under continuous acoustic perturbations, <math id="p7.m6" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\bar{\eta}_{CP}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mrow><mi>C</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\bar{\eta}_{CP}</annotation></semantics></math>, is always smaller than the time-averaged viscosity under intermittent acoustic perturbations, <math id="p7.m7" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\bar{\eta}_{IP}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\bar{\eta}_{IP}</annotation></semantics></math>, whereas in the fully dethickened state, these viscosities are identical. Thus, for each value of <math id="p7.m8" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>off</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{off}}</annotation></semantics></math> we measure the magnitude of metamaterial viscosity <math id="p7.m9" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\bar{\eta}_{M}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mi>M</mi></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\bar{\eta}_{M}</annotation></semantics></math> defined as the normalized difference of the time averaged viscosities (<math id="p7.m10" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\bar{\eta}_{IP}-\bar{\eta}_{CP})/{\bar{\eta}_{CP}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mrow><mo fence="true" rspace="0em">OPEN</mo><mrow><msub><mover </div>
<div id="p8" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p8.1" class="ltx_p">Collectively, from these observations, we composed a qualitative phase diagram describing the region in the <math id="p8.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="1/T_{\text{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mn>1</mn><mo>/</mo><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>off</mtext></msub></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">1/T_{\text{off}}</annotation></semantics></math> versus <math id="p8.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\dot{\gamma}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mover accent="true"><mi>γ</mi><mo>˙</mo></mover><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\dot{\gamma}</annotation></semantics></math> parameter space where viscosity metamaterials can be generated (Fig. <a href="#S0.F3" title="Figure 3 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>(c)). At low shear rates, the viscosity is Newtonian (grey) and insensitive to acoustic perturbations. Similarly, we anticipate that at extremely high shear rates inaccessible in our rheology measurements, the applied power will be insufficient to dethicken the system and the viscosity reflects the fully thickened state (yellow). At intermediate shear rates and finite values of <math id="p8.m3" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>off</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>, the boundary between the fully dethickened (orange) and viscosity metamaterial region (green) is governed by the interplay between acoustic perturbations and shear flows as measured in Fig. <a href="#S0.F3" title="Figure 3 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>(b). Moreover, these boundaries can also be shifted with the acoustic power <math id="p8.m4" class="ltx_Math" alttext="P_{\text{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>P</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P_{\text{on}}</annotation></semantics></math> and <math id="p8.m5" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{on}}</annotation></semantics></math> (dashed lines). Importantly, at each point within the viscosity metamaterial region, we can dramatically tune the viscosity envelope that characterizes the metamaterial (Fig. <a href="#S0.F2" title="Figure 2 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">2</span></a>(c)). Specifically, by precisely controlling <math id="p8.m6" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{off}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>off</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{off}}</annotation></semantics></math>, <math id="p8.m7" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{on}}</annotation></semantics></math>, the acoustic power, and strain rate, vastly different combinations of the minimum, average, and maximum viscosity can be obtained. As such, acoustic perturbations enable us to access novel metamaterial states over a broad range of parameters, with the potential to engineer responses that are impossible to achieve in conventional fluids.</p>
</div>
<div id="p9" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p9.1" class="ltx_p">To illustrate one such response, we use viscosity metamaterials to engineer a negative viscosity.
Such negative viscosity states have been reported in previous work on active bacterial suspensions <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib37" title="" class="ltx_ref">37</a>]</cite>, and in shear thickening fluids driven by oscillatory shear <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib38" title="" class="ltx_ref">38</a>]</cite>. In contrast to the viscosity metamaterials described here, however, such responses could only be achieved over an extremely limited range of parameters. To achieve negative viscosities in our system, we oscillate the driving stress sinusoidally with a positive bias and apply acoustics to generate low viscosity states during selective points in the cycle (Fig. <a href="#S0.F4" title="Figure 4 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4</span></a>(a)). If the acoustics are applied in phase with the stress (<math id="p9.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\theta=0" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mo>=</mo><mn>0</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta=0</annotation></semantics></math>), the low viscosity states are generated while the suspension is flowing forward, resulting in an effective low positive viscosity state. In contrast if the acoustics are applied with a phase lag <math id="p9.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\theta=\pi" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>θ</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>π</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\theta=\pi</annotation></semantics></math>, the low viscosity states are generated while the suspension is flowing <span id="p9.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_italic">backwards</span>, resulting in a net negative strain rate (Fig. <a href="#S0.F4" title="Figure 4 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4</span></a>(b)). In this scenario, the suspension flows in the direction opposite to the average applied stress, and displays an negative effective viscosity.</p>

<div id="p11" class="ltx_para">
<p id="p11.1" class="ltx_p">Because these precise acoustic oscillation protocols requires only that we attach a piezo at the boundary, achieving the demonstrated metamaterial states should be straightforward even in complex flow geometries.
Moreover, similar viscosity oscillations could be implemented in more specialized suspensions whose thickening can be controlled magneto or electro-rheologically <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib39" title="" class="ltx_ref">39</a>, <a href="#bib.bib40" title="" class="ltx_ref">40</a>, <a href="#bib.bib41" title="" class="ltx_ref">41</a>]</cite>. As such, viscosity metamaterials open uncharted avenues for conveniently generating novel fluid flows, transient flow behaviours, and geometry-specific responses, all of which could be used in next-generation applications. These include new methods for processing dense coatings, generating hydrodynamic cloaking <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib6" title="" class="ltx_ref">6</a>, <a href="#bib.bib42" title="" class="ltx_ref">42</a>, <a href="#bib.bib43" title="" class="ltx_ref">43</a>, <a href="#bib.bib44" title="" class="ltx_ref">44</a>, <a href="#bib.bib45" title="" class="ltx_ref">45</a>, <a href="#bib.bib46" title="" class="ltx_ref">46</a>, <a href="#bib.bib47" title="" class="ltx_ref">47</a>]</cite>, and processing dense suspension in microfluidic environments during 3D printing <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib48" title="" class="ltx_ref">48</a>]</cite>.</p>

<p id="S0.SSx1.p1.2" class="ltx_p"><span id="S0.SSx1.p1.2.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Apparatus:</span> We work with charge-stabilized, monodispersed colloidal suspensions consisting of 2-<math id="S0.SSx1.p1.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\mu" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mi>μ</mi><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\mu</annotation></semantics></math>m silica particles suspended in dipropylene glycol (<math id="S0.SSx1.p1.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\eta=0.11\text{ Pa s}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>η</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mn>0.11</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mtext> Pa s</mtext></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\eta=0.11\text{ Pa s}</annotation></semantics></math>) at a volume fraction of <math id="S0.SSx1.p1.m3" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\phi\simeq 0.53" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>ϕ</mi><mo>≃</mo><mn>0.53</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\phi\simeq 0.53</annotation></semantics></math>. The test setup consists of a piezoelectric disk bonded via epoxy to a custom-made aluminum bottom-plate (details in <cite class="ltx_cite ltx_citemacro_cite">[<a href="#bib.bib10" title="" class="ltx_ref">10</a>]</cite>). The acoustic perturbations are generated by applying an AC voltage signal to the piezoelectric disk at resonance frequency <math id="S0.SSx1.p1.m4" class="ltx_Math" alttext="f=1.15\text{ MHz}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>f</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mn>1.15</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mtext> MHz</mtext></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">f=1.15\text{ MHz}</annotation></semantics></math> corresponding to its thickness mode of vibration. For imaging the particle distributions in the presence of perturbations, the piezo-plate setup is integrated with a Zeiss 5 Live inverted confocal microscope, wherein the suspension is confined between the aluminum bottom-plate and a glass slide. For measuring the rheological response in the presence of acoustic perturbations and shear, the piezo-plate setup is integrated with the Anton-Paar MCR 702 Rheometer. The glass top plate is used to apply steady shear to the suspension and measure the shear viscosity. The gap between the bottom plate and the top plate is set to 0.64 mm.</p>

<div id="S0.SSx1.p3" class="ltx_para">
<p id="S0.SSx1.p3.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S0.SSx1.p3.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Protocol for measuring the magnitude of metamaterial viscosity (Fig.  <a href="#S0.F3" title="Figure 3 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">3</span></a>a):</span> First, we preshear the suspension to achieve the steady state. Following preshear, we shear the suspension at a fixed strain rate for <math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="t\simeq~60" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>t</mi><mo rspace="0.608em">≃</mo><mn>60</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">t\simeq~60</annotation></semantics></math> s. Concurrently, we apply continuous perturbations (<math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="P_{\text{c}}=250" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>P</mi><mtext>c</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>250</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P_{\text{c}}=250</annotation></semantics></math> mW) for the first 20 s and then apply intermittent perturbations (<math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m3" class="ltx_Math" alttext="P_{\text{on}}=250" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>P</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>250</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P_{\text{on}}=250</annotation></semantics></math> mW) of desired off-time for the next 40 s. We repeat this process for different strain rates and off times in a systematic manner, while keeping the <math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m4" class="ltx_Math" alttext="P_{\text{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>P</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">P_{\text{on}}</annotation></semantics></math> and <math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m5" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T_{\text{on}}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><msub><mi>T</mi><mtext>on</mtext></msub><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T_{\text{on}}</annotation></semantics></math> fixed. From the instantaneous viscosity response at each condition, we measure the time averaged viscosities during the IP and CP, and calculate the magnitude of metamaterial viscosity as <math id="S0.SSx1.p3.m6" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\bar{\eta}_{M}=(\bar{\eta}_{IP}-\bar{\eta}_{CP})/\bar{\eta}_{CP}" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mi>M</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mrow><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mrow><mi>I</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub><mo>−</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mrow><mi>C</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow><mo>/</mo><msub><mover accent="true"><mi>η</mi><mo>¯</mo></mover><mrow><mi>C</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>P</mi></mrow></msub></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\bar{\eta}_{M}=(\bar{\eta}_{IP}-\bar{\eta}_{CP})/\bar{\eta}_{CP}</annotation></semantics></math>.</p>

<div id="S0.SSx1.p4" class="ltx_para">
<p id="S0.SSx1.p4.1" class="ltx_p"><span id="S0.SSx1.p4.1.1" class="ltx_text ltx_font_bold">Protocol for generating the negative-viscosity states (Fig.  <a href="#S0.F4" title="Figure 4 ‣ Viscosity Metamaterials" class="ltx_ref"><span class="ltx_text ltx_ref_tag">4</span></a>):</span> First, we preshear the suspension to achieve the steady state. Following the preshear, we measure the viscosity at different stresses to identify the optimal mean stress, <math id="S0.SSx1.p4.m1" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\sigma_{0}=34.8" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>σ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>34.8</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sigma_{0}=34.8</annotation></semantics></math> Pa and the amplitude of the stress oscillations, <math id="S0.SSx1.p4.m2" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\sigma_{a}=139.1" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><msub><mi>σ</mi><mi>a</mi></msub><mo>=</mo><mn>139.1</mn></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sigma_{a}=139.1</annotation></semantics></math>Pa. We then apply a stress, <math id="S0.SSx1.p4.m3" class="ltx_Math" alttext="\sigma=\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{a}\sin(2\pi t/T)" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>σ</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><msub><mi>σ</mi><mn>0</mn></msub><mo>+</mo><mrow><msub><mi>σ</mi><mi>a</mi></msub><mo lspace="0.167em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mrow><mi>sin</mi><mo>⁡</mo><mrow><mo stretchy="false">(</mo><mrow><mrow><mn>2</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>π</mi><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>t</mi></mrow><mo>/</mo><mi>T</mi></mrow><mo stretchy="false">)</mo></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">\sigma=\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{a}\sin(2\pi t/T)</annotation></semantics></math>, where t is the time in seconds, and <math id="S0.SSx1.p4.m4" class="ltx_Math" alttext="T=10s" display="inline" intent=":literal"><semantics><mrow><mi>T</mi><mo>=</mo><mrow><mn>10</mn><mo lspace="0em" rspace="0em">​</mo><mi>s</mi></mrow></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">T=10s</annotation></semantics></math> is the time period of the oscillations. Concurrently, we apply a square acoustic wave by simply turning the piezoelectric disk on and off at the appropriate points on the cycle. We repeat this process over a range of phase lags between the acoustics and the driving stress. We apply two stress oscillation cycles and average the stress, strain rate and viscosity over both the cycles.</p>
