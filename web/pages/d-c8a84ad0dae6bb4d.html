Abstract
The completely positive maps, a generalization of the nonnegative matrices, are a well-studied class of maps from n\times n matrices to m\times m matrices. The existence of the operator analogues of doubly stochastic scalings of matrices, the study of which is known as operator scaling, is equivalent to a multitude of problems in computer science and mathematics such rational identity testing in non-commuting variables, noncommutative rank of symbolic matrices, and a basic problem in invariant theory (Garg et. al., 2016).
We study operator scaling with specified marginals, which is the operator analogue of scaling matrices to specified row and column sums (or marginals). We characterize the operators which can be scaled to given marginals, much in the spirit of the Gurvits’ algorithmic characterization of the operators that can be scaled to doubly stochastic (Gurvits, 2004). Our algorithm, which is a modified version of Gurvits’ algorithm, produces approximate scalings in time \operatorname{poly}(n,m) whenever scalings exist. A central ingredient in our analysis is a reduction from operator scaling with specified marginals to operator scaling in the doubly stochastic setting.
Instances of operator scaling with specified marginals arise in diverse areas of study such as the Brascamp-Lieb inequalities, communication complexity, eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian matrices, and quantum information theory. Some of the known theorems in these areas, several of which had no algorithmic proof, are straightforward consequences of our characterization theorem. For instance, we obtain a simple algorithm to find, when it exists, a tuple of Hermitian matrices with given spectra whose sum has a given spectrum. We also prove new theorems such as a generalization of Forster’s theorem (Forster, 2002) concerning radial isotropic position.

1 Introduction
Completely positive maps are linear maps between spaces of matrices that, informally speaking, preserve positive-semidefiniteness in a strong sense. Completely positive maps generalize the nonnegative matrices and arise in quantum information theory [20]. To each completely positive map T:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}(\mathbb{C}) is associated another completely positive map T^{*}:\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C}) known as the dual of T. In analogy with the matrix case, say a completely positive map is doubly stochastic if n=m and T(I_{n})=I_{n} and T^{*}(I_{n})=I_{n}. A scaling T^{\prime} of a completely positive map T by a pair of invertible linear maps (g,h) is another completely positive map T_{g,h}:X\mapsto g^{\dagger}T(hXh^{\dagger})g. One is led to ask which completely positive maps have doubly stochastic scalings; operator scaling is the study of this question. In fact, several other problems such as rational identity testing in non-commuting variables [14], membership in the null-cone of the left-right action of \operatorname{SL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{SL}_{m}(\mathbb{C}) [13], and a special case of Edmonds’ problem [16] each reduce to (or are equivalent to) an approximate version of this question. In [16], Gurvits gave two useful equivalent conditions for approximate scalability: a completely positive map T:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n} can be approximately scaled to doubly stochastic if and only if T is rank-nondecreasing, i.e. \operatorname{rank}T(X)\geq\operatorname{rank}X for all X\succeq 0, or equivalently \operatorname{cap}T>0 where
\operatorname{cap}T:=\inf_{X\succ 0}\frac{\det T(X)}{\det X}.
Gurvits also gave an algorithm to compute approximate scalings if either of these equivalent conditions hold. The authors of [14], [13],[16] analyzed the same algorithm to obtain polynomial-time decision algorithms for each of the aforementioned problems.
We consider a natural generalization of doubly stochastic scalings. Say T maps (A\to B,C\to D) if T(A)=B and T^{*}(C)=D.
Say T is (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalable if there exists a scaling of T that maps (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P).11

Question 1.
Given positive semidefinite matrices P and Q, which completely positive maps are (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalable?
Note that (I_{n}\to I_{n},I_{n}\to I_{n})-scalability is precisely the doubly stochastic case treated by Gurvits in [16]. We extend Gurvits’ characterization of approximate scalability to the setting of Question 1. As in [16], our proofs show how to efficiently produce approximate scalings when they exist. Our first main theorem, which closely resembles the characterization in [16], characterizes the existence of approximate (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalability by block-upper-triangular matrices. Next we extend this characterization to handle scaling by the full group of invertible matrices with a somewhat surprising outcome - informally, a completely positive map T is approximately (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalable if and only if a random scaling of T is approximately (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalable by upper triangular matrices with high probability.
An interesting property of the characterizations is that for T fixed, the admissible spectra of P and Q form a convex polytope known as a moment polytope. This is a special case of a more general phenomenon obeyed by group actions studied in algebra, geometry and physics; see [31].
1.1 Prior Work
Following a close variant of Question 1 asked in [15], a special case of our characterization had already been proven in [11] via fixed-point theorems apparently unrelated to our techniques: namely, it was shown that any positivity-improving operator, or operator that maps the nonzero positive-semidefinite matrices to the positive-definite matrices, is (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scalable.22

The convexity of the marginals of scalings of T follows from well-known theorems on moment polytopes; for a survey of these results see [31]. These techniques could also likely be used to deduce our characterization of (I\to Q,I\to P)-scalability [10], but to the author’s knowledge this has not been done explicitly in the literature. Further, these techniques do not result in algorithms and rely on powerful theorems from geometric invariant theory. In contrast, our proofs yield algorithms for computing approximate scalings and require only the results of [16],[14] and a few basic facts from algebraic geometry. One similarity does exist: the so-called shifting trick [3] in geometric invariant theory is a different type of reduction, but one that does not stay within the realm of operator scaling.

Question 2 (Matrix scaling).
Given a nonnegative matrix A\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m,n}(\mathbb{R}) and nonnegative row- and column-sum vectors \mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0} and \mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}, do there exist diagonal matrices X,Y such that the row (resp. column) sums of A^{\prime}=XAY are \mathbf{r} (resp. \mathbf{c})?

Question 5 (Forster’s scalings).
Given vectors u_{1},\dots,u_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}, nonnegative numbers p_{1},\dots,p_{n}, and a positive-semidefinite matrix Q, when does there exist an invertible linear transformation B:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m} such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\frac{Bu_{i}(Bu_{i})^{\dagger}}{\|Bu_{i}\|^{2}}=Q?
Forster answered Question 5 in the positive for p_{i}=1, u_{i} in general position, and Q=\frac{n}{m}I_{m}; as a consequence he was able to prove previously unattainable lower bounds in communication complexity [9]. As noted in [16], Forster’s result is a consequence of Gurvits’ characterization of doubly stochastic scalings. Independently, Barthe [2] answered this question completely for the case Q=I_{m} in order to study the rank-one Brascamp-Lieb inequalities. He showed the left hand side can approach the right hand side if and only if (p_{1},\dots,p_{n}) lies in the basis polytope of u_{1},\dots,u_{n}. In Section 6.3 we reduce the general case of Question 5 to an instance of Question 1, and use this reduction to answer the approximate version of Question 5. For fixed u_{1},\dots,u_{n} and Q, the admissible (p_{1},\dots,p_{n}) form a convex polytope with known as an polymatroid, of which the basis polytope is a special case. It would be interesting to find an application of our more general characterization, perhaps along the lines of the use of Barthe’s theorem in subspace recovery in [17].
Question 6 (Quantum marginals).
For which positive-semidefinite operators \rho:\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{m} and positive-semidefinite operators P:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}, Q:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m} can the reduced density matrices of (g\otimes h)\rho(g\otimes h)^{\dagger} be made arbitrarily close to P and Q?
Question 6 is equivalent to (the approximate version of) Question 1 by a correspondence known as state-channel duality [20]. The convex polytope of admissible spectra for the tripartite version of Question 6, in which there are three specified reduced density matrices P,Q and R rather than two, is is called the Kronecker polytope and arises in the representation theory of the symmetric group. A polynomial time algorithm for Question 1 would confirm that membership in the Kronecker polytope is in \mathbf{P}, whereas it is only known to be in \mathbf{coNP}\cap\mathbf{NP} [4]. The author views this paper as a step towards solving this problem - however, we have only two specified marginals and can produce only a very inefficient membership oracle for our polytope. The work [5], which came shortly after this one, remedies the former issue but not the latter.

2 Background and results
2.1 Preliminaries
We will require a few definitions. If A is a matrix, \|A\|:=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}AA^{\dagger}}.
Definition 7 (completely positive maps).
A completely positive map is a map T:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}(\mathbb{C}) of the form
T:X\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}XA_{i}^{\dagger},
A_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{m} are linear maps called Kraus operators of T. Note that T preserves positive-semidefiniteness. The map T^{*}:\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C}) is given by
T^{*}:X\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{r}A_{i}^{\dagger}XA_{i},
and is the adjoint of T in the trace inner product \langle A,B\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}A^{\dagger}B, where A^{\dagger} is the conjugate transpose of A, where any \mathbb{C}^{d} is understood to be equipped with the standard Hermitian inner product.
Definition 8 (approximate scalings).
Say a scaling T^{\prime} of T is an \epsilon-(A\to B,C\to D)-scaling if T^{\prime} maps (A\to B^{\prime},C\to D^{\prime}) with \|B-B^{\prime}\|\leq\epsilon and \|D-D^{\prime}\|\leq\epsilon. If G\subset\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}) and H\subset\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C}), say T is approximately G\times H-scalable to (A\to B,C\to D) if for all \epsilon>0, T has an \epsilon-(A\to B,C\to D)-scaling T^{\prime} by (g,h)\in G\times H.
Throughout, we’ll let E=(e_{1},\dots,e_{n}) and F=(f_{1},\dots,f_{m}) be the standard orthonormal bases for \mathbb{C}^{n} and \mathbb{C}^{m}. We’ll assume that
P=\operatorname{diag}(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\textrm{ and }Q=\operatorname{diag}(q_{1},\dots,q_{n}),
where \mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n}) and \mathbf{q}=\operatorname{diag}(q_{1},\dots,q_{n}) are non-increasing sequences of nonnegative real numbers.
This is without loss of generality: the groups G and H of interest will contain the diagonalizing unitaries for anything the image of T and T^{*}, respectively, and if T_{g,h} is an (I\to Q,I\to P)-scaling of T then T_{gV,hU} is an (I_{n}\to V^{\dagger}QV,I_{m}\to U^{\dagger}PU)-scaling for any unitaries U and V.
If \mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{k}) is a non-increasing sequence of real numbers, the shorthand
\Delta a_{i}=a_{i}-a_{i+1}\textrm{ where }a_{k+1}:=0
will also be helpful.
Definition 9 (Flags).
We’ll first consider scalings by upper triangular matrices. Equivalently, the scalings must preserve the standard flag
E_{\bullet}=(\;\langle e_{1}\rangle,\langle e_{1},e_{2}\rangle,\dots,\langle e_{1},\dots,e_{n-1}\rangle\;)
in the basis E and the standard flag F_{\bullet} in the basis F. More generally, a flag is an increasing sequence of subspaces.
2.2 Capacity and Rank-Nondecreasingness Extended
Our results split into two parts: a characterization of scalability by upper-triangular matrices, and the application of this characterization to T_{g,h} for generic g and h. Our description of scalability by upper-triangular matrices is very similar to Gurvits’ characterization. We first need analogues of rank-nondecreasingness and capacity.
Rank-nondecreasingness
Recall that a completely positive operator T:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n} is rank-nondecreasing if \operatorname{rank}T(X)\geq\operatorname{rank}X for all X\succeq 0. Rank-nondecreasingness is much like a Hall’s condition for completely positive operators. We restate the definition using a notion we call T-independence, which is motivated by independent sets in bipartite graphs.
Definition 10 (T-independence).
Suppose T is as in 2.1. We say a pair of subspaces (L\subset\mathbb{C}^{m},R\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}) is T-independent if
L\subset(A_{i}R)^{\perp}\textrm{ for all }i\in[r].
Equivalently, the pair (L,R) is T-independent if and only if \pi_{L}T(\pi_{R})=0 where \pi_{L},\pi_{R} are the orthogonal projections to the subspaces L and R.
In this language, it’s not hard to see that T is rank-nondecreasing if and only if \operatorname{rank}L+\operatorname{rank}R\leq n for all T-independent pairs (L,R) - analogous to the fact that a bipartite graph G on [n]\cup[n] satisfies Hall’s condition if and only if the cardinality of the largest independent set is at most n. We extend the definition as follows:
Definition 11 (rank-nondecreasingness for specified marginals).
Suppose T,P,Q are as in 2.1. Say T is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing if for all T-independent pairs (L,R),
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta q_{i}\dim E_{i}\cap L+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta p_{j}\dim F_{j}\cap R\leq\operatorname{Tr}P.
(1)
Remark 12.
The purpose of this remark is to relate our rank-nondecreasingness condition with some familiar mathematical objects; namely the Schubert cells and varieties; see the introduction [23]. The k-dimensional subspaces of \mathbb{C}^{m} satisfying d_{i}=\dim E_{i}\cap L for all i\in[m] for a fixed sequence d is known as the Schubert cell \Omega^{\circ}_{I}(E_{\bullet}), where I=\{i:d_{i}-d_{i-1}\cap L>0\}\subset[m] is the sequence of “jumps” in the dimension. Observe that if L\in\Omega^{\circ}_{I}(E_{\bullet}) we have
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta q_{i}\dim E_{i}\cap L=\sum_{i\in I}q_{i}.
The Schubert cells partition the collection of k-dimensional subspaces of \mathbb{C}^{m} (the Grassmanian \operatorname{Gr(n,k)}). The Schubert variety \Omega^{\bullet}_{I}(E_{\bullet}) is the Zariski closure of \Omega^{\circ}_{I}(E_{\bullet}) in \operatorname{Gr}(m,k), and can be equivalently described as the k-dimensional subspaces such that for all j\in[k], \dim E_{i_{j}}\cap L\geq j where I=\{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}\}. Further, if p is monotone decreasing then the function \sum_{i\in I}p_{i} is monotone decreasing in the lattice of Schubert varieties ordered by inclusion. All this goes to show that T is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing if and only if
\sum_{i\in I}q_{i}+\sum_{j\in J}p_{i}\leq\operatorname{Tr}P
(2)
for all I\subset[m], J\subset[n] such that \Omega_{I}^{\bullet}(E_{\bullet})\times\Omega_{J}^{\bullet}(F_{\bullet}) contains a T-independent pair. This formulation also enjoys a similarity with Rothblum and Schneider’s condition for approximate scalability of matrices to specified row and column sums [28].

2.3 Main Theorems
We are ready to state our analogue of Gurvits’ characterization for upper-triangular scalings. Recall Gurvits’ theorem:
Theorem 17 (Gurvits [16]).
Let T:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C} be a completely positive map. The following are equivalent:
1.
T is rank-nondecreasing.
2.
\operatorname{cap}T>0.
3.
T is approximately \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to
(I_{n}\to I_{n},I_{n}\to I_{n}).
The QR-decomposition shows that in the setting of the above theorem there is no loss of generality in scaling by \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}) rather than \operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}). This is no longer true in our setting, which is why we need separate theorems for upper-triangular scalings and general scalings.
Theorem 18 (Main theorem for upper-triangular scalings).
Let T,P,Q be as in 2.1. The following are equivalent:
1.
T is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing.
2.
\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)>0.
3.
T is approximately \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to
(I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P).

2.
\operatorname{cap}(T_{g,h},P,Q)>0 for generic
(g^{\dagger},h)\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}).
3.

2.4 Techniques
Here we list the main technical issues that arise and how we overcome them.
Reduction to the Doubly Stochastic Case.
The main difficulty in proving Theorem 18, the characterization of upper-triangular scalability, is in guessing the correct notions of capacity and rank-nondecreasingness. In fact, they were not guessed directly, but rather follow from the existence of a reduction to the doubly stochastic case. The reduction, which is only defined when P,Q have integral spectra, is a map
T\mapsto\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T,
where \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T:\operatorname{Mat}_{\operatorname{Tr}P,\operatorname{Tr}P}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{\operatorname{Tr}Q,\operatorname{Tr}Q} is yet another completely positive map. \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q} has the property that T is scalable to (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P) if and only if \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T can be scaled to doubly stochastic.
One might be tempted to prove our main theorems by applying the results from [16] directly to \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. However, this has two drawbacks: firstly, the algorithms are only guaranteed to run in polynomial time if the spectra of P and Q are represented in unary. Secondly, the reduction does not work for irrational spectra, and so separate reasoning is necessary to extend Theorem 18 to that case.
Running Time.
After an initial random scaling step, our algorithm is a natural variant of the Sinkhorn-Knopp algorithm for matrix scaling [29]. This algorithm alternately scales so that T(P)=I or T^{*}(Q)=I, which are individually easy to enforce. Each step will increase \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q) unless T is very close to mapping (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n}). While this proves that nonzero capacity implies approximate (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n})-scalability, it will not give any upper bound on the number of scaling steps unless we have a lower bound on the capacity.
The lower bound ontained by computing the capacity lower bounds from [14] on \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is not sufficient to prove Theorem 21, which asserts the existence of a polynomial time algorithm for scaling. This lower bound relies on bounds on the degrees of polynomials invariant under scaling [7]. However, the required degree bound has since been improved - surprisingly, this seemingly minor improvement is enough. We are able to make a further improvement using the fact that e^{H(\textbf{p})}\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q) is log-concave in p and q. This results in a lower bound for \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q) which depends only on T,m, and n subject to \operatorname{Tr}P=\operatorname{Tr}Q=1.
General Scalings from Triangular Scalings
The definition of the reduction \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}, the proofs of its properties, and the proof of Theorem 18 require only the results of [16], elementary linear algebra, and calculus.
Theorem 19 and Theorem 21, however, require some algebraic geometry. Both use that T_{g,h} fails to be (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing if and only if (g^{\dagger},h) are in some affine variety depending only on P and Q. When the spectra of P and Q are rational the algebraic geometry is elementary55
5
only after relying on [7] to find the polynomials!. When the spectra are irrational we require Chevalley’s Theorem on quantifier elimination in algebra (see [27]).
3 Reduction to the doubly stochastic case
In this section we prove Theorem 22 below. The reduction, a map T\mapsto\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T, is inspired by the reduction between instances of the Brascamp-Lieb inequality in [13]. Item 1 will be obvious from our construction. We only prove Items 2 and 3 in this section; we prove Item 4 in Appendix A.2. The attentive reader will notice that we do not use Item 2 directly in the proof of Theorem 18, but we include the proof anyway to motivate our construction.
Theorem 22.
Suppose \mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q} are integral and that the sum of each is N. There exists a map T\mapsto\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T such that
1.
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T:\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C} is a completely positive operator.
2.
If, in addition, p and q are positive, \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}) if and only if T is approximately \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P).
3.
\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T=\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q), and
4.
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is rank-nondecreasing if and only if T is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing.
We first design a “gadget” to compose with T to enforce the marginal conditions of \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. Recall that a partition \lambda of a nonnegative integer l with k parts is a weakly decreasing sequence (\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}) of nonnegative integers summing to l.
Lemma 23.
For any partition \lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}) of l with \lambda_{k}>0, there is an injective, completely positive map G_{\lambda}:\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times k}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{l\times l}\mathbb{C} such that
\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(I_{k})=I_{l}\textrm{ and }G_{\lambda}^{*}(I_{l})=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda).
(14)
G_{\lambda} further satisfies
\displaystyle G_{\lambda}(Xh)=G_{\lambda}(X)G_{\lambda}(h)
(15)
for any X\in\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times k}(\mathbb{C}) and any upper-triangular h\in\operatorname{Mat}_{k\times k}(\mathbb{C}), and
\displaystyle\det G_{\lambda}(X)=\det(\operatorname{diag}(\lambda),X).
(16)
We delay the proof of Lemma 23 to Section 3.3, before which we show how to use Lemma 23 to construct and prove all the properties of the reduction.
Definition 24.
Think of \mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q} as partitions of N. Define
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T:=G_{\mathbf{q}}\circ T\circ G_{\mathbf{p}}^{*}.
3.1 Scalability Under the Reduction
We now show Item 2 of Theorem 22 with \operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C}) replaced by a smaller group G\times H; afterwards we will extend the proof using [16].
Here is a useful observation that simplifies our techniques; the proof is an easy change of variables argument.
Proposition 25.
Suppose P and Q are nonsingular. Then
T is \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{m}) if and only if
T is \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to (I_{m}\to Q,I_{n}\to P).
The above proposition shows for P,Q nonsingular, it is equivalent to characterize approximate (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{m})-scalability.
Lemma 26.
Let G=G_{\mathbf{q}}\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}), and H=G_{\mathbf{p}}\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). Then
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately
G\times H-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}) if and only if T is approximately
\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})-scalable to (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{m}).
Proof.
Assuming Lemma 23, we can immediately see that
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T(I_{N})=G_{\mathbf{q}}(T(P)), which is equal to I_{N} if and only if T(P)=I_{m}. Since (\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)^{*}=\operatorname{trun}_{Q,P}(T^{*}), by symmetry we have
\displaystyle\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T\textrm{ is d. s. }\iff T\textrm{ maps }(P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{m}).
(17)
If G^{\prime} and H^{\prime} are groups and S is a completely positive map, let S_{G^{\prime},H^{\prime}}=\{S_{g,h}:g\in G^{\prime},h\in H^{\prime}\}. Then approximate G^{\prime}\times H^{\prime}-scalability of S to (A\to B,C\to D) is equivalent to the statement that \overline{S_{G,H}} contains an element that maps (A\to B,C\to D). The closure here can be taken in, say, the operator norm.
In the next proposition we’ll see that Eq. 15 shows that scaling T by (g,h) corresponds to scaling \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T by (G_{\mathbf{q}}(g),G_{\mathbf{p}}(h)).
Proposition 27.
Let (g_{0},h_{0})\in\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). Then
\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{g_{0},h_{0}}=(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)_{G_{\mathbf{q}}(g_{0}),G_{\mathbf{p}}(h_{0})}.
Proof.
By definition,
(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{g_{0},h_{0}})(X)=G_{\mathbf{q}}(g_{0}^{\dagger}T(h_{0}G_{\mathbf{p}}^{*}(X)h_{0}^{\dagger})g_{0}).
To complete the proof, apply Eq. 15 and the equivalent dual version (G_{\mathbf{p}}^{*})_{h_{0},I_{N}}=(G_{\mathbf{p}})_{I_{N},h_{0}}^{*}=(G_{\mathbf{p}})^{*}_{G_{\mathbf{p}}(h_{0}),I_{l}}=(G_{\mathbf{p}}^{*})_{I_{l},G_{\mathbf{p}}(h_{0})}.
∎
We now finish the proof of Lemma 26. By Proposition 27,
\displaystyle\overline{(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)_{G,H}}=\overline{\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}),\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}}.
T\mapsto\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is an injective, linear map, so
\overline{\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}),\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}}=\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}\overline{T_{\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}),\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}}.
The above chain of equalities and Eq. 17 imply \overline{(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)_{G,H}} contains a doubly stochastic element if and only if \overline{T_{\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}),\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}} contains an element that maps (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{m}), so we are done. ∎
Proof of Item 2 of Theorem 22.
Lemma 26 and Proposition 25 imply that it is enough to show that \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately
G\times H-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}) if and only if \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately
\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}). The “only if” implication is immediate, but the “if” direction is subtler.
The key is that if \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately
\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}), then by Theorem 4.6 in [16], operator Sinkhorn iteration converges. That is, if we set T_{0}=\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T and for t\geq 1 set
\displaystyle T_{2t-1}=(T_{2t-2})_{g(t),I_{N}}
\displaystyle\textrm{ and }T_{2t}=(T_{2t-1})_{I_{N},h(t)}
\displaystyle\textrm{where }g(t)^{\dagger}T_{2t-2}(I_{N})g(t)=I_{N}
\displaystyle\textrm{ and }h(t)^{\dagger}T_{2t-1}^{*}(I_{N})h(t)=I_{N},
then T_{t}(I_{N}) and T_{t}^{*}(I_{N}) converge to I_{N}.
Luckily, we may take g(t)\in G and h(t)\in H! We only show this for g; the proof is similar for h. Inductively, suppose we have taken g(s)\in G and h(s)\in H for s<t. Let g=g(1)\dots g(s) and h=h(1)\dots h(s). Eq.15 implies in particular that G_{\mathbf{p}}:\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})\to\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C}) and G_{\mathbf{q}}:\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\to\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C}) are group homomorphisms, and so g=G_{\mathbf{q}}(g_{0}) and h=G_{\mathbf{p}}(h_{0}) for some (g_{0},h_{0})\in\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). By Proposition 27,
T_{2t-2}=(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)_{g,h}=(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{g_{0},h_{0}}).
Choose g_{1}\in\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet}) such that g_{1}^{\dagger}T_{g_{0},h_{0}}(P)g_{1}=T_{g_{0}g_{1},h_{0}}(P)=I_{m} and set g(t)=G_{\mathbf{q}}(g_{1}). This is possible by the existence of the Cholesky decomposition and the fact that the scaling procedure converges (in particular, T_{g_{0},h_{0}}(P)=T_{2t-2}(I_{N}) won’t be singular). By the above identity,
g(t)^{\dagger}T_{2t-2}(X)g(t)^{\dagger}=(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T_{g_{0}g_{1},h_{0}})(P)=I_{N}.
Thus, if \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{N}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}), then it is approximately G\times H-scalable to (I_{N}\to I_{N},I_{N}\to I_{N}).
∎
3.2 Capacity Under the Reduction
We still need to compute the capacity of \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. We first show that the infimum in \operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T can be taken over the smaller group H, where H is as in 26, without changing the value.
Lemma 28.
Define
\operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T=\inf_{h\in H}\frac{\det(\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T)(hh^{\dagger})}{\det hh^{\dagger}}.
Then
\operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T=\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T.
Proof.
We will show \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T=\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T using the proof of Theorem 17 in [16]. Clearly \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T\geq\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. We need to show that \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T\leq\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. It is enough to prove this when \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T>0. Suppose this is true, and let T_{0},T_{1},\dots be the operators resulting from operator Sinkhorn iteration on \operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T. As shown in the Proof of Item 2 of Theorem 22, the scalings can be taken in the smaller groups G and H. In [16] it is shown that at every step, \operatorname{cap}T_{t} increases by a factor that is at least f(\epsilon)>1 if the distance from doubly stochastic is at least \epsilon. Critically, the same argument shows \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}T_{t} changes by the exact same factor in each step! Since \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}T_{t}\leq 1 for t\geq 1, this shows that if \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}\operatorname{trun}_{P,Q}T>0, then T_{t}(I_{N}), T^{*}_{t}(I_{N}) converge to I_{N}. Since \operatorname{cap}T_{t} tends to one as T_{t}(I),T_{t}^{*}(I) tend to the identity ([14], Lemma 2.27), \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}T_{t} must also tend to one. Since \operatorname{cap}^{\prime}T_{t} and \operatorname{cap}T_{t} changed by the same factor at each step, they must have been equal to begin with.∎
The remainder of the proof is purely computational.
Proof of Item 3 of Theorem 22.

4 Triangular Scalings
This section contains the proof of Theorem 18, as well as some bounds on the running time of our algorithms.
4.1 Upper Triangular Scaling Algorithm
Here we show the implication \ref{delt_cap}\implies\ref{delt_scal} of Theorem 18. By Proposition 25, it is enough to show \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)>0 implies approximate (P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n})-scalability. The ideas here follow the ideas in [16] very closely. We use Jensen’s inequality to show capacity increases by a function of \epsilon in each step of Algorithm 3 unless T is already an \epsilon-(P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n})-scaling, and we show it is always bounded by one. Thus, if the capacity is nonzero to begin with, Sinkhorn scaling eventually results in an \epsilon-(P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n})-scaling.
Reducing to the nonsingular case

4.2 Proof of Theorem 18
Here we finish the proof of Theorem 18 by showing \ref{delt_rnd}\implies\ref{delt_cap}. We prove the stronger statement that approximate scalability implies approximate rank-nondecreasingness.
Proposition 42 (scalability implies rank-nondecreasingness).
Suppose there exists an \epsilon-(I_{m}\to Q,I_{n}\to P)-scaling of T by \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). Then |\operatorname{Tr}Q-\operatorname{Tr}P|\leq(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m})\epsilon and

Proof.
Suppose there exists an \epsilon-(I_{V}\to Q,I_{W}\to P)-scaling T_{g,h} of T by \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). Firstly,
\displaystyle|\operatorname{Tr}Q-\operatorname{Tr}P|
\displaystyle\leq|\operatorname{Tr}(Q-T_{g,h}(I))|+|\operatorname{Tr}(P-T_{h,g}^{*}(I))|
\displaystyle\leq(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m})\epsilon.
Next, consider any T-independent pair (L,R). Observe that (\underline{L},\underline{R})=(g^{-1}L,h^{-1}R) is a T_{g,h}-independent pair and \dim\underline{L}\cap E_{i}=\dim L\cap E_{i} for all i\in[m] and \dim\underline{R}\cap F_{i}=\dim R\cap F_{i} for all i\in[n] by membership of (g,h)\in\operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}).
We use a standard fact, which is essentially an inequality for the Rayleigh trace. See [12].
Fact 43.
Suppose a is a nonincreasing sequence of positive real numbers. Set A=\operatorname{diag}(\textbf{a}). Let E_{\bullet} be the standard flag of \mathbb{C}^{d}, and \pi_{L} the orthogonal projection to the subspace L\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}. Then
\operatorname{Tr}A\pi_{L}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\Delta a_{i}\dim L\cap E_{i}
with equality if and only if \pi_{L} commutes with A.
Since (\underline{L},\underline{R}) is T-independent, T_{g,h}^{*}(\pi_{\underline{L}})\pi_{\underline{R}}=0. Thus
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta q_{i}\dim L\cap E_{i}\leq\operatorname{Tr}Q\pi_{\underline{L}}
\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Tr}T_{g,h}(I)\pi_{\underline{L}}+\epsilon\sqrt{m}
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}T^{*}_{h,g}(\pi_{\underline{L}})+\epsilon\sqrt{m}
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}T^{*}_{h,g}(\pi_{\underline{L}})(I-\pi_{\underline{R}})+\epsilon\sqrt{m}
\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Tr}T^{*}_{h,g}(I)(I-\pi_{\underline{R}})+\epsilon\sqrt{m}
\displaystyle\leq\operatorname{Tr}P(I-\pi_{\underline{R}})+(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m})\epsilon
\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}P-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta p_{i}\dim F_{j}\cap R+(\sqrt{n}+\sqrt{m})\epsilon.
∎

If \epsilon is smaller than the minimum nonzero number among
\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}+\sqrt{n}}\left|\operatorname{Tr}P-\sum_{i\in I}q_{i}-\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}.\right|:I\subset[m],J\subset[n]\right\},
(19)
and there exists an \epsilon-(I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P)-scaling of T by \operatorname{GL}(E_{\bullet})\times\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}), then T is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing.
We first show that the set of \mathbf{p},\mathbf{q} such that \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q) is nonzero is convex. This immediately follows from the next proposition:
Proposition 45 (\log-concavity).
e^{H(\mathbf{p})}\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q) is \log-concave in \mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q}.
Proof.
First, one can make a change of variables argument, which we omit, to see that
\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)=e^{-H(\mathbf{p})}\inf_{h\in\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}\frac{\det(Q,T(hh^{\dagger}))}{\det(P,h^{\dagger}h)}.
However, the left-hand side is manifestly \log-convex in \mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q}! To see why, let’s expand.
\displaystyle\log\inf_{h\in\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}\frac{\det(Q,T(hh^{\dagger}))}{\det(P,h^{\dagger}h)}=\inf_{h\in\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}\log\frac{\det(Q,T(hh^{\dagger}))}{\det(P,h^{\dagger}h)}
\displaystyle=\inf_{h\in\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet})}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta q_{i}\log\det\eta_{i}T\left(hh^{\dagger}\right)\eta_{i}^{\dagger}-\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta p_{j}\log\det\eta_{j}h^{\dagger}h\eta_{j}^{\dagger}
with the convention 0\log 0=0. Is of the form f:\mathbb{R}^{m+n}_{+}\times U\to\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\}
defined by
f:(x,u)\mapsto\langle x,g(u)\rangle
where U is some set; in this case U=\operatorname{GL}(F_{\bullet}). It is easy to check that f is always concave when x,g(u) remain finite; with slightly more care one can check it when g(u)_{i}\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\} with the convention x_{i}g(u)_{i}=0 when x_{i}=0 and g(u)_{i}=-\infty.

4.3 Running Time of Algorithm 3
In order to use the guarantees from the previous subsection to bound the running time of Algorithm 3, we must bound the capacity below. For this we will need to use a nontrivial lower bound on \operatorname{cap}T from [14].
Theorem 47 (Garg et. al. [14]).
If T:\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C} is a rank-nondecreasing completely positive operator with Kraus operators A_{1}\dots A_{R} with Gaussian integer entries, then
\operatorname{cap}T\geq e^{-N\log(RN^{4})}.
This is implicit in the proof of Theorem 2.21 in [14], which gives the bound \exp(-O(N^{2}\log(RN^{4})). One of the bounds used there has since been improved; we discuss this degree bound, since we will use it later.
Definition 48.
Let T:\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{N\times N}\mathbb{C} be a completely positive map with Kraus operators A_{1}\dots A_{R}. The quantity \sigma(N,R) is defined to be the minimal d such that T is rank-nondecreasing if and only if the polynomial p:\operatorname{Mat}_{d\times d}(\mathbb{C})^{R}\to\mathbb{C} given by
p(B_{1},\dots,B_{R})=\det\left(\sum_{i=1}^{R}A_{i}\otimes B_{i}\right)
is not identically zero.
It is interesting that \sigma(N,R) even exists. The bound \sigma(N,R)\leq(N+1)! was used in [14], but a better bound appeared afterwards.
Theorem 49 (Derksen, Makam [7]).
\sigma(N,R)\leq N-1.
We now prove our lower bound.
Theorem 50.
Suppose T,P,Q are of bit-complexity at most b as per Definition 20. If \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)>0, then
\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)\geq\exp(-10b).
Proof.
Choose an integer N\leq 2^{b} such that \mathbf{p}^{\prime}=N\mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q}^{\prime}=N\mathbf{q} have integer entries and such that N^{2}T has Gaussian integer entries. First note that if T^{\prime} is the completely positive map obtained by scaling the Kraus operators of T by N, then the Kraus operators of T^{\prime} have Gaussian integer entries and \operatorname{cap}(T^{\prime},\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q})=N^{2}\operatorname{cap}(T,\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}). By Proposition 32, \operatorname{trun}_{NP,NQ}T^{\prime} has N^{2}p_{1}q_{1}r\leq N^{2}r many N\times N Kraus operators filled with Gaussian integer entries. Further, \operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)>0 then \operatorname{trun}_{NP,NQ}T^{\prime} is rank-nondecreasing. By Theorem 47,
\operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{NP,NQ}T^{\prime}>e^{N\log(N^{6}r)}.
However, it’s not hard to check that
\operatorname{cap}(T^{\prime},P,Q)=\frac{1}{N}\operatorname{cap}(T^{\prime},NP,NQ)^{1/N},
and we know \operatorname{cap}\operatorname{trun}_{NP,NQ}T^{\prime}=\operatorname{cap}(T^{\prime},NP,NQ). Therefore
\operatorname{cap}(T,P,Q)\geq\frac{1}{N^{3}}e^{-\log(N^{6}r)}\geq e^{-9\log(Nr)}.
Using N\leq 2^{b} and b\geq\log_{2}r completes the proof. ∎
We also need to ensure that the capacity does not decrease too much after the first step of Algorithm 3. The proof is straightforward and is in Appendix A.

The extreme points of \mathcal{K}(T,E_{\bullet},F_{\bullet}) are rational and have worst-case bit-complexity depending polynomially on m and n; one runs the proof of Theorem 50 on the vertex where the minimum is attained. 5 General linear scalings
The proof of Theorem 19 is quite simple once we have the following lemma, which essentially says that if there are B and C such that T_{B,C} is be (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing, then T_{B,C} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing for generic B,C.
Definition 53.
The set of common zeroes of a collection of polynomials in \mathbb{C}[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}] is called an affine variety in \mathbb{C}^{d}. We say a property holds for generic x\in S if it holds for all x in S\setminus V for some fixed affine variety V not containing S.
Lemma 54.
The set of pairs (B^{\dagger},C)\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C}\times\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C} such that T_{B,C} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing is the complement of an affine variety V(T,P,Q). Further, if P and Q have integral spectra, then V(T,P,Q) is generated by finitely many polynomials of degree at most 2(\operatorname{Tr}P)^{2}.

5.2 Correctness of Algorithm 2
Here we show that Algorithm 2 works in polynomially many steps. This essentially follows from the Schwarz-Zippel lemma combined with the Derksen’s degree bound in Theorem 49.
Proposition 56.
Suppose T,P,Q have bit-complexity at most b and that T is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{m}\to Q,I_{n}\to P). If \epsilon<1 and
TIME\geq\frac{400bm}{\min\{q_{m},p_{n}\}\epsilon^{2}}
then Algorithm 2 outputs ERROR with probability at most 1/3.
Proof.
Suppose T is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P). By Theorem 19, T_{g_{0},h_{0}} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing for generic (g_{0}^{\dagger},h_{0})\in\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}). Since P,Q have bit-complexity at most b, there is a number \gamma\leq 2^{b} such that \gamma P,\gamma Q have integral spectra. In particular, by Lemma 54,
V^{\prime}=V(T,P,Q)\cup\{B:\det B=0\}\cup\{C:\det C=0\}
is an affine algebraic variety in \operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C}\times\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C} generated by polynomials of degree at most \max\{2\gamma^{2},n,m\}\leq 2\cdot 2^{2b} that does not contain all of \operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C}).
There must be some polynomial p:\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C}\times\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C} of at degree at most 2\cdot 2^{2b} that vanishes on V^{\prime} but not on all of \operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C}\times\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C}). By the Schwarz-Zippel lemma, p vanishes on our random choice of (g_{0},h_{0})\in\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C}\times\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C} with entries in 6\cdot 2^{2b} with probability at most 1/3. With probability at least 2/3 we have found g_{0},h_{0} such that T_{g_{0},h_{0}} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing.
The bit-complexity of the entries of T_{g_{0},h_{0}} is at most \log(m)+\log(n)+3b\leq 4b. The rest of the algorithm is the same as Algorithm 3, except we accept only when T_{g_{j},h_{j}} is an \epsilon-(P\to I_{m},Q\to I_{n})-scaling of T, rather than the less stringent requirement that \operatorname{ds}_{P,Q}T_{g_{j},h_{j}}<\epsilon. However, as we remarked in Definiton 35, if \operatorname{ds}_{P,Q}T_{g_{j},h_{j}}<\epsilon^{\prime}=\min\{p_{n},q_{m}\}\epsilon^{2} then T_{g_{j},h_{j}} is an \epsilon-scaling of T. This gives the required upper bound on TIME.
∎
Remark 57 (Numerical issues).
We should not expect to be able to compute each step of Algorithm 3 exactly, but rather to polynomially many bits of precision. The previous version of this paper had a rather messy analysis of the rounding; [5] contains a much more pleasant analysis, which we now sketch.
In each iteration of Algorithm 3, simply compute g (resp. h) to some precision 2^{-t} (ensuring that they are upper triangular). We need to check that progress is still made per step and that the capacity is bounded above the entire time. For this we need to emulate the proof of Lemma 40 with some error; it is enough to show that the rounded h^{\prime} satisfies 1/\det(Q,h^{-\dagger}h^{-1})\approx 1/\det(Q,h^{\prime-\dagger}h^{\prime-1}). Since h^{-\dagger}h^{-1}=T^{*}(Q), it is enough to show that the least eigenvalues of T^{*}(Q) (resp T(P)) stays bounded singly exponentially away from zero throughout. If this holds, the capacity is also bounded because T(P)\approx I or T^{*}(Q)\approx I. Fortunately, t can be chosen \operatorname{poly}(TIME,p_{n}^{-1},q_{m}^{-1},m,n,b) such that the least eigenvalues do stay large enough throughout.

\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{t}\operatorname{GL}_{m_{i}}(\mathbb{C})
and \operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C})=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{s}\operatorname{GL}_{n_{j}}(\mathbb{C}). For i\in[t], define

6.1 Matrix Scaling
Say XAY is an \epsilon-(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scaling of a nonnegative matrix A if X and Y are diagonal matrices and the and column sum vectors of XAY are at most \epsilon from \mathbf{r} and \mathbf{c}, respectively, in (say) Euclidean distance and that A is approximately (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scalable if for every \epsilon>0 there exists an \epsilon-(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scaling of A. Given A,r,c, the (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scaling problem consists of deciding the existence of and finding \epsilon-(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scalings. The (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scaling problem has practical applications such as statistics, numerical analysis, engineering, and image reconstruction, and theoretical uses such as strongly polynomial time algorithms for approximating the permanent [29], [28],[24].
There is a simple criterion for approximate (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scalability.
Theorem 63 (Rothblum and Schneider [28]).
A nonnegative matrix A is approximately (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}) scalable if and only if \sum_{i}r_{i}=\sum_{j}c_{j} and for every zero submatrix L\times R of A,
\sum_{i\in L}r_{i}\leq\sum_{j\not\in R}c_{j}.
We can reduce this to an instance of Question 1 as follows:
Definition 64.
Suppose A is a nonnegative m\times n matrix. For i\in[m],j\in[n], define e_{ij} to be the m\times n matrix with a one in the ij entry and zeros elsewhere. Let T_{A}:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C} be the completely positive map with Kraus operators E_{ij}=\sqrt{A_{ij}}e_{ij}, i\in[m],j\in[n].
If \mathbf{m}=(1,\dots,1) and \mathbf{n}=(1,\dots,1), then T_{A} is (\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})-block-diagonal. The next proposition is easy to check.
Proposition 65.
A is approximately (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scalable if and only if T_{A} is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to
(I_{n}\to\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{r}),I_{m}\to\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{c})).
Theorem 63 follows easily from Theorem 62.
Proof of Theorem 63.
By Proposition 65, it is enough to characterize (\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{r}),\to\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{c}))-rank-nondecreasingness of T_{A}. Since \mathbb{C}^{m_{i}} and \mathbb{C}^{n_{i}} are copies of \mathbb{C}, L_{i},R_{j} are either \{0\} or \mathbb{C}, E_{\bullet}(i)=(\{0\},\mathbb{C}) and F_{\bullet}(j)=(\{0\},\mathbb{C}). Let L=\{i:L_{i}=\mathbb{C}\}\subset[m] and R=\{j:R_{j}=\mathbb{C}\}\subset[n]. Note that \mathbf{L} and \mathbf{R} are block-T_{A}-independent if and only if T\times L is a zero submatrix of A, and the group elements \mathbf{g} and \mathbf{h} make no difference. Equation 21 becomes
\sum_{i\in L}c_{i}+\sum_{j\in R}r_{i}\leq\sum_{j\in[n]}r_{i},
which is equivalent to the condition for approximate (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scalability in 63.

6.2 Eigenvalues of Sums of Hermitian Matrices
Here is an old question in linear algebra, apparently originally due to Weyl. It is also sometimes called Horn’s Problem.
Question 66 (Weyl).
Let \alpha,\beta,\gamma be nonincreasing sequences of m real numbers. When are \alpha,\beta,\gamma the spectra of some m\times m Hermitian matrices A,B,C satisfying A+B=C?
This question essentially asks for a complete list of inequalities satisfied by the eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian matrices. Klyachko showed a relationship between the eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian operators and certain constants known as the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients.
Definition 67.
If I=\{i_{1}<\dots<i_{k}\}\subset[m], let \rho(I) be the partition
\rho(I)=(i_{k}-k,\dots,i_{2}-2,i_{1}-1).
The Littlewood-Richardson coefficient of the partitions \lambda,\mu, and \nu is a nonnegative integer denoted c^{\lambda}_{\mu,\nu}.
Theorem 68 (Klyachko [21]).
The three nonincreasing sequences \alpha,\beta,\gamma of length m are the spectra of some m\times m Hermitian matrices A,B,C satisfying A+B=C if and only if \sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}+\beta_{i}-\gamma_{i}=0
and for all n<m,
\sum_{i\in I}\alpha_{i}+\sum_{j\in J}\beta_{j}\leq\sum_{k\in K}\gamma_{k}
for all |I|=|J|=|K|=n such that the Littlewood-Richardson coefficient c^{\rho(K)}_{\rho(I),\rho(J)} is positive.
Though computing c^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu} is \#P-hard, there exists an algorithm to decide if c^{\nu}_{\lambda,\mu}>0 in strongly polynomial time [26].
Combined with Theorem 68, Knutson and Tao’s answer to the Saturation conjecture in the positive [22] and a different result of Klyachko [21] show that the admissible spectra are described by a recursive system of inequalities originally conjectured by Alfred Horn [19].
We show that Question 66 can be reduced to an instance of Question 1, after which Theorem 68 will be a corollary of our main theorem. This results in an algorithmic proof of Theorem 68.
We can restate Question 66 for more than 3 matrices. We will instead search for tuples of matrices with given spectra that add to a multiple of the identity; this is equivalent because we may subtract the target matrix from both sides and then add suitable scalar multiples of the identity to left and right hand side. This also shows it is enough to find the matrices when all the spectra are positive.
Question 69.
For which tuples (\mathbf{p}(1)\dots\mathbf{p}(s)) of length-m weakly decreasing sequences positive numbers do there exist positive-definite m\times m Hermitian matrices H_{i}, i\in[s] such that \lambda(H_{i})=\mathbf{p}(s) and
\sum_{i=1}^{t}H_{i}=I_{m}?
Klyachko answered Question 69 in terms of intersections of Schubert varieties with respect to generic flags, which can in turn be described by the combinatorially defined higher Littlewood-Richardson coefficients c^{\lambda}_{\mu_{1},\dots\mu_{r}}. Here is the statement we wish to reprove via operator scaling. We do not show that it is the same as Theorem 68, but instead refer the reader to [12]. Let \mathbf{n}=(m,\dots,m), be a sequence of s many m^{\prime}s. Let \operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C}) and F_{\bullet}(i),i\in[s] be as in Definition 58.
Theorem 70 (Klyachko, [21]).
The answer to Question 69 is positive if and only if \operatorname{Tr}P=m and for a generic tuple
\mathbf{h}=(h(1),\dots,h(s))\in\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C}),
the inequality
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Delta p_{j}(i)\dim R\cap h(i)F_{j}(i)\leq\dim R
(22)
holds for all R\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}.
We now reduce Question 69 to Question 59.
Definition 71.
Let n=ms, and again let \mathbf{n}=(m,\dots,m), be a sequence of s many m^{\prime}s, and let \mathbf{m}=(m).
Define T_{m}^{s}:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}\mathbb{C}\to\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}\mathbb{C} to be the (\mathbf{m},\mathbf{n})-block-diagonal competely positive map with Kraus operators
A_{i}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0_{m,mi-m}&I_{m}&0_{m,mi}\end{array}\right]
for i\in[s].
Proposition 72.
The answer to Question 69 is positive for the tuple
(\mathbf{p}(1)\dots\mathbf{p}(s)) if and only if T_{m}^{s} is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C})-scalable to (I_{n}\to I_{m},I_{m}\to P).
Proof.
Set T:=T_{m}^{s}.
First we prove the “only if” statement. Suppose there exist H_{1},\dots,H_{r} with \lambda(H_{i})=\mathbf{p}(i) and \sum_{i}H_{i}=I_{m}. As H_{i}\succeq 0, we can write H_{i}=B_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger} where B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}=P(i). This is because B_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger} and B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i} have the same spectrum and B_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger} is invariant under B_{i}\to B_{i}U_{i} for U_{i} unitary. Since B_{i} is invertible for i\in[r], it follows that \mathbf{h}=\bigoplus_{i\in[r]}B_{i}\in\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C}) and
T_{I,\mathbf{h}}(I_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{s}B_{i}B_{i}^{\dagger}=I_{m}\textrm{ and }(T_{I,\mathbf{h}})^{*}(I_{m})=\oplus_{i=1}^{s}B_{i}^{\dagger}B_{i}=P.
so T is approximately \operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{m}}(\mathbb{C})\times\operatorname{GL}_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbb{C}) scalable to (P,I).
The “if” direction is also easy; suppose (\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{h}_{k}) is a sequence of elements of G\times H such that
T_{\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{h}_{k}}(I_{n})\to I_{m}\textrm{ and }(T_{\mathbf{g}_{k},\mathbf{h}_{k}})^{*}(I_{m})\to P
as k\to\infty
and that \mathbf{h}_{k}=\oplus_{i\in[s]}h_{k}(i). Set B_{k}(i)=\mathbf{g}_{k}^{\dagger}h_{k}(i); thus, we have
\sum_{i\in[s]}B_{k}(i)B_{k}(i)^{\dagger}\to I_{m}
and, for all i\in[s],
B_{k}(i)^{\dagger}B_{k}(i)\to P(i).
Since the B_{k}(i)B_{k}(i)^{\dagger} are positive definite, eventually for all i,
B_{k}(i)B_{k}(i)^{\dagger} remains in the compact set \{X:0\leq X\leq 2I_{m}\}. Thus, we may pass to a subsequence such that for all i\in[s] we have B_{k}(i)B_{k}(i)^{\dagger}\to H_{i}; by continuity the H_{i} satisfy
\sum_{i=1}^{s}H_{i}=I_{m}.
and \lambda(H_{i})=\mathbf{p}(i) for i\in[s].∎

6.3 Extensions of Theorems of Barthe and Schur-Horn
Let \mathbf{U}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{n} be an ordered tuple of complex m-vectors, and \mathbf{p}=(p_{1},\dots,p_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}. Say a linear transformation B:\mathbb{C}^{m}\to\mathbb{C}^{m} puts a collection of vectors \mathbf{U} in radial isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p} if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\frac{Bu_{i}(Bu_{i})^{\dagger}}{\|Bu_{i}\|^{2}}=I.
Question 73.
Given \mathbf{U} and \mathbf{p}, when is there a linear transformation B that puts \mathbf{U} in isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p}?
Barthe showed Question 73 has a positive answer if p lies in a certain polytope, which we now describe.
Definition 74.
Let \mathbf{U}=(u_{1},\dots,u_{n})\in(\mathbb{C}^{m})^{n} be an ordered tuple of complex m-vectors. Let \mathbf{B}\subset\binom{[n]}{k} be the collection of m-subsets S of [n] such that \{u_{i}:i\in S\} forms a basis of \mathbb{C}^{m}. If \mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m}) is a sequence of nonnegative numbers, define
\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{U})=\operatorname{conv}\{(1_{S}(i)q_{\sigma}(i):i\in[n]):S\in\mathbf{B},\sigma:S\leftrightarrow[m]\}
Informally, each vertex of the polytope is the indicator vector for each basis in \mathbf{U} with the nonzero entries replaced by q_{1},\dots q_{m} in some order. If \mathbf{q} is the all-ones vector, B(\mathbf{U}):=\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{U}) is known as the basis polytope.
Theorem 75 (Barthe [2]).
\mathbf{p}\in B(\mathbf{U}) if and only if there are linear transformations B that put \mathbf{U} arbitrarily close to radial isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p}.
Further, if p is in the relative interior of B(\mathbf{U}), then there are linear transformations B that put \mathbf{p} in radial isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p}.
As a partial answer to Question 5, we prove a generalization of Barthe’s Theorem.
Definition 76.
Say \mathbf{U} can be approximately put in Q-isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p} if for every \epsilon>0 there exists an invertible linear transformation B
such that
\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\frac{Bu_{i}(Bu_{i})^{\dagger}}{\|Bu_{i}\|}-Q\right\|\leq\epsilon.
(25)
Theorem 77.
Suppose Q is a positive-definite matrix with spectrum \mathbf{q}=(q_{1},\dots,q_{m}). \mathbf{U} can be approximately put in Q-isotropic position with respect to \mathbf{p} if and only if \mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{U}).
Theorem 77 follows immediately from the next two propositions. The first follows from Theorem 19, and the second from Edmonds’ work on polymatroids.
Proposition 78.
\bm{U} can be approximately put in Q-isotropic position with respect to \bm{p}
if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}
and
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{j}:j\in J\rangle}q_{i}
for all J\subset[n].
Proposition 79.
\bm{p} is in \mathcal{K}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}) if and only if
\sum_{j=1}^{n}p_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}
(26)
and
\sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{j}:j\in J\rangle}q_{i}
(27)
for all J\subset[n].
Proof of Proposition 79.
Polytopes of the form \sum_{j\in J}p_{j}\leq f(J) for submodular set functions f are well-understood, so we first check that our constraints take this form.
Lemma 80.
The function
f_{\bm{q}}(J)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{j}:j\in J\rangle}q_{j}&J\neq\emptyset\\
f_{\bm{q}}(J)=0&J=\emptyset\end{array}\right.
(28)
is a nonnegative, monotone, submodular function on the lattice of subsets of [n].
Proof of Lemma 80.
Nonnegativity and monotonicity are clear. To show that f_{\bm{q}} is submodular, it is enough to show f_{\bm{q}} gives decreasing marginal returns, that is, for X\subset Y and x\in[n]\setminus Y,
f_{\bm{q}}(X\cup\{j\})-f_{\bm{q}}(X)\geq f_{\bm{q}}(Y\cup\{j\})-f_{\bm{q}}(Y).
Indeed,
\displaystyle f_{\bm{q}}(X\cup\{j\})-f_{\bm{q}}(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{\dim(\langle u_{i}:i\in X\rangle+u_{j})}q_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in X\rangle}q_{i}
\displaystyle=1_{u_{j}\not\in\langle u_{i}:i\in X\rangle}q_{\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in X\rangle+1}\geq 1_{u_{j}\not\in\langle u_{i}:i\in Y\rangle}q_{\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in Y\rangle+1}
\displaystyle=f_{\bm{q}}(Y\cup\{j\})-f_{\bm{q}}(Y).
∎
Next we use a theorem of Edmonds.
Theorem 81 ([8]).
If E is a finite set and L is an intersection-closed family of subsets of E, let
P(E,f)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{E}_{+}:\forall S\in L-\emptyset,\;\sum_{i\in S}x_{i}\leq f(S)\}.
If f is a nonnegative, monotone function on 2^{E} with f(\emptyset)=0, then each vertex x of P(E,f) is given by
x_{\sigma}(i)=f(\{\sigma(j):j\leq i\})-f(\{\sigma(j):j<i\})
for some ordering \sigma:[|E|]\leftrightarrow E, and every ordering corresponds to such a vertex.
Next note that p satisfies the equality 26 and the inequality 27 if and only if
p\in\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}):=P([n],f_{\bm{q}})\cap\left\{p:\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right\}.
For x\in P([n],f_{\bm{q}}), \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i} by the constraint when J=[n]. Thus, \mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}) is the convex hull of the vertices of P([n],f_{\lambda}) that are contained in the hyperplane \left\{p:\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}\right\}. Recall from Definition 74 that these are exactly the vertices of \mathcal{K}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}); hence \mathcal{K}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U})=\mathcal{K}^{\prime}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}). The proposition is proved.
∎
Proof of Lemma 78.
Consider the completely positive map T_{\bm{U}}:\operatorname{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})\to\operatorname{Mat}_{m\times m}(\mathbb{C}) with Kraus operators
\displaystyle A_{i}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}0_{m,i-1}&u_{i}&0_{m,n-i}\end{array}\right]
for i\in[n]. Here 0_{m,k} denotes an m\times k zero submatrix. Then it is not hard to see that \bm{U} can be approximately put in Q-isotropic position with respect to p if and only if T_{\bm{U}} is approximately (G=\operatorname{GL}_{m}(\mathbb{C}),H=\operatorname{GL}_{\bm{n}}(\mathbb{C}))-scalable to (I_{n}\to Q,I_{m}\to P), where \bm{n}=(1,\dots,1). That is, H is the group of diagonal invertible complex matrices.
By Theorem 19, T_{\bm{U}} is approximately (G,H)-scalable to (P,Q) if and only if (T_{\bm{U}})_{g,h} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing for a generic (g^{\dagger},h)\in G\times H. However, multiplication by h doesn’t affect (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasingness because h is diagonal, so T_{\bm{U}} is approximately (G,H)-scalable to (P,Q) if and only if (T_{\bm{U}})_{g,I_{n}}=T_{g\bm{U}} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing for a generic g\in G.
By Theorem 61, we need only check that (T_{g\bm{U}}) is block-(P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing. Since the R_{i} are one dimensional subspaces, only S=\{i:L_{i}=\{0\}\} matters. Further, by maximality, we may assume L=\langle u_{i}:i\in S\rangle^{\perp}. Thus, T_{g\bm{U}} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing if and only if
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}+\sum_{i=1}^{m}\Delta q_{i}\langle gu_{i}:i\in S\rangle^{\perp}\cap F_{i}\leq\operatorname{Tr}Q
for all S\subset[n]. For generic g, the left-hand side of the above inequality is equal to
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}+\sum_{i=\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in S\rangle^{\perp}+1}^{m}q_{i}
for all S\subset[n]; thus, T_{g\bm{U}} is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing for a generic g\in G if and only if
\sum_{j\in S}p_{j}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in S\rangle}q_{i}
for all S\subset[n].∎
Remark 82 (Algorithms).
Because \mathcal{K}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}) is a polymatroid and we an compute \sum_{i=1}^{\dim\langle u_{i}:i\in S\rangle}q_{i} from S easily, we can easily test if \bm{p}\in\mathcal{K}_{\bm{q}}(\bm{U}) [8]. However, it is not clear if we can put \bm{U} in Q-isotropic position with respect to \bm{p} in time \operatorname{poly}(m,n,b,\log(1/\epsilon)). The \operatorname{poly}(m,n,b,\log(1/\epsilon)) algorithm for the Q=I case in [30] suggests this may be possible; one just needs to minimize the convex program
\inf_{\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\log\det\left(Q,\sum_{i=1}^{n}e^{t_{i}}u_{i}u_{i}^{\dagger}\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}t_{i}.
We wonder if this can also be formulated in terms of relative entropy.
It’s not too hard to see that Theorem 77 implies the more difficult “if” direction of the classic Schur-Horn theorem relating the diagonal and spectra of a Hermitian matrix.
Theorem 83 (Schur-Horn [18]).
There is a Hermitian n\times n matrix with diagonal p_{1}\geq\dots\geq p_{n} and spectrum q_{1}\geq\dots\geq q_{n} if and only if q_{1},\dots,q_{n} majorizes p_{1},\dots,p_{n}. That is, for all i\leq n,
\sum_{j=1}^{i}p_{i}\leq\sum_{j=1}^{i}q_{i}.
To prove the Schur-Horn theorem, simply pick \mathbf{U} to be in general position - note that \mathcal{K}_{\mathbf{q}}(\mathbf{U}) is then the permutohedron of the vector (q_{1},\dots,q_{m},0,\dots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}, which is precisely the set of \mathbf{p} that is majorized by (q_{1},\dots,q_{m},0,\dots,0)!
7 Future Work
We wonder if in this setting there is an algorithm to find approximate scalings in time polynomial in -\log(\epsilon) rather than \epsilon^{-1}. As it is, our algorithm resembles alternating minimization; perhaps other optimization techniques could result in faster algorithms. The recent fast (\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c})-scaling algorithms [1], [6] give hope that this is possible.
Finding a \operatorname{poly}\log(1/\epsilon) algorithm has another benefit: the algorithms herein are not capable of deciding (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasingness in strongly polynomial time. By Corollary 44, in order to certify (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasingness one requires \epsilon-(I_{m}\to Q,I_{n}\to P)-scalings for \epsilon as small as a common denominator of all the entries of \mathbf{p} and \mathbf{q}. However, our algorithm depends polynomially on \epsilon^{-1}. In fact, this decision problem was shown to be in \mathbf{NP}\cap\mathbf{coNP} and is conjectured to be in \mathbf{P}; at least for 3 there is a strongly polynomial time algorithm to decide if the reduction is (P,Q)-rank-nondecreasing that has nothing to do with operator scaling [26].
