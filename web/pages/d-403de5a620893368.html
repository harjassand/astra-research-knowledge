Abstract.
In this paper, we consider the Rosenthal – Halmos problem of almost commuting matrices with respect to the normalized Hilbert – Schmidt norm ∥⋅∥2,d=d−1/2∥⋅∥2\|\cdot\|_{2,d}=d^{-1/2}\|\cdot\|_{2}. We show that if XX and YY are self-adjoint matrices with ‖X‖≤1\|X\|\leq 1, ‖Y‖≤1\|Y\|\leq 1, then there exist commuting self-adjoint matrices X′,Y′X^{\prime},Y^{\prime} such that ‖X−X′‖2,d+‖Y−Y′‖2,d≤5​‖[X,Y]‖2,d1/3\|X-X^{\prime}\|_{2,d}+\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|_{2,d}\leq 5\|[X,Y]\|_{2,d}^{1/3}, and [X,X′]=0[X,X^{\prime}]=0. Within these constraints, the exponent 1/31/3 cannot be improved.
1. Introduction and main results
The topic of almost commuting matrices dates back to Rosenthal [23] and Halmos [18], with the original question that can be traced back to von Neumann [26, 27].
Suppose, XX and YY are two self-adjoint matrices such their commutator [X,Y][X,Y] is small in some way. Can one find commuting matrices X′,Y′X^{\prime},Y^{\prime} that are close to XX and YY, respectively, in the same way? Many versions and generalizations of this question exist, depending on the notion of smallness and specific types of matrices, with two main paths to obtaining positive results. Abstract approaches usually involve proofs by contradiction. That is, non-existence of a dimension-independent estimate leads to an element with impossible properties in a certain C∗C^{*}-algebra associated to sequences of matrices. Quantitative approaches, on the other hand, usually deal with the original matrices directly, in a more model-specific way, but are often still motivated by C∗C^{*}-algebraic intuition.
In the case when smallness/closeness is understood in terms of the operator norm, a major breakthrough was obtained by Lin in [22], resulting in an abstract approach implying the the existence of a dimension-independent distance estimate to commuting pairs in terms of ‖[X,Y]‖\|[X,Y]\|. The proof was later simplified in [8]. A quantitative result demonstrating existence of X′,Y′X^{\prime},Y^{\prime} such that
(1.1)
‖X−X′‖+‖Y−Y′‖≤C​‖[X,Y]‖1/2\|X-X^{\prime}\|+\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|\leq C\|[X,Y]\|^{1/2}
was obtained much later in [21] (see also [17, 19]), where the exponent 1/21/2 cannot be improved further.
The original question of Rosenthal was posed in terms of the Hilbert – Schmidt norm. For the purpose of dimension-independent estimates, one naturally arrives to normalized Hilbert–Schmidt norm
(1.2)
‖X‖2,d2:=1d​∑i,j=1d|Ai​j|2,X∈Md.\|X\|_{{2,d}}^{2}:=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j=1}^{d}|A_{ij}|^{2},\quad X\in{\mathrm{M}}_{d}.
The first result that matches Lin’s theorem in this setting was obtained in [13] (see also [16, 14, 15]), in a bigger generality that allows to deal with an arbitrary finite number of almost commuting matrices. An independent proof for two matrices was obtained in [7]. In both cases, the proofs are of an abstract existence-only kind. The first quantitative result involving the norm (1.2) was obtained in [11], providing an analogue of (1.1) with the exponent 1/61/6 in the right hand side. The exponent was later improved to 1/41/4 in [7] (see also [24, 9]). In these results, the key feature is that X′X^{\prime} commutes with XX, which allows to iterate the construction in order to extend the estimates to a larger number of almost commuting matrices with meaningful distance estimates.
In the present paper, we study the Rosenthal – Halmos problem with respect to the norm (1.2), under the additional constraint that X′X^{\prime} must commute with XX, motivated by [11, 5]. Our main result is Theorem 1.1 below, which obtains a distance estimate with an exponent 1/31/3. As Theorem 1.2 shows, this exponent is sharp. Unlike [11, 5], the conclusion of Theorem 1.1 cannot be immediately iterated in order to cover more than two almost commuting matrices. However, an appropriate modification is obtained in our Theorem 1.3.

1.2. Three or more matrices
The complexity of operator norm version of the Rosenthal – Halmos problem increases drastically, once one departs from the case of two self-adjoint matrices. Indeed, topological obstructions appear in the case of two unitary matrices [25, 4, 12, 3] as well as in the case of three self-adjoint matrices [2, 10], and the situation is only expected to become harder in more involved cases. On the other hand, as first observed in [13], many of these obstructions do not appear in the case of the normalized Hilbert – Schmidt norm (however, we refer the reader to [1, 20] for some more delicate cases). As was noted in [11, Theorem 3] (see also [5, Theorem 3]), the constructions of [11] and [5] can be iterated in order to obtain quantitative bounds for arbitrary number of mutually almost commuting matrices.
The above iterations rely on the fact that the operator X′X^{\prime} in [11, 5] does not depend on YY, which does not apply directly to our case of Theorem 1.1. However, one can observe by inspecting the proof of Theorem 1.1 that the discrete optimization part of the proof can be modified in order to treat a kk-tuple of matrices YY instead of a single one, as follows.
Theorem 1.3.
For any X,Y1,…,YN∈BdX,Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N}\in{\mathrm{B}}_{d}, there exist X′,Y1′,…,YN′∈BdX^{\prime},Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{N}^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{B}}_{d} such that
[X′,Y1′]=…=[X′,YN′]=[X′,X]=0,[X^{\prime},Y_{1}^{\prime}]=\ldots=[X^{\prime},Y_{N}^{\prime}]=[X^{\prime},X]=0,
and
‖X−X′‖2,d2+‖Y1−Y1′‖2,d2+…+‖YN−YN′‖2,d2≤24​N​(‖[X,Y1]‖2,d2+…+‖[X,YN]‖2,d2)1/3.\|X-X^{\prime}\|_{2,d}^{2}+\|Y_{1}-Y_{1}^{\prime}\|_{2,d}^{2}+\ldots+\|Y_{N}-Y_{N}^{\prime}\|_{2,d}^{2}\leq 24\sqrt{N}\left(\|[X,Y_{1}]\|^{2}_{2,d}+\ldots+\|[X,Y_{N}]\|^{2}_{2,d}\right)^{1/3}.
As mentioned above, the proof of Theorem 1.3 follows the proof of Theorem 1.1. In order to avoid both unnecessary repetitions and over-generalizing the main result, we explain the relevant modifications after each step of the proof of Theorem 1.1, in Remarks 2.2, 3.5, and 4.1.
Similarly to [11, 5], the reader can now iterate Theorem 1.3 for the matrices Y1′,…,YN′Y_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,Y_{N}^{\prime} restricted into the eigenspaces of XX, without violating commutation with XX.
Corollary 1.4.
For any X1,…,XN∈BdX_{1},\ldots,X_{N}\in{\mathrm{B}}_{d}, there exist commuting matrices X1′,…,XN′∈BdX_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,X_{N}^{\prime}\in{\mathrm{B}}_{d} such that
(1.6)
‖X−X1′‖2,d2+…+‖XN−XN′‖2,d2≤C⁡(N)​maxi,j​(‖[Xi,Xj]‖2,d)23N−1,\|X-X_{1}^{\prime}\|_{2,d}^{2}+\ldots+\|X_{N}-X_{N}^{\prime}\|_{2,d}^{2}\leq C(N)\max_{i,j}\left(\|[X_{i},X_{j}]\|_{2,d}\right)^{\frac{2}{3^{N-1}}},
where C⁡(N)C(N) only depends on NN.
While (1.6) improves on the previously known results, we do not expect it to be optimal.
1.3. Structure of the paper
In Section 2 we show that, if one additionally prescribes the multiplicities of the eigenvalues of X′X^{\prime}, then the of X′X^{\prime} and Y′Y^{\prime} can be obtained by an explicitly solvable optimization problem, thus reducing the original question to optimizing the choice of said multiplicities, which is a discrete optimization problem.
In Section 3 we discuss two technical lemmas, which collectively conclude that, without loss of generality one can assume that XX has no large spectral gaps.
In Section 4 we prove the main result, Theorem 1.1. The main idea is based on carefully estimating a particularly chosen weighted average of the solution candidates to the discrete optimization problem, thus providing an upper bound on the actual minimizer.
In Section 5, we use the ideas obtained from Section 2 to prove Theorem 1.2, which provides a lower bound on the exponent in Theorem 1.1.
As mentioned in the previous subsections, the proof of Theorem 1.3 is contained in Remarks 2.2, 3.5, and 4.1
With all the preparations, the proof of Corollary 1.4 is a straightforward consequence of the iteration argument described in the same subsection. We refer the reader to [5] for additional details.
2. An optimization solution
Since X′X^{\prime} commutes with XX, they can be jointly diagonalized by a unitary conjugation. As the conjugation preserves all norms under consideration, one can assume without loss of generality that both XX and X′X^{\prime} are diagonal. Additionally, by approximation, one can also assume that all eigenvalues of XX are distinct. Therefore, let us denote
Bddiag:={X∈Md(ℂ):X=X∗,∥X∥≤1,Xis diagonal with simple spectrum}.{\mathrm{B}}^{\operatorname{diag}}_{d}:=\{X\in{\mathrm{M}}_{d}({\mathbb{C}})\colon X=X^{*},\,\|X\|\leq 1,\,\,X\,\text{is diagonal with simple spectrum}\}.

X′=diag⁡{t1,…,td},−1≤t1≤…≤td≤1.X^{\prime}=\operatorname{diag}\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{d}\},\quad-1\leq t_{1}\leq\ldots\leq t_{d}\leq 1.

Indeed, otherwise Y′Y^{\prime} would also have to be diagonal, which is too wasteful for the purpose of minimizing ‖Y−Y′‖2,d\|Y-Y^{\prime}\|_{2,d}. 3. Dimension reductions
In this section, we will describe two cases in which the problem can be reduced to that in a smaller dimension. The first lemma is, essentially a restatement of the fact that ∥⋅∥2,d\|\cdot\|_{2,d} “scales correctly” with the dimension for the purpose of the question of almost commuting matrices.
