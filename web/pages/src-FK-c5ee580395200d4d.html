# Carrier-gated dislocation reactions: primary-source audit

**Scope.** Literature audit of whether carrier-dependent dislocation motion has already been used to select a reaction and leave a verified lower-defect state. No experiment or calibrated material simulation was performed. The source claims, derived arithmetic, and proposed test below are kept separate.

## Finding

The ZnS studies establish two real control levers: electric-field polarity moves selected charged partials in opposite directions, and 365 nm illumination changes the relative glide mobility of Zn-rich and S-rich dislocations. **No same-platform ZnS operation in the audited studies demonstrates selective annihilation.** They do not show an illumination- or field-selected annihilation reaction. The closest direct semiconductor precedent is GaAs/Si under recombination-enhanced glide: Callahan et al. imaged one threading-dislocation pair reacting and interpreted the event as annihilation, leaving a short interfacial segment. That experiment did not compare reaction outcomes under a chosen carrier gate and a matched control.

Thus, “carriers can move dislocations,” “carriers can change which core class moves faster,” and “carriers select a reaction that leaves a retained repair” are three different claims. The first two have primary experimental support. Carrier-assisted area filtering is also prior art: Hughes et al. report a beam-driven decline in the count of mobile threads leaving an imaged AlGaAs/Si area. The third claim—selection among reaction outcomes with the product verified after the gate is removed—remains unshown in the sources inspected. A narrower, testable contribution would be a measured change in the *outcome distribution of a geometrically identified, compatible dislocation pair* under a carrier condition, with its product checked after the stimulus is removed.

The field study also compares a modeled 3 V/nm field in its atomistic barrier calculation with experimental estimates around $10^7$ V/m. That modeled field is roughly 125–500 times the experimental field scale, so its calculated barrier reduction is not a quantitative experimental barrier measurement at the reported test field. The authors themselves note temperature dependence as open. The first field test’s inconsistent field estimates are another reason not to quote a precise universal threshold.

The optical paper reports a separate growth result, not a dynamic repair operation. Its 30 nm Al-doped ZnS films were grown on GaP. At $N_{Al}=4\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³, the reported Zn-core and S-core misfit line densities are 9.1 and 5.3 µm⁻¹; at $4\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ they are 0.9 and 5.0 µm⁻¹. This is about a 10.1-fold Zn-core reduction while S-core density stays near its earlier value. The authors explicitly distinguish donor doping from conventional current- or field-modulated electroplasticity and state that the measurements do not provide direct spectroscopic proof of the proposed core mechanism. It is a strong same-material growth comparator, but not evidence of post-growth carrier-gated annihilation.

The factor-of-20 and factor-of-5 relative mobility shifts above are arithmetic from the paper’s stated dark/light reductions: $100/5=20$ for 20 nm and $10/2=5$ for 25 nm. They are *not* reaction-probability gains. The reproducibility appendix uses two film thicknesses and binary light/dark comparisons but does not state an independent-sample count for each condition in the accessible v2 text. The main power sweep uses sequential cycles at one location; the cycle history and changing strain matter even though the authors normalize mobility by measured strain.

## The closest carrier-assisted reaction precedent

Callahan et al., *Physical Review Materials* 2, 081601(R) (2018), Figs. 1–4, directly observed recombination-enhanced dislocation glide in strained GaAs on Si using ECCI. Their 3.1 µm, unintentionally p-type GaAs film ((<10^{16}\) cm⁻³) was grown on on-axis (001) GaP/Si. Cooling left about 0.2% tensile strain, providing a mechanical driving force. The glide-set α misfit has an As-rich core and [\(1\bar{1}0\)] line direction; the β misfit has a Ga-rich core and [110] direction. The associated threading segments can have intermediate line directions. The paper does not index the two Fig. 4 reaction Burgers vectors numerically in the text; its caption says they differ.

For the main imaging condition, a 30 kV SEM beam at about 3.4–3.7 nA (roughly 1.9–2.0 mA/cm²) generated an estimated $10^{17}$–$10^{18}$ electron–hole pairs/cm³. A central region received about 35 minutes of raster exposure at room temperature. The observed influence extended 2–3 µm beyond the beam region; the paper attributes this to carrier diffusion lengths around 1–3 µm. Velocities varied widely with local interactions and repeated pinning/unpinning. At higher than 30 nA, more dislocations escaped at the surface. Temperature-dependent measurements ran from −20 °C to 80 °C; a 0.2 eV activation energy was estimated from the fastest, least-pinned lines, not from the population average.

In Fig. 4(a,b), two threads on parallel glide planes, with different Burgers vectors, approach from opposite glide directions and react at roughly 70 nm plane separation. A model cited by the authors puts the GaAs interaction radius around 50–100 nm. Cross-slip was considered likely and climb unlikely to close the separation within less than one 13.5-second frame. The authors interpret the lines vanishing from view as an annihilation reaction, leaving a short dislocation segment at the heterointerface; they call this direct evidence of individual annihilation. Fig. 4(c,d) separately shows lines leaving an etched mesa edge. These are meaningful reaction and removal precedents, but the study did not compare light/carrier schedules, did not demonstrate a selected reaction over a matched alternative, and did not test survival after carrier injection was stopped. Its beam also both creates carriers and images the structure. The authors report that REDG can increase overall network complexity even while individual reactions or surface exits reduce local defect content.

This sets a hard novelty boundary: carrier-assisted dislocation annihilation and carrier-assisted removal of mobile threads from a selected observation area are both prior art. A possible new claim must concern a measured, reproducible *bias in which compatible reaction occurs*, with the product and post-gate state identified; a mobility change, an isolated annihilation event, or an area-local count decline is insufficient.

Do not combine these operating windows into one demonstrated protocol. The 2023 ZnS field experiment is a tip-driven nanoscale TEM configuration with strong, nonuniform local fields and no stated specimen temperature. The ZnS optical experiment uses a different epitaxial geometry and 350 °C anneals followed by room-temperature imaging. The GaAs/Si experiment uses a 30 kV electron beam to generate carriers and a residual tensile stress at room temperature. Each is a distinct material state and stimulus.

## What is measurable now, and what would establish reaction selection

The control already measured most cleanly is the same-stress, core-conditioned relative mobility

\[
\Gamma(P)=\mu_{Zn}(P)/\mu_{S}(P).
\]

The 2026 source reports a change from $\Gamma>10$ at low illumination to nearly 1 at its highest optical power in the 20 nm sweep; its additional light/dark tests show relative shifts of roughly 20× and 5× for 20 and 25 nm films. This establishes non-proportional control of two actual dislocation classes, within that high-temperature, epitaxial regime. The next new quantity is not another maximum velocity. It is the conditional reaction outcome for a pre-identified pair, for example

\[
\Theta=\frac{\Pr(\text{verified target annihilation}\mid\text{light gate, mapped pair})}
{\Pr(\text{verified target annihilation}\mid\text{matched dark/thermal control, mapped pair})},
\]

with competing outcomes (junction formation, pinning, surface export, climb, motion out of view, and contrast loss) reported separately. No source supplies $\Theta$, a reaction yield per area, a retained-defect fraction after operation, or a processing throughput.

## Smallest decisive test

Use one platform, ZnS/GaP(001), because that is where the source already measures a core-specific optical response. Keep it within its evidenced regime rather than transferring field thresholds from a different ZnS TEM sample or carrier kinetics from GaAs/Si.

1. On an undoped 20–25 nm ZnS/GaP(001) specimen, map a sparse local network and identify a candidate interacting pair. Determine both full Burgers vectors, slip planes, core classes, signs, and starting separation before assigning a target reaction. The 20 nm source film has few threads and clean mobility measurements; the 25 nm comparison has more pinning and a smaller light/dark response. If no naturally occurring, geometrically compatible pair is found, stop: a carrier-selective reaction is not yet instantiated in this platform.
2. Randomly assign independent matched pairs or regions to a 350 °C dark anneal and a 350 °C, 365 nm light anneal. Measure the specimen temperature, hold time, optical intensity, illuminated area, and exposure duration. Use the source’s 0.07–0.67 mW/cm² range as a starting range, not a known dose recipe; its exposure duration and absorbed dose are absent. Include a temperature-matched dark control. If claiming schedule selection, add a constant-light arm with the same integrated photon fluence as the schedule arm; first measure whether the mobility response is fast enough for pulsing.
3. Keep the imaging beam off during the controlled anneal, as in the source’s post-cycle imaging workflow, and include an imaging-dose control because ECCI itself injects carriers. Reimage the same registered region after each independent cycle. Do not treat successive light/dark cycles on one evolving network as independent replicas.
4. After the gate is removed and the sample cools, identify the final topology under multiple diffraction conditions. Count annihilation only when the line pair is topologically resolved and the expected Burgers-vector closure/product is confirmed; distinguish this from a junction, an exported line, a hidden/out-of-view line, or contrast loss. Inspect any residual stacking fault or interfacial segment and record plastic strain, so zero net Burgers content is not mistaken for zero damage.
5. Compare reaction-outcome frequencies across independently prepared pairs, with sample size set from a predeclared meaningful effect and the wide pinning variability already observed in these sources. A pilot with one event can establish feasibility but cannot establish selection. A positive result would show a scoped mechanism; throughput and material-quality claims would still require an area/time and energy comparison against ZnS donor-doping and strain-engineered growth controls.

The practical pivot is therefore from “post-growth repair at useful throughput” to “does the already measured ZnS core-selective glide control change one mapped reaction outcome during a 350 °C strain-relaxation cycle?” If it does not, the evidence supports selective glide or growth engineering only. A maximum segment speed, a retained displacement, or a lower line density in a doped as-grown film cannot substitute for the missing reaction-outcome measurement.
