A decision-level thermal-emulation test—and a counterexample that prevents a false discovery
I did not establish a fundamentally new physical operation meeting the full mission. I developed a candidate that could answer a consequential, restricted experimental decision without reconstructing the microscopic mechanism. However, its first formulation failed an explicit physical counterexample, and the corrected formulation has not demonstrated an advantage over the strongest existing methods.

The strongest surviving candidate is this:

Construct an experimentally measured set of competing physical operations, then test whether the proposed operation achieves a decision-relevant outcome outside that set under the same resource constraints.

I developed this for light-driven catalysis: determining whether illumination provides useful chemical performance that an appropriately controlled thermal process cannot reproduce.

The substantive results are an explicit counterexample to a seemingly powerful measurement principle, a corrected mathematical certificate, a specified acquisition protocol, and executed synthetic tests. There are no new laboratory measurements in this result, and historical novelty is unestablished.

1. The consequential decision
For a light-driven catalytic process, two different questions are often entangled:

Mechanistic question: Does illumination alter chemistry through energetic charge carriers, electromagnetic fields, heating, or some combination?

Technological question: Does adding illumination achieve a useful product distribution, production rate, or resource efficiency that the strongest relevant heating-only alternative cannot achieve?

The second question can matter without a complete answer to the first. A nonthermal mechanism could exist but provide no useful engineering advantage. Conversely, light-induced heating could create a valuable operating regime even when no nonthermal chemistry is required.

I selected this problem after rejecting a simpler battery-diagnostic direction: nonlinear frequency-response measurements have already been used to identify lithium plating, so merely proposing higher-harmonic detection would not establish a new operation. 

For catalysis, the information-interface problem is concrete. A rhodium CO₂-hydrogenation study reported substantial changes in methane selectivity under illumination. Separately, thermal analyses have challenged interpretations of some plasmonic experiments by showing how temperature errors and gradients can imitate apparently nonthermal rate enhancements. These are competing interpretations to investigate—not grounds for assuming that either explanation universally wins. 

There are also strong experimental competitors. A 2025 study used operando X-ray diffraction to measure catalyst and support lattice temperatures during CO oxidation, and reported that heating explained the response for its particular catalyst and reaction. A new proposal must compete with such physical measurements, not merely with an inadequately placed thermometer. 

The decision I pursued was therefore:

For a specified catalyst device, feed, product requirement and resource budget, does illumination outperform the experimentally obtainable dark-control alternatives?

This deliberately avoids requiring a complete microscopic reconstruction.

A crucial asymmetry: an upper bound is not an executable emulator
The convex relaxation permits mixing operating modes. Physical switching can introduce heating delays, transient product release, catalyst memory, and extra energy consumption.

Those costs cannot be silently discarded.

But an interior point of the relaxation is not evidence that a matching dark process can actually be operated. A purported matching schedule must be replayed experimentally, including its switching and preparation costs.

4. The physical acquisition contract
The following is a proposed implementation, not a report of a constructed apparatus. Independently controlled gas-phase microreactor channels have published precedent, but the accuracy of the complete proposed arrangement has not been established here. 

Preparation and actuation
Use a small array of electrically heated, optically accessible reactor modules with separate gas channels and fixed catalyst inventories.

Each module is calibrated in place. Moving a catalyst between nominally equivalent reactors would introduce additional changes in thermal contact, transport and surface history.

The dark library consists of actual actuator settings—not ideal mathematical temperatures. A mode can initially be a constant heater setting after a measured settling interval. More complicated modes must be calibrated as complete protocols.

Separate channels make the required independence more physically defensible than treating particles inside one reacting powder bed as independent. Nevertheless, thermal cross-talk, shared feed limitations and pressure changes remain possible.

The decisive validation is to measure held-out combinations of module settings and compare their joint output with the sum predicted from individual calibrations. Failure invalidates the reduced model. A few successful combinations are evidence of consistency, not a universal proof of independence.

Product detection
Sample individual outlets and the pooled outlet while keeping flow resistance and pressure controlled.

A plausible detection arrangement combines mass spectrometry with chromatographic checks of product identity and cross-sensitivity. The required calibration includes background subtraction, water interference, detector saturation, carbon balance and response drift.

Resources and fairness
The accounting must include actual heater and illumination power, common apparatus overhead, gas handling, settling time, and any regeneration or preparation costs.

Thermal isolation may itself reduce efficiency. The comparison must not penalise the dark baseline for isolation while granting the illuminated system uncharged heat recovery.

Catalyst lifetime is another separate requirement. A short stable measurement does not establish long-term durability, and a degradation proxy should not be treated as an additive lifetime cost without validation.

6. Comparison with the strongest established approaches
The novelty test substantially limits the result.

Product-selectivity measurements. In the homogeneous, two-product limit, the proposed witness reduces exactly to a selectivity comparison. Selectivity was already central to the published rhodium work. There is no new information operation in that special case. 

Temperature-profile reconstruction. Existing work measures top- and bottom-surface temperatures and uses a thermal-profile model to estimate the effective thermal response. The proposed operational test avoids reconstructing that profile only by asking a narrower question about an acquired control library. It does not supersede those methods for identifying mechanisms. 

Direct operando thermometry. The 2025 X-ray-diffraction experiment acquired physical information about nanoparticle and support temperatures. My candidate potentially avoids that measurement for its restricted decision, but adds modular apparatus and substantial calibration. No matched-cost comparison establishes that it is better. 

Established discriminating interventions. Baffou and colleagues already reviewed and proposed several experimentally accessible procedures for distinguishing thermal and nonthermal contributions without demanding elaborate thermal microscopy. The general ambition of replacing uncertain temperature inference with a better experimental discriminator is therefore established prior work. 

The remaining possible contribution is the combination of physically addressable calibration, a conservative resource-constrained response envelope, and an explicit separation between performance certification and mechanism identification.

I have not established historical priority for that combination. Its mathematical components are not fundamentally new, and no empirical advantage over established experimental design has been demonstrated.

7. What this says about the closure hypothesis
This investigation supports a narrower conclusion than the original hypothesis:

A physically justified information interface can be sufficient for a useful decision without being sufficient for a microscopic explanation. But specifying that interface is not itself a scientific breakthrough.

The distinction was productive here. It exposed why the initial measurement proposal could generate a false discovery, and it produced a corrected test whose conclusion is tied to quantities an experiment could actually acquire.

However, this run does not show that absence of such interfaces is the principal cause of the mathematical–physical capability gap in Astra. It contains no controlled comparison of AI research workflows, and the strongest existing experimental methods already address closely related interface problems.

Nor has closure-based research demonstrated a new advantage here over those methods. What improved was the handling of a failure mode in the initial proposal.

The research outcome should therefore be recorded as follows: a concrete counterexample, a conditional and physically specified decision test, and reproducible implementation checks—not a discovered source of new physical power. No advance in energy availability, chemical manufacturing capability or civilisation-scale technology has been established.

The most important retained result is that a complete-looking calibration can still omit the correlations an intervention exploits. For Astra, that is a specific falsification target with mathematical and physical content, rather than another general instruction to “validate the model.”
