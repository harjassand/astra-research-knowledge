A randomized algorithm for simultaneously
diagonalizing symmetric matrices by congruence
Haoze He∗ Daniel Kressner∗
April 30, 2025
Abstract
A family of symmetric matrices A1, . . . , Ad is SDC (simultaneous diago-
nalization by congruence, also called non-orthogonal joint diagonalization) if
there is an invertible matrix X such that every X T AkX is diagonal. In this
work, a novel randomized SDC (RSDC) algorithm is proposed that reduces
SDC to a generalized eigenvalue problem by considering two (random) linear
combinations of the family. We establish exact recovery: RSDC achieves di-
agonalization with probability 1 if the family is exactly SDC. Under a mild
regularity assumption, robust recovery is also established: Given a family
that is ϵ-close to SDC then RSDC diagonalizes, with high probability, the
family up to an error of norm O(ϵ). Under a positive definiteness assumption,
which often holds in applications, stronger results are established, including
a bound on the condition number of the transformation matrix. For practi-
cal use, we suggest to combine RSDC with an optimization algorithm. The
performance of the resulting method is verified for synthetic data, image sepa-
ration and EEG analysis tasks. It turns out that our newly developed method
outperforms existing optimization-based methods in terms of efficiency while
achieving a comparable level of accuracy.
1 Introduction
A family of real symmetric n × n matrices (A1, . . . , Ad) is called simultaneously diag-
onalizable by congruence (SDC) if there is an invertible matrix X such that X T AkX
is diagonal [19, 37, 43]. Under the stronger assumption that X is orthogonal, such a
family is usually called jointly diagonalizable (JD), which is equivalent to assuming
that it is commuting. In the signal processing community, it is more common to
refer to SDC as non-orthogonal joint diagonalization [1, 2, 4, 16, 22]. In this work,
we extend our previous study [36] on randomized methods for JD to SDC.

Optimization-based SDC algorithms enjoy two major advantages: Their conver-

In this paper, we propose and analyze a novel randomized SDC (RSDC) algo-
rithm that is not only simpler but often also significantly faster than optimization-
based SDC algorithms. The core concept is simple: Similar to existing methods for
JD [36, 31], for learning latent variable models [7, 6, 5], for CP decomposition (see
Section 1.2 below) and for joint Schur decomposition [24], we obtain ˜X by diago-
nalizing two random linear combinations of the matrices in the family. In contrast,
existing work [33, 25] on SDC in the context of the CP decomposition utilizes two
fixed matrices from the family. RSDC is straightforward to implement and exploits
existing high-quality software for generalized eigenvalue problems implemented in,
e.g., LAPACK [8, 41, 49]. Moreover, unlike optimization-based methods, RSDC
comes with recovery guarantees: Given a family that is (exactly) SDC, RSDC re-
turns, with probability one, a matrix X that transforms the family to diagonal form
by congruence. Given a well-behaved (the precise meaning will be clarified in Sec-
tion 2) family that is nearly SDC, RSDC returns after column normalization, with
high probability, an error (1.2) of O(ϵ). Furthermore, the accuracy can be further
improved by refining the output of RSDC with the quasi-Newton method from [56].
1.1 Connection with partially symmetric CP decomposition
Joint decompositions for matrix families are closely related to tensor decompositions.
To explain this connection, let A ∈ Rn×n×d denote the tensor that has the matrices
of the family ( A1, . . . , Ad) as frontal slices, that is, A(i, j, k) = Ak(i, j). Because
each Ak is symmetric, the tensor A is partially symmetric , with symmetry in the
first two modes [21, 39]. Such a tensor is also called SFS (symmetric frontal slices);
see [29, 32].

1.2 Pencil-based CP decomposition and its instability
As mentioned above, our proposed RSDC (Algorithm 1) proceeds by taking two
random linear combinations of the matrix family. Similar techniques have been pro-
posed for computing the general CP decomposition of a tensor A ∈ Rn×n×d. Such
“pencil-based” algorithms [12] proceed by also taking two linear combinations of the
frontal slices and then solving the generalized eigenvalue problem associated with
this matrix pencil. For example, a suitably reordered generalized Schur decompo-
sition of two fixed frontal slices is computed in [33], whereas two input-dependent
linear combinations of frontal slices are used in [28, 30]. In [34], multiple pairs
of linear combinations of frontal slices are used to enhance the robustness of the
algorithm, including the use of random linear combinations.
In [12], the numerical stability of such pencil-based algorithms has been ques-
tioned by analyzing the condition number of a (unique) CP decomposition in terms
of its factor matrices [18]. Although the results in [12] are stated for general CP
decompositions, it is not unlikely that the arguments in [12] carry over to SFS-CP
decompositions and, hence, to SDC matrix families.
First, Theorem 6.1 in [12] shows that for any pair of linear combinations, there
exists an adversarial input A ∈ Rn×n×d with d ≥ r + 2 such that the following holds:
The condition number of the generalized eigenvalue problem (associated with those
linear combinations of the frontal slices of A) is arbitrarily larger than the condition
number of the CP decomposition. Thus, any pencil-based algorithm that uses fixed
linear combinations can be defeated numerically, highlighting the importance of
using random linear combinations.
Second, Theorem 1.4 in [12] considers random tensors in Rn×n×d that are con-
structed as a CP decomposition with r rank-1 terms such that each term has fixed,
arbitrary vectors in the first two modes and independent Gaussian random vectors
in the third mode. It is shown that condition number for the CP decomposition of
such tensors grows, with high probability, at least proportionally with r2/(d−1) as r
increases. As linear combinations of frontal slices do not change the distribution of

the potential numerical instability of RSDC can be mitigated by combining RSDC
with optimization techniques. Specifically, our numerical experiments in Section 6
show that using the output from RSDC as a starting point of FFDIAG [56] yields
good accuracy and efficiency. It remains an open problem to theoretically analyze
the quality of the output from RSDC as an initial guess for an optimization-based
algorithm like FFDIAG.

3 Exact recovery of SDC
3.1 Basic idea and template algorithm
In [4], it is explained why JD is generically a one-matrix problem, while SDC is
generically a two-matrix problem. In analogy to our work on JD in [36, Algorithm
1], this suggests that one can hope to solve the SDC problem from two linear com-
binations. More formally, given a family ( A1, . . . , Ad) that is SDC, we form two
random linear combinations
A(µ) = µ1A1 + . . . + µdAd, A (θ) = θ1A1 + . . . + θdAd,
and find an invertible X (if it exists) such that X T A(µ)X and X T A(θ)X are diago-
nal. This idea leads to the Randomized SDC (RSDC) summarized in Algorithm 1.
Implementation details, especially concerning Line 3, will be covered in Section 5.
We will establish exact and robust recovery of this algorithm in Sections 3.2 and 4,
respectively.
Algorithm 1 Randomized Simultaneous Diagonalization via Congruence (RSDC)
Input: An SDC family ( A1, . . . , Ad).
Output: Invertible matrix X such that X T AkX is diagonal for k = 1, . . . , d.
1: Draw µ, θ ∈ Rd from specified distributions.
2: Compute A(µ) = µ1A1 + · · · + µdAd, A(θ) = θ1A1 + · · · + θdAd.
3: Compute an invertible X such that X T A(µ)X and X T A(θ)X are diagonal.
4: return X

4.1 Perturbation results
This section collects preliminary results on the perturbation theory for generalized
eigenvalue problems [50, 42].
Given a regular matrix pair ( A, B), a scalar λ is called a (finite) eigenvalue with
associated eigenvector x ̸= 0 if Ax = λBx. The eigenspace associated with λ is
ker(A − λB) and λ is a semi-simple eigenvalue if the dimension of its eigenspace
matches its algebraic multiplicity. Deflating subspaces generalize eigenspaces of
matrix pairs; just as invariant subspaces generalize eigenspaces of matrices. Con-

5.2 Iterative refinement with FFDIAG
When the noise level is high and the dimension n is large, Algorithm 2 alone –
especially with a limited number of trials – may not provide sufficient accuracy, as
indicated by the O(n2ϵ) factor in Theorem 15 and also demonstrated by numerical
experiments in Section 6. To improve the accuracy of Algorithm 2 more efficiently
instead of merely increasing the number of trials, we propose to combine it with
optimization-based algorithms. Our results from Section 4 provide some indication
that RSDC can be expected to deliver decent initial guesses for an optimization
method; at least for moderate n and small ϵ when compared to the trivial initial
guess. As we will see in the numerical experiments in Section 6, this combined
approach can efficiently produce accurate results even for a large dimension n and a
high noise level. However, developing a theoretical understanding of how using the
output from RSDC as an initial guess improves the recovery result of optimization-
based algorithms for SDC remains an open challenge.
The particular optimization algorithm considered in this work is the quasi-
Newton method FFDIAG from [56], which aims at minimizing the off-diagonal er-
ror (1.2) by multiplicative updates of the form ˜Xi+1 = (I + W ) ˜Xi for some carefully
chosen W ; see [56, Algorithm 1] for more details. FFDIAG uses the identity matrix
as the starting point and, quite remarkably, the need for investigating a smarter
initialization is explicitly mentioned in [56]; we believe that RSDC is an excellent
candidate. For the implementation of FFDIAG, we follow the library PYBSS 1 and
further improve the efficiency by vectorizing for loops. The stopping criterion of
FFDIAG considered throughout this paper is ∥ ˜Xi+1 − ˜Xi∥F ≤ 1 × 10−8, which is
the default used in PYBSS.

7 Conclusions
In this paper, we have proposed and analyzed RSDC, a novel randomized algorithm
for performing (approximate) simultaneous diagonalization by congruence. Our nu-
merical experiments show that this algorithm is best used in combination with an
optimization method that uses the output of RSDC as starting point. The resulting
algorithm, RFFDIAG, appears to offer a good compromise between efficiency and
accuracy, outperforming existing solvers; sometimes by a large margin. While empir-
ical results are promising, a rigorous theoretical analysis of RFFDIAG’s robustness
remains an important direction for future research.
