# Ionic Peltier cooling in gated nanopores: flux-identification boundary

## Verdict

The observed local cooling is credible source-reported evidence of active ionic heat pumping, but current and a point-temperature trace do not isolate an ionic Peltier coefficient when a salt gradient is present unless the thermal circuit, other heat channels, and neutral salt flux are also specified. I derive an exact two-ion flux identity and a constructive same-current counterexample: for the Na/Cl heat-of-transport values used by the 2025 paper, two flux states with identical charge current predict ionic heat fluxes differing by **20.7 nW per 1 μA**. The unmeasured degree of freedom is neutral salt flux. Solvent advection and electrode reaction heat add further unmeasured channels.

This is a conditional identifiability theorem for a binary-electrolyte transport model, not a new transport law or a nature-level refutation. I do not dispute the reported temperature decrease. The demonstrated configuration is electrically driven cooling with a maintained $10^4$ salinity ratio; it is not a heat-to-electricity measurement. The generic nanopore Peltier-cooling mechanism is already direct primary prior art from 2024. The narrower 2025 contribution is gate-controlled reversal/amplification in a thermocouple-integrated pore.

## Exact interface and conditional theorem

Take one-dimensional, signed molar fluxes $J_+$ and $J_-$ for a monovalent cation and anion, both measured along the same spatial axis. Let $F$ be Faraday’s constant, let $Q_+$ and $Q_-$ be constant species heats of transport, and define

\[
 I=F(J_+-J_-),\qquad J_s=(J_++J_-)/2,\qquad
 J_Q^{\rm ion}=Q_+J_+ + Q_-J_-.
\]

Here $I$ is current density, $J_s$ is the neutral-salt/common-mode flux, and $J_Q^{\rm ion}$ is the model’s ionic heat-flux contribution. Solving for $J_\pm=J_s\pm I/(2F)$ gives the exact identity

\[
J_Q^{\rm ion}=\underbrace{\frac{Q_+-Q_-}{2F}}_{\Pi_{J_s=0}} I +(Q_++Q_-)J_s. \tag{1}
\]

The proof is substitution. Therefore the heat-per-charge ratio equals a Peltier coefficient only after imposing $J_s=0$, or after independently acquiring $J_s$ and subtracting its contribution. If $Q_++Q_-\ne0$, the transformation $(J_+,J_-)\mapsto(J_++a,J_-+a)$ leaves $I$ unchanged while changing $J_Q^{\rm ion}$ by $(Q_++Q_-)a$. Current alone cannot identify the ionic heat flux.

The point thermocouple does not directly measure $J_Q^{\rm ion}$. It measures temperature at a location about 100 nm from the pore center; inferring ionic heat flux also requires the thermal conductance, spatial Joule-source partition, heat carried by solvent, and electrode/bath boundary conditions. If these are all independently calibrated, a thermal trace could constrain the total heat budget, but it still would not label the species flux without either $J_s$ or a validated transport model.

This is already a hostile counterexample within the admitted two-ion model. At the same $I>0$:

- cation-selective flux has $(J_+,J_-)=(I/F,0)$, so $J_Q^{\rm ion}/I=Q_+/F$;
- zero-salt-flux counterflow has $(J_+,J_-)=(I/(2F),-I/(2F))$, so $J_Q^{\rm ion}/I=(Q_+-Q_-)/(2F)$.

The current is identical; the difference in heat flux is $(Q_++Q_-)I/(2F)$. The source’s concentration gradient means $J_s=0$ is not supplied, and neither total-current measurement nor open-circuit voltage measures $J_s$.

## Numerical prediction from the 2025 paper’s supplied values

Tsutsui et al. report $Q_{\rm Na}=3.5$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ and $Q_{\rm Cl}=0.5$ kJ mol$^{-1}$, citing the heat-of-transport literature, and use a 70-nm pore with $c_{\rm trans}=1.4$ M and $c_{\rm cis}=0.00014$ M in the largest-gradient condition. Substitution into (1), with $F=96485.33212$ C mol$^{-1}$, gives:

| Flux condition | Heat per charge | Value |
|---|---:|---:|
| Na-only, $J_+=I/F,\ J_-=0$ | $Q_{\rm Na}/F$ | 36.27 mV |
| Zero salt flux, $J_+=I/(2F),\ J_-=-I/(2F)$ | $(Q_{\rm Na}-Q_{\rm Cl})/(2F)$ | 15.55 mV |
| Difference at same current | $(Q_{\rm Na}+Q_{\rm Cl})/(2F)$ | 20.73 mV |

Thus the same 1-μA current permits a **20.73-nW** change in predicted ionic heat flux across these two transport states. This is a model prediction conditional on the source’s $Q_i$ values being appropriate, constant transport heats and the two-ion representation. It is not a prediction that the reported pore actually carries either ideal flux state. The 2025 source itself says ion–ion electrostatic screening is neglected in its expression for $Q_{\rm ion}$; confinement, activities, electroosmosis and surface charge can change the coefficients and fluxes.

At 293 K, an ideal monovalent diffusion potential across $r=10^4$ is $(RT/F)\ln r=0.23255$ V. The ideal chemical-potential difference for a neutral 1:1 salt unit is $2RT\ln r=44.88$ kJ mol$^{-1}$. The source uses concentrations as activity proxies; at 1.4 M, nonideal activities must be measured before treating these as specimen values. These numbers expose a second input channel, not an energy surplus: the large maintained salinity gradient supplies chemical free energy and changes permselectivity.

The source-parameter arithmetic and flux-decomposition routine are reproducible in [flux_decomposition.py](flux_decomposition.py). It is a small calculator, not a physical nanopore simulator.

## Primary-source audit and interpretation boundary

The target primary study is Tsutsui et al., “Gate-Tunable Ionothermoelectric Cooling in a Solid-State Nanopore,” *ACS Nano* 19 (2025), 41076–41085, DOI [10.1021/acsnano.5c13339](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsnano.5c13339); the full open article is available through [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12874645/). It reports a 70-nm thermocouple-embedded, gate-all-around pore; $V_b=\pm1.4$ V, $V_g=\pm0.8$ V, and $10^3$–$10^4$ salt ratios; $V_{\rm off}=V_{\rm red}+V_{\rm dif}$; and a temperature reading below ambient by more than 2 K in the $10^4$, negative-bias, negative-gate condition. The paper uses $V_{\rm dif}$ and a Nernst-style fit as an indicator of permselectivity. This is not a direct measurement of the signed Na and Cl fluxes or their common-mode $J_s$. The thermocouple is about 100 nm from the pore center; the article says this geometry reduces sensitivity by 67% and estimates the actual in-pore drop could exceed 3 K. Those are source-reported results, not independently replayed measurements.

The 2025 paper defines its plotted input power as $P=I_{\rm ion}V_b$, but separately resolves the open-circuit offset into redox and diffusion potentials. Consequently $IV_b$ is the external electrical-source work under that terminal convention; it is not automatically the local Joule heat or the complete free-energy input. The chemical reservoirs, gate supply/leakage, electrode reactions, solvent motion, and heat rejected to the reservoirs belong in a full COP or device-energy balance. No cooling load or complete calorimetric heat flux is reported, so the temperature response alone does not give COP or scalable chip-cooling power.

The generic effect is preempted by Tsutsui et al., “Peltier cooling for thermal management in nanofluidic devices,” *Device* 2 (2024), 100188, DOI [10.1016/j.device.2023.100188](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.device.2023.100188). That primary paper already reports charge-selective nanopore cooling, a cooling slope of −0.2 K/nW, cooling at a salinity ratio up to $10^5$, cooling even without a salt gradient in dilute solution, and a measured 3.5 mV K$^{-1}$ thermopower with an inferred $ST\approx1$ V Peltier coefficient. Its paper text explicitly defines effective pore dissipation as $I(V_b-V_{\rm ele})$ under a salinity gradient and describes the heat-transport/counterion mechanism. Thus the 2025 source should be credited for field-effect control and the particular sensor/gate architecture, not discovery of ionic Peltier cooling.

Two adjacent 2024–2026 lines are distinct. Qian et al., “Thermodynamics of Ionic Thermoelectrics for Low-Grade Heat Harvesting,” *ACS Energy Letters* 9 (2024), 679–706, DOI [10.1021/acsenergylett.3c02448](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsenergylett.3c02448), is a review emphasizing transport and reaction dynamics in device efficiency; it is not experimental priority evidence. Song et al., “Conveyor mode enabling continuous ionic thermoelectric conversion,” *Joule* 8 (2024), 3217–3232, DOI [10.1016/j.joule.2024.08.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joule.2024.08.004), reports a different mixed ion/electron conductor with a closed ionic loop for continuous heat-to-electricity generation. It is not the voltage-driven nanopore refrigerator studied here.

## Decisive assay, costs, and falsifiers

For the existing pore interface, acquire at each fixed $(c_{\rm cis},c_{\rm trans},V_b,V_g)$: (1) terminal current and electrode potentials separately; (2) signed cation and anion molar fluxes or an independent neutral-salt flux $J_s$; (3) calibrated pore temperature and a measured thermal conductance/heat flux; (4) reservoir concentration/activity changes, gate leakage, and solvent flow. Then compute (1) before fitting a heat-per-charge coefficient. A practical species-flux assay would require timed reservoir sampling with ion chromatography/ICP-MS or validated isotope tracers, likely on a parallel-pore array to acquire enough material; array thermal uniformity and pore-to-pore selectivity become extra assumptions. Use same-pore sequential reversals only after checking reservoir depletion and drift.

The discriminating comparison is a preregistered bias/gate/gradient reversal set at matched $|I|$ and measured Joule input, with one condition held out. In the model, removing or measuring $J_s$ must collapse the apparent heat-per-charge variation to (1), using independently calibrated $Q_i$. If the fitted heat flux changes with no corresponding change in $I,J_s$, or if solvent advection/electrode heat explains the residual, the constant-$Q_i$ two-ion transfer is rejected. If the source-predicted sign reversal does not reproduce on an independently calibrated pore, the nature-level gate-transfer claim fails; that would not contradict identity (1).

Costs are not free: the 70-nm pore/gate/nanothermocouple fabrication, stable Ag/AgCl electrodes, salt-reservoir maintenance, gate and bias power, local thermal calibration, activity coefficients, ionic-flux sensing and array yield all need accounting. The present report uses no material-specific flux data, no independent pore replay, and no external proof/priority validation.
