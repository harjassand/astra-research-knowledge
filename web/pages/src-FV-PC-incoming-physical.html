Research outcome: stress-selective dissolution—and the obstruction that decides it
The strongest lead from this investigation is mechanically controlled dissolution and regrowth at a crystal surface, with the surrounding liquid kept near atmospheric pressure. The intended capability is a closed-cycle heat pump that accesses dissolution heat without pressurizing the entire working fluid.
However, the investigation did not establish a new useful physical capability. It produced a specific operation hypothesis, a conditional argument that rules out its simplest implementation, and an experiment that could determine whether a more selective implementation survives.
The decisive finding is this:
The solution needed to restore the crystal at the warm end also promotes unwanted crystallization on exposed, unloaded crystal surfaces at the cold end. In the unprotected, near-equilibrium architecture, the proposed refrigeration cycle cannot close.

That changes the research objective. The next question is not whether salt dissolution produces cooling—it already does—but whether one physical interface can simultaneously support load, exchange material rapidly, suppress competing crystallization, and regenerate reversibly.

2. The proposed operation: stress the reactive solid, not the whole liquid
Uniform hydrostatic pressure acts on both crystal and solution. A wetted mechanical contact can instead impose excess normal stress on the crystal while the bulk solution remains near atmospheric pressure.
In the leading pressure-solution description, that contact stress contributes approximately stress times crystal molar volume to the solid’s chemical potential. This is established physical machinery, not a newly discovered effect. arXiv
The proposed closed transformation is:
At the cold end, load a wetted crystal contact so that dissolution becomes favourable. The crystal retreats under the load, and—provided the complete heat balance is favourable—absorbs heat from an external cold load.
At the warm end, reduce the contact stress and recrystallize the dissolved material at that same working interface. Reject heat to the warmer sink and restore the original crystal geometry, solution composition and actuator state.
Temperature switching and sensible-heat regeneration must also be included. Merely moving dissolved salt elsewhere is not a completed cycle.
The potential leverage is architectural: avoid making the entire liquid inventory and its heat-exchange circuit withstand the large hydrostatic pressure. It does not follow that the required local stress is small, that the work is small, or that the apparatus is cheaper.
Pressure solution, crystallization pressure and solution-based refrigeration all have prior art. I have not established historical priority for this particular combination.
A minimal governing model
Let \(c\) denote composition, such as molality, rather than a concentration coordinate that changes simply because the liquid thermally expands. Define
\[
\Gamma(T,c)
=
\mu_{\mathrm{salt\,in\,solution}}(T,c,p_0)
-
\mu_{\mathrm{unstressed\,crystal}}(T,p_0).
\]
Let \(v_s\) be the crystal molar volume and \(\sigma\geq0\) the excess normal contact stress. The leading interfacial model is
\[
g_{\mathrm{diss}}(T,c,\sigma)
=
\Gamma(T,c)-\sigma v_s.
\]
Dissolution is favourable when \(g_{\mathrm{diss}}<0\); crystallization is favourable when it is positive. The stress term follows the established contact model. Applying it quantitatively to ammonium thiocyanate remains an experimental obligation. arXiv
This is not a universal scalar chemical potential for every nonhydrostatically stressed solid. Surface curvature, elastic energy, interfacial forces, changing contact area and concentrated-solution nonideality can matter.
The distinction from hydrostatic pressure is important:
\[
\frac{\partial g_{\mathrm{diss}}}{\partial p}
=
\bar v_{\mathrm{salt\,in\,solution}}-v_s
\]
for uniform pressure, whereas the leading contact intervention supplies \(-\sigma v_s\).
These are different couplings. Comparing their required pressures demands actual partial molar volumes and interface measurements; bulk density alone cannot settle it.
A necessary finite-cycle condition
Suppose cold dissolution at \(T_c\) and stress \(\sigma_H\) increases composition from \(c_0\) to \(c_1\). Warm regrowth at \(T_h>T_c\) and lower stress \(\sigma_L\) must restore \(c_1\) to \(c_0\).
If \(\Gamma\) increases with concentration over the operating interval, necessary endpoint conditions are
\[
\Gamma(T_c,c_1)<\sigma_Hv_s,
\qquad
\Gamma(T_h,c_0)>\sigma_Lv_s.
\]
Consequently,
\[
\boxed{
v_s(\sigma_H-\sigma_L)
>
\Gamma(T_c,c_1)-\Gamma(T_h,c_0).
}
\]
This is a necessary condition, not a performance prediction. It charges the stress swing for both the temperature lift and the concentration excursion.
For an infinitesimal concentration excursion and approximately constant positive partial dissolution entropy,
\[
v_s\Delta\sigma
\gtrsim
\Delta\bar s_{\mathrm{diss}}\Delta T,
\]
before kinetic overdrive and hysteresis.
I have not assigned a numerical temperature lift from this relation. The required same-regime partial entropy, stress-dependent solubility and contact behaviour have not been obtained. A reported bulk mixing-entropy value is not an interchangeable substitute.
3. The decisive obstruction: unwanted crystallization prevents regeneration
The simplest architecture places a loaded crystal surface and exposed, unloaded crystal surfaces in communication with the same solution.
That appears attractive because the exposed regions and fluid channels provide easy solvent access. The cycle analysis shows why this can defeat the operation.
Conditional impossibility result
Assume the following operating conditions: the same closed chemical inventory is used throughout; stress-free solubility increases between \(T_c\) and \(T_h\); compression raises contact-equilibrium solubility; and an exposed unloaded crystal face equilibrates with the solution during the cold stroke. No external separator, evaporation step, selective passivation or other intervention prevents that equilibration.
Write \(c_{\rm eq}(T,\sigma)\) for the equilibrium composition against a crystal surface at temperature \(T\) and excess contact stress \(\sigma\).
Warm regrowth under nonnegative load requires
\[
c>c_{\rm eq}(T_h,\sigma_L)
\geq c_{\rm eq}(T_h,0).
\]
But increasing stress-free solubility gives
\[
c_{\rm eq}(T_h,0)>c_{\rm eq}(T_c,0).
\]
Therefore, every composition capable of supplying the required warm regrowth is supersaturated against an unloaded crystal face during the cold stroke.
That face precipitates salt. Under the assumed equilibration, it holds the solution at approximately \(c_{\rm eq}(T_c,0)\). After warming, this solution is undersaturated even against an unloaded crystal, so it cannot restore the intended working contact.
Thus the specified unprotected, quasistatic cycle cannot close under these assumptions.
This is an analytic result of this investigation; I am not claiming historical novelty for the argument.
What apparent success could actually mean
The loaded region may visibly dissolve while another region grows. A microscope could show a substantial controllable response, yet the apparatus would be relocating crystal material rather than retaining dissolved salt for the intended warm regeneration.
In a steady redistribution process, gross dissolution is not the useful thermal output. Cold-end precipitation returns dissolution heat, while mechanical work and other losses still have to be accounted for.
This also exposes a design conflict:
Adding grooves to improve solvent access can expose the very unloaded crystal surfaces that enable unwanted precipitation and blockage.

A patterned platen is therefore not automatically a solution.
What the result does not rule out
A finite-rate device might preserve supersaturation long enough to complete a useful stroke. Selective surface protection might suppress growth everywhere except the working contact. An isolated solution reservoir, different phase behaviour or another paid intervention could also change the assumptions.
But each escape creates an indispensable new requirement. It cannot be treated as an available component until measured.
The question becomes whether unwanted precipitation can be suppressed without simultaneously blocking solvent access, slowing regrowth or introducing rapid damage.
A separate measured obstruction: loaded growth can nearly stop
Li and colleagues’ calcite experiments provide a direct warning. Under increased contact loading, upward growth fell from approximately 2.6 nm/min to below 0.04 nm/hour, while lateral growth continued. The observed contact pressures were below the estimated 35 MPa thermodynamic crystallization-pressure limit. Their analysis implicated interfacial adhesion and confined-water behaviour, rather than the equilibrium limit alone. arXiv
Those are calcite measurements, not ammonium-thiocyanate properties. They show why a favourable chemical-potential inequality does not establish usable regeneration speed. I do not combine the calcite kinetics with the salt’s cooling measurements to manufacture a favourable device estimate.
4. Complete heat, work and material accounting
The physical endpoint must be externally delivered cooling with the whole working system restored.
For a closed cell at constant background pressure \(p_0\), take heat and non-\(pV\) work entering the cell as positive. The cold-stroke first law is
\[
Q_c
=
\Delta(U+p_0V)_{\rm cold}
-
W_{\mathrm{non-}pV,\rm cold}.
\]
For a complete restored cycle, with \(Q_h\) defined as heat rejected to the warm sink,
\[
Q_h=Q_c+W_{\rm net},
\qquad
\mathrm{COP}_{\rm system}=\frac{Q_c}{W_{\rm net}}.
\]
For an ideal two-reservoir work-driven refrigerator,
\[
\mathrm{COP}_{\rm system}
\leq
\frac{T_c}{T_h-T_c}.
\]
Any additional consumed chemical resource or unrecovered material state must enter the boundary rather than being hidden inside “regeneration.”
The mechanical work is not just spring-loading energy
If a piston follows a dissolving load-bearing crystal, the leading transformation work is
\[
dW_{\rm contact}\simeq \sigma v_s\,dn_{\rm diss}.
\]
That work continues as material dissolves and the piston moves. It is not merely the energy needed to elastically preload a fixture.
Using the supplementary information’s reported solid density of \(1.31\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\), a hypothetical 10 MPa contact gives a leading work scale
\[
\frac{\sigma}{\rho_s}\approx 7.63\ \mathrm{J/g}
\]
of dissolved crystal. This is arithmetic from a reported density—not a measured operating point, cooling capacity or efficiency. Springer Nature Media
Credit work returned during regrowth only when the apparatus actually recovers it.
Net retained transformation—not gross dissolution
In a restricted isothermal approximation, let \(n_d\) be gross contact dissolution and \(n_p\) unwanted cold-end precipitation. With approximately constant dissolution enthalpy and negligible changes in other stored energies,
\[
Q_c
\approx
\Delta\bar h_{\rm diss}(n_d-n_p)
-
W_{\rm contact}
-
Q_{\rm other\,cold\,losses}.
\]
This expression is illustrative; the measured first-law balance takes precedence.
It makes the central issue explicit: large gross dissolution can coexist with negligible or negative useful cooling.
The full resource boundary also includes actuator and frame losses, pumps, temperature switching, imperfect sensible-heat regeneration, solvent containment, surface preparation, replacement and disposal. Crystal cracking, coating degradation and irreversible redistribution are working-body consumption unless reversed within that boundary.
5. The experiment that could change the decision
The appropriate next step is a same-platform interface experiment, not a refrigerator prototype.
Ammonium thiocyanate and water are a justified starting pair because the dissolution-caloric response has been measured. They are not yet a demonstrated stress-selective working material. Nature
First establish the interface operation isothermally
Use a contained, compliant-volume cell that keeps the bulk liquid near atmospheric pressure while independently controlling contact force.
A proposed nominal \(1\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) contact at \(10\ \mathrm{MPa}\) requires \(10\ \mathrm N\). This makes the force requirement explicit; it does not establish uniform wet contact, sufficient crystal strength or useful transport.
The same specimen and operating condition must supply measurements of actual contact area and stress, interface retreat and regrowth, solution composition, lateral precipitation, heat exchange and force–displacement work.
The key comparison is between an exposed-face control and a genuinely selectively reactive interface. A chemically inert mechanical blank is also needed to distinguish dissolution-related heat from elastic and instrumental transients.
The first decisive question is:
Can stress drive measurable dissolution while the dissolved material remains available for later regeneration, rather than immediately precipitating elsewhere?
A proposed coating or surface treatment must earn its place experimentally. Its permeability, chemical compatibility, adhesion and cyclic integrity are not free assumptions.
Then test a closed two-temperature cycle
Only after the interface test passes should the experiment attempt cold dissolution followed by warmer regrowth. For example, 300 K and 310 K would be proposed discriminator temperatures, not a previously established operating window.
A mechanism-level pass requires resolved positive heat extraction from the cold bath, heat rejection to the warm bath, and restoration of the working crystal geometry, composition and solvent inventory. The energy balance must close within calibrated uncertainty.
Repeated cycling must show that the apparent output is not progressively consuming salt, losing solvent or changing the working contact. A short successful run would establish neither industrial lifetime nor competitive economics.
The most informative falsifiers would be:
- Unwanted precipitation removes the supersaturation required for warm regrowth.
- The contact becomes adhesive or transport-limited when enough load is applied.
- Calorimetric cooling disappears after actuator work and full regeneration are included.
These outcomes would directly reject the proposed implementation, rather than merely suggest another parameter sweep.
What computation can—and cannot—supply
A moving-interface transport model could become useful after measurements provide the phase relation, interfacial kinetics and competing precipitation rates.
Before that acquisition, a simulation would have to assume the very behaviour that decides feasibility. An equilibrium calculation cannot establish cyclic film mobility, adhesion, nucleation selectivity or lifetime.
No validated material simulation or physical experiment was performed in this investigation.

A 10 K laboratory demonstration must not be compared directly with the commercial rating above, which operates under different conditions.
Throughput is a separate unresolved requirement
For an idealized diffusion-fed contact, dimensional scaling suggests
\[
j\sim
\frac{D_\parallel b\,\Delta c}{\ell^2},
\]
where \(b\) is fluid-film thickness, \(\ell\) the lateral transport distance and \(D_\parallel\) the effective interfacial diffusivity. Geometry and reaction resistance can modify this relationship.
This is a scaling model, not a fit to the proposed material. In particular, bulk-water diffusivity cannot simply be assigned to a highly concentrated, compressed interfacial film.
The area requirement is unavoidable:
\[
A=\frac{\dot Q}{q''}.
\]
At a hypothetical useful heat flux of \(1\ \mathrm{kW/m^2}\), a \(1\ \mathrm{MW}\) device needs \(1{,}000\ \mathrm{m^2}\) of active interface. At \(100\ \mathrm{W/m^2}\), it needs \(10{,}000\ \mathrm{m^2}\). Neither flux has been demonstrated here.
Layered stacks can reuse a mechanical load path, so summed interface force is not automatically the external foundation load. But load uniformity, fluid access, thermal mass, fabrication yield and replacement remain real obligations.
The compounding benefit would have to come from demonstrated system performance. At fixed cooling duty,

8. Programme decision
Reject the unprotected, quasistatic common-solution implementation under the stated assumptions. Its cold-end exposed surfaces prevent the specified warm-end regeneration.
Retain the selectively reactive implementation only as an experimentally unresolved lead. The decisive missing information is a jointly measured interface that bears load, exchanges material quickly, suppresses unwanted precipitation and restores itself without excessive work or damage.
Do not yet promote it as a foundational technological breakthrough. Neither historical originality nor an advantage over efficient refrigeration has been established. Another equilibrium calculation would not materially change that assessment.
The strongest preserved result is the identification of the missing operation:
Useful stress-controlled dissolution requires selective, reversible material exchange under load—not merely a large dissolution heat or a favourable pressure dependence.

That is a concrete physical requirement and a discriminating experiment. Whether nature permits it at technologically useful rates remains unresolved.
