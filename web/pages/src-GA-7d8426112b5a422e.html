## Finite frequency compiler

The compiler's existence proof uses four sound steps:

1. A nonzero signed functional has a nonzero Fourier polarization coefficient on a zero-sum frequency tuple. Smooth Fourier approximation first reduces this to a finite Fourier sum if necessary.
2. After eliminating the last frequency, that coefficient is a polynomial of degree at most four in every remaining frequency coordinate. Each coordinate occurs only in its own Hessian slot and the last Hessian slot, each with degree at most two. Thus a product grid of five positive coordinate values must detect a nonzero coefficient.
3. With the first \(m-1\) frequencies componentwise positive and the last their negative sum, a signed zero sum of all \(m\) frequencies has only the two choices with all signs equal. This remains true with repeated frequencies. Therefore the cosine-product mean is exactly \(2^{1-m}\).
4. The coefficient of the product of the \(m\) labelled amplitudes is the nonzero polarization coefficient multiplied by \(2^{1-m}\). The average is a nonzero polynomial in those amplitudes, of degree at most \(m\) in each, and a grid of \(m+1\) values detects it.

One distinction needs to be explicit in the implementation or theorem wording. Repeated same-source cosine slots can merge to one Fourier mode. The current labelled-slot amplitude search bounds each labelled term by \(m+1\); it can only guarantee a bound \(m(m+1)\) on a merged Fourier amplitude. To retain the stated bound \(m+1\) on each actual distinct mode, **merge equal frequencies belonging to the same source before the amplitude search**.

The resulting average polynomial in the distinct group amplitudes is still nonzero: the original nonzero labelled polynomial is its pullback by the group-sum map. It still has degree at most \(m\) in each group amplitude, and there are at most \(m\) groups. Searching group amplitudes in \(\{1,\ldots,m+1\}\) proves exactly the stated bound, using at most the same \((m+1)^m\) tests. Frequencies may first be identified modulo sign, since cosine is even. This is a direct repair of the finite recipe, with no change to its frequency bound or stated test count.

The stated rational arithmetic and exact divergence certificate are correct. Each gradient entry has epsilon degree at most three, so a degree-\(m\) polynomial mean has degree at most \(3m\). The coefficient-sum remainder bound and the threshold

N\ge\max\{1,\lceil2S/|I_j|\rceil\}
\]

give the claimed numerator bound. Fourier coefficient sums bound all finite Lebesgue norms from above. The quotient certificate uses the core range \(0<\alpha\le m\), so \(m-\alpha\ge0\); this range should remain attached to that displayed certificate.

Positive defect holds for the compiled witnesses: nonzero scalar data give nonzero \(q\), while zero scalar data leave a nonzero \(\psi_{xx}\) because the frequency bank has nonzero \(x\) coordinates. The construction is exponential and makes no unsupported efficiency or bit-cost claim.

## General double-characteristic pencil

The Jordan reduction is correct for a homogeneous first-order symbol

\[
L(\xi)=A\xi_x+B\xi_y
\]

under the stated determinant and rank assumptions. Once the characteristic form is chosen as \(\xi_x\), \(A\) is invertible, \(N=A^{-1}B\) has zero trace and determinant, and the rank assumption gives \(N\ne0\). Cayley–Hamilton gives \(N^2=0\). A vector \(v\) with \(Nv\ne0\) yields the independent basis \((Nv,v)\), in which \(N\) is the standard nonzero nilpotent Jordan block. Left and right multiplication therefore give the displayed canonical symbol.

All transformations are fixed invertible real maps. They preserve polynomial degree, compare finite Lebesgue norms by fixed constants, and send a true gradient determinant to a nonzero scalar multiple of a true gradient determinant. The word “first-order” should retain this homogeneous-symbol meaning; the argument does not remove arbitrary additional zeroth-order terms.

The source's lattice qualification is necessary. A general real physical-coordinate change produces a transformed flat torus rather than an integer automorphism of the square torus. The classification extends to that transformed torus, but its analytic and necessity transfer deserve a short localization argument rather than the word “immediate.” The following arguments complete the transfer.

For sufficiency, the square-torus inequality gives the whole-plane inequality for compactly supported maps by zero extension and isotropic rescaling. The exponent balance removes the area factor. For a smooth map periodic on an arbitrary lattice, multiply its potentials by a cutoff \(\eta(x/R)\), apply the compactly supported inequality, and divide the integral by \(R^2\). Derivative errors have an extra \(R^{-1}\), while periodic averaging gives the limits of the principal terms. Approximating an indicator cutoff gives the arbitrary-lattice periodic estimate with a finite constant. This avoids an unsupported coordinatewise Fourier assertion on a skew lattice.

For necessity, compactly supported witnesses follow by localizing the two-scale family through its stream potential, which preserves the exact zero-defect leading construction. More explicitly, set

\[
\Psi_N=\varepsilon^2\eta_L(x,y)\psi(x,Ny),\qquad
\eta_L(x,y)=\eta(x/L,y/L),

and use

\[
\phi_N=
(\partial_y\Psi_N+\varepsilon^2\eta_L F(x,Ny),

\]

The leading rescaled variables differ from

\[
\eta_L(\psi_{xy},\psi_{yy},-\sigma\psi_{xx},k)

only by terms involving \(\partial_x\eta_L\) or \(\partial_{xx}\eta_L\). Those are \(O(L^{-1})\) relative errors in the normalized leading mean. First let \(N\to\infty\) at fixed \(L\), using fast-variable periodic averaging. Then divide the leading mean by \(L^2\) and let \(L\to\infty\); its limit is \(I\int\eta^m\ne0\), where \(I\) is the original periodic witness mean. Fix a sufficiently large \(L\). The compact sequence has bounded full gradients and defect \(O(N^{-2})\), with the required nonzero leading mean. A fixed isotropic rescaling embeds its support into any torus chart or open planar domain. Choose the cutoff nonnegative and nonzero so that \(\int\eta^m>0\), including odd \(m\).
