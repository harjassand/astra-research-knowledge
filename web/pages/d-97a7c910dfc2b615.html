Abstract
Multi-metric learning techniques are designed to enhance optimization processes, 
such as imputing missing data in high-dimensional, multimodal datasets, where 
multiple local distance functions are defined. Here, we first outline the theoretical 
foundations of multi-distance metric learning and relevant concepts from group 
theory. Convergence results, followed by experimental evaluations on the LINCS 
dataset, including comparisons with two other methods are presented. Finally, an 
ablation study to illustrate how incorporating each additional metric contributes to 
improved overall performance is provided.
Keywords Distance metric learning · Multi-metric learning · Metric tensors · 
Local metrics · Reductive groups · Group actions
1 Single-metric and multi-metric learning
Metric tensor (or simply metric) is an additional structure on a manifold (such as 
a surface) that allows defining distances and angles, just as the inner product on a 
Euclidean space allows defining distances and angles there. More precisely, a metric 
tensor at a point p of a manifold, M, is a bilinear form defined on the tangent space 
at p, say Tp (that is, a bilinear function that maps pairs of tangent vectors to real num-
bers), and a metric tensor on the manifold consists of a metric tensor at each point p 
that varies smoothly with p.

any two points in a given space can be computed. Its components can be viewed as

Definition 3 ( Reductive Group ) A reductive group is a linear algebraic group G,

 ● The radical of the connected component G0 of the unit element of G is an alge-

2.1.1 Minimal solution using a proper group action and Kempf–Ness theorem
In this section, we outline the properties and results of The Kempf-Ness theorem [29] 
for reductive groups acting on R, with the group action defined in Sect. 2.1. It shows 
that there is essentially a unique minimal solution for the main problem we later 
propose in Sect. 3.
The classical Kempf–Ness theorem establishes a fundamental connection between 
algebraic geometry and symplectic geometry for complex reductive groups, relating 
closed orbits in a representation space to minima of a norm function invariant under 
a maximal compact subgroup. In many applications, however, one works with real 
reductive Lie groups acting on real vector spaces.
In this setting, a closely analogous result holds: the orbit of a vector is closed if and 
only if the squared norm function, defined with respect to a K-invariant inner product, 
attains a global minimum. Moreover, the set of minimizers of this function coincides 
with a single orbit under the maximal compact subgroup. This result, often referred to 
as the Real Kempf–Ness theorem, allows one to transfer geometric insights from the 
complex case to real representation spaces [9, 10, Theorem 1.1].
A vector v ∈ V  is stable if and only if the function
 φ :G → R,φ (g)= ∥g ·v∥2
attains a minimum. Equivalently, v is stable if its G-orbit is closed [22].
Theorem 5 (Complex Kempf–Ness [ 22]) Let G be a complex reductive group with 
maximal compact subgroup K ⊂ G and let V be a G-representation with K-invariant 
Hermitian norm. For a nonzero vector v ∈ V , if φ(g)= ∥g · v∥ 2  admits a critical 
point, then it is an absolute minimum. Moreover , the set of critical points forms a 
single K-orbit.

Theorem 6 (Real Kempf–Ness) Let G be a real reductive Lie group with Cartan 
decomposition G = K exp(p), where K  is a maximal compact subgroup of G. 
Suppose G acts linearly on a finite-dimensional real vector space V preserving a 
K-invariant inner product. For nonzero v ∈ V , define φ(g)= ∥g · v∥ 2 . Then 
1. The orbit G · v is closed in V.
2. φ attains a global minimum on G.
Furthermore, if these conditions hold, the set of minimizers of φ is exactly a single 
K-orbit in G · v [9, 10, Theorem 1.1].
The Kempf-Ness theorem can be thought of as a non-commutative duality theory 
paralleling the linear programming duality given by Farkas lemma [26] which cor-
responds to the commutative world. The theorem, which is a result of the geodesic 
convexity of the mapping φ implies that there is a “unique” metric, which is the 
Euclidean metric “after” the change of coordinates given by the group action, here g.
To avoid a degenerated case, in the absence of any critical point, one may choose 
a slightly smaller group G, e.g. SL, special linear group , as opposed to GL, the 
group Gm, along with its products which are called algebraic tori group, or utilize 
a regularization that is compatible with the theory setup. Therefore, the choice of G 
does indeed determine the optimal metric. It is noteworthy that there exist arbitrary 
change of coordinates that may be used in order to normalize the tensor data; how -
ever, they could change certain characteristics such as rank of the tensor which is of 
main importance.

5 Experimental results
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we employ the Library of 
Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) dataset [ 33], a large-scale 
perturbational genomics resource which provides large-scale gene expression profiles 
of multiple human cell lines exposed to diverse chemical, genetic, and pharmacologi-
cal perturbations.1 The LINCS project was designed to systematically characterize 
cellular responses to external perturbations and to uncover functional relationships 
among drugs, genes, and diseases. Owing to its large scale and diversity of perturba-
tional conditions, the dataset is particularly suitable for studying drug–target–disease 
interactions and evaluating tensor-based machine learning frameworks.
Specifically, the target tensor in this work is constructed using Level 5 data from the 
Broad Institute LINCS Phase 1 L1000 collection [ 15]. The L1000 platform directly 
measures the expression levels of 978 carefully selected “landmark” genes and com-
putationally infers the remaining transcriptome, thereby enabling high-throughput 
gene expression profiling at reduced experimental cost. Level 5 data correspond to 
consensus gene expression signatures generated by aggregating multiple experimen-
tal replicates using a moderated z-score procedure, which improves robustness and 
reduces experimental noise. Each signature captures the differential transcriptional 
response of a specific cell line under a given perturbation condition, including infor-
mation such as compound identity, dosage, and treatment duration.
In our framework, these processed gene expression signatures are organized into 
a multidimensional tensor representation that preserves the intrinsic multi-way rela -
tionships among drugs, genes, cell lines, and disease-related entities. This tensorized 
representation provides a natural setting for tensor completion, representation learn -
ing, and downstream predictive analysis.

To generate the similarity tensors S(i) for each mode, as defined in Eq. (21),

level of a particular gene in a specific cell line following treatment with a given drug.
To evaluate the proposed framework, we compare its performance against repre -
sentative methods from the two principal categories of tensor completion approaches: 
(i) methods that do not leverage auxiliary or side information, and (ii) methods that 
incorporate such information when available, but are inherently constrained either by 
the limited number of modalities they can couple or by their ability to utilize only a 
single side-information matrix per mode.
In particular, we select HaLRTC [ 24] as a representative convex optimization–
based low-rank tensor completion method without side information, and CTRC [20] 
as a representative decomposition-based coupled tensor completion approach that 
can incorporate auxiliary information through latent factorization.
CTRC [20]: Coupled Tensor Ring Completion represents a tensor ring decomposi-
tion–based model designed for coupled tensor completion. The method employs a 
block coordinate descent optimization scheme to iteratively refine the factor matri-
ces and achieve convergence to a locally optimal solution.
HaLRTC [24]: High-Accuracy Low-Rank Tensor Completion formulates the com-
pletion problem as a convex optimization task under a tensor-specific trace norm 
regularization. The trace norm serves as a tight convex surrogate for the tensor rank, 
thereby enabling the recovery of low-rank tensor structures from incomplete obser-
vations with theoretical guarantees of optimality and stability (Table 1).

improving tensor completion accuracy. Each learned metric tensor contributes dis -
tinct structural information: for instance, the first mode may encode sample-level 
relationships, the second mode captures feature correlations, and the third mode 
reflects temporal or contextual dependencies. By integrating them in a unified geo -
metric framework, the proposed TML model effectively adapts the distance computa-
tion to the intrinsic structure of the data, yielding substantial gains over the Euclidean 
baseline.
6 Conclusion
In this manuscript, we introduced a simultaneous multi-metric learning framework 
and demonstrated its application in tensor analysis. The performance of the proposed 
method has been compared against alternative approaches. Other related tensor 
methods have been previously developed using metric learning approaches [ 4, 5]. 
The group action defined in our framework, grounded in the Kempf–Ness theorem 
and results from group representation theory, provides a theoretical foundation for 
identifying critical points that minimize the objective function. Our experimental 
results, including evaluations on the LINCS dataset and an ablation study, further 
illustrate how incorporating multiple local distance metrics enhances performance 
and robustness across high-dimensional, multimodal data. As future work, one may 
consider developing a theoretical framework to use metric learning approaches to 
better approximate the rank of a tensor, as a continuation of the work in [2].
