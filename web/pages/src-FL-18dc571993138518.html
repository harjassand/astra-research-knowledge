# What isotope-count data identify

This note gives a formal information boundary for the temporal-ancestry proposal. It treats a product's atom-entry ages as an unordered multiset and uses only the label and count observation model stated in the FL source. The results are mathematical statements about that model; they do not establish a catalytic ancestry law or an experiment.

## 1. Observation model

Fix a product with exactly \(n\) tracked carbon atoms. Write its unordered entry-age multiset as
\[
\Xi=\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{A_i},\qquad A_i\geq 0.
\]
For convenience, represent this multiset by an exchangeable lift \((A_1,\ldots,A_n)\): randomly permute its atoms, with multiplicity retained. Let \(\mu\) be the law of this lift. For a product collected at time \(t\), \(p(a)\) denotes the *actual, calibrated* heavy-label probability measured at the declared feed boundary at clock time \(t-a\). Shifting the measured feed waveform relative to product collection programs \(p\). Conditional on \(\Xi\), distinct entering carbon molecules receive independent labels with these probabilities. The measured product statistic is the whole-product heavy-carbon count \(K\in\{0,\ldots,n\}\).

The conditional probability generating function and its population average are
\[
G_{\mu,p}(z)
=\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{i=1}^n\bigl(1-p(A_i)+zp(A_i)\bigr),
\qquad
P_{\mu,p}(K=k)=[z^k]G_{\mu,p}(z).
\tag{1}
\]
For a fixed \(p\), these \(n+1\) values are expectations of symmetric functions of the unordered ages. They do not label carbon positions or identify the pathway that produced the ages.

The model assumes a known number \(n\), one tracked carbon per independently labelled feed molecule, isotope-neutral ancestry dynamics, an accounted carbon inventory, calibrated boundary waveform, and a calibrated whole-parent-product count channel. If one of these conditions fails, (1) is not the observation law for the claimed ancestry distribution.

## 2. Ideal arbitrary-control identifiability

**Theorem 1 (polarization identifies the unordered law in the ideal limit).** Let \(\mu\) and \(\nu\) be exchangeable probability laws on \(\mathcal A^n\), where \(\mathcal A\) is a known age domain. Suppose:

1. the actual feed-label probability can be programmed to any measurable \(p:\mathcal A\to[p_-,p_+]\), with \(0\leq p_-<p_+\leq1\);
2. the exact whole-product count distribution is available for every such program, with the control and product-time clocks aligned; and
3. the conditional independent-label model (1) is valid.

If \(P_{\mu,p}(K=k)=P_{\nu,p}(K=k)\) for every \(k\) and every allowed \(p\), then \(\mu=\nu\). Equivalently, ideal arbitrary waveforms and exact count histograms identify the full unordered \(n\)-atom entry-age law. Atom positions and microscopic reaction states remain outside the identified object.

**Proof.** It is enough to use the top-count bin
\[
H_\mu[p]:=P_{\mu,p}(K=n)=\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{i=1}^n p(A_i).
\tag{2}
\]
This is a homogeneous degree-\(n\) polynomial functional. Set \(p_0=(p_-+p_+)/2\). For any measurable \(q:\mathcal A\to[0,1]\), \(p_0+tq\) is an allowed control for all \(t\) in some open interval around zero. The function \(t\mapsto H_\mu[p_0+tq]\) is a polynomial of degree at most \(n\); its coefficient of \(t^n\) is \(H_\mu[q]\). Exact values on an interval determine this coefficient, so equality of the observations implies \(H_\mu[q]=H_\nu[q]\) for every \(q\in[0,1]^{\mathcal A}\).

Now take bounded nonnegative test functions \(f_1,\ldots,f_n\). The coefficient of \(t_1\cdots t_n\) in
\[
H_\mu\!\left[\sum_{j=1}^n t_j f_j\right]
\]
is
\[
\sum_{\pi\in S_n}\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{j=1}^n f_{\pi(j)}(A_j)
=n!\,\mathbb E_\mu\prod_{j=1}^n f_j(A_j),
\tag{3}
\]
where the final equality uses exchangeability. The coefficient is determined by values of \(H_\mu\) on a positive open box of sufficiently small \(t_j\). Taking \(f_j=\mathbf 1_{B_j}\) in (3) recovers \(\mu(B_1\times\cdots\times B_n)\) for every measurable rectangle. Rectangles determine the probability measure \(\mu\), hence the law of the unordered multiset. \(\square\)

The proof is an identifiability theorem, not a finite-cost reconstruction algorithm. It uses exact probabilities for an open family of waveforms and, on an unbounded age domain, control over the entire relevant prehistory. Full count histograms are sufficient because they contain \(P(K=n)\); the other bins can improve estimation or support other target functionals.

The interval-valued control assumption matters. The source telegraph implementation uses only two composition levels. Arbitrary timing of a binary low/high waveform is not by itself equivalent to access to every real-valued \(p\).

**Proposition 1 (two-level controls need not identify the full law).** Use six atoms and three possible ages \(0,\tau,2\tau\). Let \(N=(N_0,N_1,N_2)\) be the occupancy vector. In model E, choose uniformly among the even permutations of \((0,1,5)\); in model O, choose uniformly among the odd permutations. For any binary waveform on these three nodes, let \(B\) be the subset of nodes assigned the high isotope probability. The number \(J_B\) of atoms at high probability is \(\sum_{i\in B}N_i\). If \(B\) is a singleton, this has the same uniform \(\{0,1,5\}\) law in E and O; if \(B\) has two nodes, it is six minus a singleton count and also has the same law; empty/full \(B\) give constants. Conditional on \(J_B\), the whole-product count law is the sum of \(J_B\) Bernoulli(\(p_{\rm high}\)) and \(6-J_B\) Bernoulli(\(p_{\rm low}\)) labels. Thus **every binary waveform on this grid gives the same whole-product count distribution**, even across all eight possible masks and at any number of repeated readouts. Yet the unordered laws differ: the event \(N=(0,1,5)\) has probability \(1/3\) in E and \(0\) in O. This countermodel shows that the full-law conclusion of Theorem 1 cannot be claimed for an interface restricted to two feed levels. The selected \(Q_6\) happens to agree for this pair; it remains a separately observable functional.

## 3. Finite controls identify only a finite span

For a finite experiment, index each distinct waveform/readout-time pair by \(a=1,\ldots,J\), with actual program \(p_a\). Its response functions on an unordered history \(x\) are
\[
f_{a,k}(x)=[z^k]\prod_{i=1}^n\bigl(1-p_a(A_i)+zp_a(A_i)\bigr).
\tag{4}
\]
The observed histogram entries are the linear functionals \(y_{a,k}=\int f_{a,k}\,d\mu\). Since \(\sum_k f_{a,k}=1\), a complete histogram contributes at most \(n\) independent real numbers.

**Theorem 2 (finite-span criterion and null-pair witness).** Let
\[
\mathcal V=\operatorname{span}\bigl(\{1\}\cup\{f_{a,k}:1\leq a\leq J,\ 0\leq k\leq n\}\bigr).
\]
A bounded decision functional \(g(x)\) has the same expectation under every pair of ancestry laws with these observed histograms if and only if \(g\in\mathcal V\).

If \(g\notin\mathcal V\), there are two distinct finite-support probability laws \(\mu_+,\mu_-\) that give exactly the same observed count histogram for every measured setting but satisfy \(\mathbb E_{\mu_+}g\ne\mathbb E_{\mu_-}g\).

**Proof.** If \(g\in\mathcal V\), its expectation is the same known linear combination of the normalization and observed histogram entries for every compatible \(\mu\). Conversely, if \(g\notin\mathcal V\), the functions \(1,f_1,\ldots,f_d,g\), where \(f_1,\ldots,f_d\) form a basis for the measured span modulo constants, are linearly independent. There are \(d+2\) history points \(x_1,\ldots,x_{d+2}\) whose evaluation matrix for these functions has full rank. The \((d+2)\)-by-\((d+1)\) evaluation matrix for \(1,f_1,\ldots,f_d\) has a nonzero null vector \(\lambda\). Full rank of the augmented matrix forces \(\sum_i\lambda_i g(x_i)\ne0\), while \(\sum_i\lambda_i=0\) and \(\sum_i\lambda_i f_j(x_i)=0\) for every measured feature. The positive and negative parts of \(\sum_i\lambda_i\delta_{x_i}\) have equal nonzero mass. Normalizing those parts gives \(\mu_+\) and \(\mu_-\), the required exact null pair. \(\square\)

Consequences:

- A finite set of finite-time count observations cannot identify an arbitrary continuous ancestry law. It identifies precisely the measured response span.
- A particular decision statistic can still be obtained without reconstructing the law when its response function lies in that span. The telegraph parity statistic below is an example.
- For laws supported on \(q\) known entry-time bins, the number of unordered \(n\)-atom histories is \(M=\binom{q+n-1}{n}\), so the probability simplex has dimension \(M-1\). With \(J\) one-time whole-count histograms, injectivity requires
\[
Jn\geq M-1.
\tag{5}
\]
For \(n=4,q=5\), \(M=70\), and at least 18 such readouts are necessary. Conversely, on a known \(q\)-bin support,
\[
H_\mu(p_1,\ldots,p_q)=\sum_{\alpha_1+\cdots+\alpha_q=n}\mu_\alpha\prod_{j=1}^q p_j^{\alpha_j},
\]
where \(\mu_\alpha\) is the probability of occupancy vector \(\alpha\). These \(M\) monomials are linearly independent, so \(M\) points selected unisolvently in any open control box \((p_-,p_+)^q\) recover every coefficient from the top-count fraction alone in exact arithmetic. For the five-bin example, 70 such controls suffice; the lower bound of 18 uses all histogram bins and is only necessary, not sufficient. Both counts ignore finite precision, correlated products, calibration, and query conditioning. Neither is a lower bound for protocols returning infinitely many time-resolved observations.
- Whenever Theorem 2 supplies a null pair for a binary decision, a rule that must choose one of the two actions has worst-case error at least \(1/2\) under exact noiseless observations. More product counts cannot separate identical laws. Abstention can avoid the forced choice.

There is also a simple statistical cost floor for a fixed protocol. If the two hypotheses induce iid whole-product count laws \(q_0,q_1\), a test with both errors at most \(\alpha<1/2\) requires
\[
N D_{\rm KL}(q_0\Vert q_1)\geq 2(1-2\alpha)^2.
\tag{5a}
\]
by the testing total-variation requirement and Pinsker's inequality. If \(q_0=q_1\), the divergence is zero and no finite product count can distinguish them. This floor assumes independent product observations under a fixed protocol; it does not convert raw ion counts or correlated reaction events into independent samples.

## 4. Explicit finite-prehistory obstruction

Suppose the controlled waveform begins at time zero and the feed was held at a constant probability \(p_0\) beforehand. Two four-atom age laws concentrated on entry times \((-4,-3,-2,-1)\tau\) and \((-40,-30,-20,-10)\tau\), respectively, give the same \(\operatorname{Binomial}(4,p_0)\) whole-product count law whenever all four atoms entered before the waveform began. Yet their adjacent-pair lengths are \(2\tau\) and \(20\tau\), so their \(Q_4(r)\) values are \(e^{-4r\tau}\) and \(e^{-40r\tau}\). At \(r\tau=2\), these are about \(3.35\cdot10^{-4}\) and \(1.80\cdot10^{-35}\).

This is an exact zero-information witness for an unlabelled old inventory. A finite control start cannot infer its detailed ancestry from future labels. It must instead measure/charge preparation, extend the labelled prehistory, or carry a quantitative residual-history error.

## 5. Gauge freedoms and other lost information

**Permutation.** A product count is invariant under permutation of the \(n\) carbon atoms. The identified object is the unordered point measure \(\Xi\), not atom-to-position assignments or a bond-level genealogy.

**Common clock origin.** Absolute entry times have no coordinate-free origin. A common shift of all entry times and the laboratory time origin is a reparameterization.

**Uncalibrated common delay.** Let the actual probability at the feed boundary be \(p_{\rm cmd}(s-\delta)\), where \(\delta\) is an unknown command-to-boundary delay. The data depend on ages and \(\delta\) only through the effective shifted ages. Replacing every entry time \(s_i\) by \(s_i+c\) and \(\delta\) by \(\delta+c\) leaves every label probability \(p_{\rm cmd}(s_i-\delta)\) unchanged. Thus the ancestry common-time component cannot be separated from an unknown common transfer delay using product counts alone. Measuring the actual waveform at the declared feed boundary and synchronizing it to product collection fixes this gauge.

**Stationary-code averaging.** A stationary telegraph code has translation-invariant finite-dimensional distributions. Its averaged parity readout is therefore insensitive to a common shift of all entry times. This is useful when the common delay is nuisance variation, but it means that this code does not locate absolute entry age.

**Unmeasured label channel.** If the commanded waveform is passed through unknown mixing, dead volume, contamination, or purity drift, only the composition after that transfer is sampled by the atoms. An unknown transfer delay/shape can be traded against an ancestry-time law. The nominal command is not the input contract.

**Detector channel.** If the true whole-product count vector is \(\pi\in\mathbb R^{n+1}\) and the measured channel is \(y=A_{\rm det}\pi+b\), recovery of \(\pi\) requires a calibrated response matrix that is injective on the relevant count contrasts (full column rank is sufficient). A rank-deficient or unknown matrix leaves a further nullspace. Fragment counts, natural-isotope corrections, backgrounds, recovery, normalization, and drift must be included in that channel model; a fragment spectrum is not automatically the parent-product count law.

## 6. What the telegraph parity observable contains

For even \(n=2m\), take a fair random telegraph sign \(\sigma(s)\) in feed-clock time with Poisson flip rate \(r\). Write \(a_0=p_{\rm high}-p_{\rm low}>0\) for isotope contrast and \(b=p_{\rm high}+p_{\rm low}-1\). For a product detected at time \(t\), the age-indexed input is
\[
p_t(a)=\frac{1+b+a_0\sigma(t-a)}2.
\]
Let \(K\) be the number of heavy atoms and \(X_i\in\{+1,-1\}\) their labels. Conditional independence gives
\[
\mathbb E\!\left[\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{X_i-b}{a_0}\,\middle|\,A_1,\ldots,A_n,\sigma\right]
=\prod_{i=1}^n\sigma(t-A_i).
\]
After sorting \(A_{(1)}\leq\cdots\leq A_{(2m)}\), the telegraph product is \((-1)^N\), where \(N\) counts flips in the reversed feed-clock intervals between \(t-A_{(2j)}\) and \(t-A_{(2j-1)}\). These disjoint intervals have total length \(L_{2m}\). The Poisson parity identity yields
\[
Q_{2m}(r)=\mathbb E e^{-2rL_{2m}},\qquad
L_{2m}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}(A_{(2j)}-A_{(2j-1)}).
\tag{6}
\]
This is a positive observable-specific acquisition: it directly obtains one decision-relevant functional without identifying the full law. With \(K\) alone, use
\[
W_n(K)=\frac{(1-b)^K(-1-b)^{n-K}}{a_0^n},\qquad
\mathbb E W_n(K)=Q_n(r).
\tag{7}
\]
For \(b=0\) and even \(n\), \(W_n(K)=(-1)^K/a_0^n\). The \(n+1\) parent-product count bins suffice. At fixed \(r\), (6) is one scalar and loses the rest of the age configuration. Even knowing \(Q_n(r)\) for every \(r>0\) determines only the distribution of \(L_n\) by uniqueness of the Laplace transform, not the joint age law. For example, four ages \((0,1,10,11)\tau\) and \((0,1,2,3)\tau\) both have \(L_4=2\tau\) but different age span.

For \(N\) independent product draws conditional on the complete code, \(|W_n|\leq B_n=((1+|b|)/a_0)^n\), so the product-sampling component has variance at most \(B_n^2/N\). This is not a total campaign bound: shared-code fluctuations, reaction correlation, detector inversion, and calibration require separate accounting. In the symmetric case the conditional product term is \(a_0^{-2n}/N\). As \(a_0\) shrinks, identification remains mathematically possible but the corrected statistic becomes noisy.

Conditional Chebyshev control of this product-sampling component around the code-conditioned mean requires \(N\geq B_n^2/(\alpha\varepsilon^2)\) to keep additive error above \(\varepsilon\) with probability at most \(\alpha\). The shared-code term is not included in that count. The supplied note's separate uniform code-variance lemma is not independently audited in this subtask.

## 7. The E/O benchmark under arbitrary controls

Take the supplied synthetic four-carbon ages
\[
A_j=C+(j-1)\tau+\tau X_j,\quad j=1,\ldots,4,\qquad C\geq0,
\]
with \(X\) uniform on even parity in model E and odd parity in model O. Every proper coordinate marginal of \(X\) agrees between the two models.

Let \(u(t)\in[-1,1]\) be any deterministic normalized isotope waveform, \(p(t)=(1+a u(t))/2\), and put \(u_k=u(t-C-k\tau)\), \(k=0,\ldots,4\). Expanding the parity-class averages in (1) gives the *entire count-generating-function difference*
\[
G_E(z)-G_O(z)
=\frac{a^4}{128}(z-1)^4\prod_{k=0}^{3}(u_k-u_{k+1}).
\tag{8}
\]
Thus all five count-bin differences are a fixed multiple of \([1,-4,6,-4,1]\). The corrected parity contrast difference is
\[
\Delta_{\rm corr}(t,C)
=\frac18\prod_{k=0}^{3}(u_k-u_{k+1}).
\tag{9}
\]
This is a fourth-order finite difference across the four adjacent age intervals. It applies to arbitrary shaped, overlapping, multipulse, and periodic deterministic controls in the stated model; the control changes the four increments. If \(u\) is \(M\)-Lipschitz, the magnitude is at most \(\min\{2,(M\tau)^4/8\}\).

An ideal two-level single step or one rectangular pulse has at most two changes across the five sampled age nodes. Then at least two factors in (8) vanish, so **the complete whole-product count distributions agree** in E and O. This is the exact, finite-count lower witness for that restricted control class: its per-product divergence is zero, so no number of repeated products can distinguish the two laws with that single-pulse design.

An equally informed square wave satisfying \(u(t-\tau)=-u(t)\) has \(|u_k-u_{k+1}|=2\) for all four gaps and product \(16\), for every common \(C\) and output phase. It attains the amplitude bound \(|\Delta_{\rm corr}|\leq2\); the raw parity gap is \(2a^4\). With \(a=49/50\), the ideal per-product count-law total variation is \(a^4=0.92236816\), so the even/odd-count classifier has error \((1-a^4)/2=0.03881592\) under either specified model. This is a finite exact toy-model counterexample to any claimed superiority of the telegraph implementation. It assumes a known temporal scale \(\tau\), ideal square-wave transitions, the stipulated synthetic laws, and a calibrated count channel.

For comparison, the source's telegraph choice \(r\tau=2\) gives corrected parity gap
\[
\frac{(1-e^{-2r\tau})^4}{8}=0.116090719\ldots,
\]
and switches at mean rate \(2/\tau\), while the tuned square wave switches once per \(\tau\) and has the same mean heavy fraction. A pair of aligned finite pulses also creates the alternating grid pattern when \(C\) is known. A continuous periodic drive preserves the square-wave relation under unknown common \(C\). These comparisons are per the synthetic model, not a total-cost optimization: finite mixing, transition slope, isotope dose, switch wear, prehistory, collection time, and detector precision must be matched on a real platform.

The source paper on isotope frequency response establishes a linear isotope-composition modulation competitor, while the cited H-ZSM-5 work already uses sequences of \(^{13}\mathrm C\)-labelled and unlabelled methanol pulses with online MS and fast-scanning pulse GC. Equation (8) provides the nonlinear count response that a fair shaped/multisine/multipulse benchmark must optimize. It does not establish priority over the full nonlinear isotope-transient literature.

## 8. What would make this decision-relevant

The present E/O laws are abstract and are not fitted to a catalyst. A consequential use needs a concrete pair of physically credible catalyst histories that:

1. both fit the strongest existing pulse, periodic, and other transient data under the same prior calibration information;
2. predict a separation in one selected functional such as (6) that exceeds calibrated experiment and model uncertainty;
3. imply different actions under an explicit catalyst/process objective, such as a change in product selectivity, stability, throughput, or energy use; and
4. can be discriminated by a measured input waveform and full-parent-product count channel at lower total cost or higher reliability than optimized pulse, multipulse, periodic, nonlinear isotope-frequency, and relevant spectroscopic controls.

The actual acquisition ledger must charge the boundary waveform and its transfer delay, isotope purities, chemical-feed constancy, isotope dose, prehistory and washout, old-carbon residual, stationary-state validation, isotope effects on kinetics, product separation, natural-isotope and fragmentation correction, detector response and covariance, normalization, observation time, switching bandwidth, and calibration. Raw ion counts are not independent product samples. The ancestry quantity is entry at the declared boundary; it is not automatically surface residence, bond-formation time, or a spatial location.

No current result supplies the catalyst-specific hypotheses, measured waveform, response matrix, acquisition covariance, total-cost comparison, or action map. Therefore the result is a rigorous ideal identifiability boundary plus a scoped synthetic control calculation, not a validated scientific decision aid.
