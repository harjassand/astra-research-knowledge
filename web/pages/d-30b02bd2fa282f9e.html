Primary-literature comparison, accessed 2026-10-09:

* Albers and Hellwig, [“On the Value of Job Migration in Online Makespan Minimization”](https://arxiv.org/abs/1111.0773), studies nonpreemptive online jobs on identical machines. With (m=2), the deterministic migration model has competitive ratio (4/3); more generally their α_m algorithm uses (O(m)) migrations, and they prove no deterministic algorithm using (o(n)) migrations can beat α_m. This is makespan with a bounded count of reassignments, not migration-delay energy plus learned forecasts.
* Lattanzi, Lavastida, Moseley, and Vassilvitskii, [“Online Scheduling via Learned Weights”](https://research.google/pubs/online-scheduling-via-learned-weights/), studies restricted/unrelated-machine makespan. It predicts a vector of machine weights and obtains prediction-error-dependent fractional assignment guarantees, then rounds online. The reported model has no charged per-migration delay or forecast-call fee.
* Murhekar, Arbour, Mai, and Rao, [“Dynamic Vector Bin Packing for Online Resource Allocation in the Cloud”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.08648), models multidimensional online VM demands and minimizes total active-bin time. For duration ratio μ and dimension (d), its Any-Fit upper bounds include ((μ+2)d+1) for First Fit and ((2μ+1)d+1) for Move-To-Front; the paper gives corresponding lower bounds for Any Fit and specific variants. It does not combine learned predictions with migration delay.
* Mellou, Molinaro, and Zhou, [“The Power of Migrations in Dynamic Bin Packing”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13178), studies active-bin time under dynamic arrivals/departures. It proves an essentially tight migration-count/competitive-ratio tradeoff and, in a separate model where each migration adds delay (C) to an item's duration, an (O(\min\{\sqrt C,\mu\})) approximation with an almost-matching lower bound. It does not use predictions.
* Drygala, Nagarajan, and Svensson, [“Online Algorithms with Costly Predictions”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v206/drygala23a.html), explicitly charges for predictor calls. Its proven settings are ski rental and the Bahncard problem; for the latter it gives a near-tight query-count result, including an Ω((N/T)) query lower bound for a ((1+ε))-approximation under its interval-prediction model. This establishes that acquisition cost itself is prior art, but not in the dynamic multidimensional allocation/migration-delay intersection.
* Indyk, Mallmann-Trenn, Mitrovic, and Rubinfeld, [“Online Page Migration with ML Advice”](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v151/indyk22a.html), studies a genuine movement-cost problem with predictions of the request sequence, obtaining (1+O(q)) consistency and an (O(1/q)) fallback guarantee in its error model. Prediction acquisition is not charged there.
* Angelopoulos et al., [“Advice Querying under Budget Constraint for Online Algorithms”](https://papers.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2023/hash/eda830e16044587b5082a853c4f25a90-Abstract-Conference.html), permits a limited number of possibly incorrect queries, including for non-clairvoyant job scheduling. It constrains query count; the present diagnostic also places a per-query fee directly in the objective.

The closest broad frontier is therefore not untouched. ## Exact diagnostic model

There are two identical machines and (k) independent deadline windows. At the start of window i, before its jobs are released, the algorithm may query a forecast for the hidden eligibility bit; query latency is charged to total end-to-end cost. The window then releases two unit-processing jobs, F_i and G_i. F_i is eligible on either machine. G_i's eligibility is a bit b_i in {0,1}, where 0 denotes machine A and 1 denotes machine B. The online protocol first requires assigning F_i; only after that choice does the input reveal G_i's eligible machine. Both jobs must finish in the window, whose deadline clock starts after the forecast call.

If (F_i) was placed on the same machine as (G_i), the only feasible schedule migrates (F_i) to the other machine. Migration takes δ time, then the jobs run concurrently for one time unit; the deadline window has length (1+δ), with (0<δ<1). If the two jobs were placed on different machines, they run concurrently for one unit and finish with no migration. The next window begins after this one. Thus every feasible schedule has total execution time

\[
T_A=k+\delta M+cQ,
\]

where (M) is the number of migrations and (Q) is the number of forecasts queried. Each query is serialized before the corresponding assignment and costs (c>0) time units in end-to-end cost. The offline optimum knows every b_i, places F_i opposite G_i, and has (T^*=k). The prediction fee is charged only to the algorithm; this is the standard online/offline comparison with an acquired-information cost included in online cost.

The model has no loss-of-service option: an algorithm that does not migrate after a same-machine conflict misses the deadline and is infeasible. Every policy considered below migrates on a conflict, so feasibility is exact and migration is a real deadline-relevant cost.

Two different information regimes are kept separate:

1. **Adversarial untrusted forecast.** A query may return any bit, with no relation to (b_i). The adversary may choose an uninformative response. No accuracy or calibration promise is supplied.
2. **Calibrated stochastic forecast.** The workload bits (b_i) are iid uniform, and a queried prediction satisfies \(\hat b_i=b_i\oplus Z_i\), where independent (Z_i\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\varepsilon)\), with a known certified \(0\le\varepsilon\le1/2\). A query costs (c). If obtaining and certifying this predictor costs a one-time (A\), that amount is also charged. The stochastic guarantee is not a worst-case guarantee.

The calibration promise is supplied as part of this regime; A is its charged acquisition/certification fee, and no finite-sample learning procedure is established here.

## Exact theorem for the diagnostic

For any fixed number q≤k of queries actually used in the calibrated regime, the optimal policy queries any q windows, assigns each queried F_i opposite the prediction, assigns each unqueried F_i to either machine, and migrates precisely when a conflict occurs. Its exact expected cost and ratio are

\[
\mathbb E[T_A]
=k+qc+\delta\left(q\varepsilon+\frac{k-q}{2}\right),
\qquad
\frac{\mathbb E[T_A]}{T^*}
=1+\frac{qc+\delta(q\varepsilon+(k-q)/2)}{k}.
\tag{1}
\]

The same value is attained by the limited-clairvoyance benchmark that receives the same q noisy bits, pays the same call fees, and sees no other future information: windows are independent, so it takes the same Bayes-optimal action. The online policy matches this benchmark exactly. Full clairvoyance, which sees all b_i without paying for forecasts, has cost k.

Among policies with exactly q queries, this is optimal. If q is instead an upper limit on calls and the predictor has already been acquired, query all q calls iff

\[
c<\delta\left(\frac12-\varepsilon\right).
\tag{2}
\]

If predictor acquisition/certification costs (A), use it and query all windows iff

\[
k\left[\delta\left(\frac12-\varepsilon\right)-c\right]>A.
\tag{3}
\]

If the predictor has not yet been acquired and a cap q_max is imposed, acquire it and use all q_max calls iff q_max[δ(1/2−ε)−c]>A; otherwise make no calls. In particular, even an error-free forecast yields ratio 1+c+A/k if all k bits are acquired; its cost is not free.

In the adversarial untrusted regime, the exact randomized minimax expected cost is (k+\delta k/2), achieved by making each placement uniformly at random and never querying. Its ratio is (1+\delta/2). The exact deterministic worst-case cost is (k+\delta k), achieved by assigning arbitrarily and migrating on every conflict; its ratio is (1+\delta). Any positive number of paid queries can be made uninformative and cannot reduce the worst-case number of conflicts, so it strictly increases the robust value by its fee. In this model, a policy cannot both query at positive cost and retain the exact sharp adversarial worst-case ratio. It can improve in the calibrated stochastic regime, but that improvement depends on an explicit quality assumption and is charged by (1)–(3).

## Proof

For a queried window, conditioned on the prediction \hat b_i, the best assignment is the machine opposite it. Its conflict probability is exactly ε, and feasibility then forces one migration on that event. A different assignment has conflict probability 1-ε\ge1/2 and cannot be better. A query therefore contributes expected incremental cost c+\delta\varepsilon.

For an unqueried window, (b_i) is an independent fair bit even after conditioning on the entire previous history. Any possibly randomized placement of (F_i) is independent of (b_i), so its conflict probability is exactly (1/2). Feasibility forces migration on conflict, giving incremental cost δ/2. Summing the (k) independent windows proves (1), and also proves that adaptively deciding query locations cannot improve the per-window lower bound. Comparing the queried and unqueried increments yields (2). Including the one-time acquisition cost gives (3).

For the adversarial regime, the no-query randomized upper bound follows from uniform placement: for every fixed (b_i), conflict probability is (1/2). For a matching lower bound, take (b_i) iid fair and the uninformative predictor output fixed. Before seeing (b_i), every policy's placement is independent of it, so the expected conflict probability is (1/2) whether or not it pays to query; a query only adds (c). Averaging gives an input distribution with expected cost at least (k+\delta k/2+cQ), hence a fixed input attaining at least the no-query value. For deterministic algorithms, after each assignment the adversary sets (b_i) equal to the machine occupied by (F_i); every window requires migration. The trivial policy attains both deterministic and randomized bounds, proving exactness in these respective minimax classes.

## Algorithm and resource accounting

The online action is local: (i) if its permitted query budget and model-acquisition break-even condition allow, buy the next forecast; (ii) place (F_i) opposite a queried prediction or by the chosen no-query baseline; (iii) when (G_i) arrives, compare its revealed eligibility bit to (F_i)'s current machine; (iv) if they coincide, migrate (F_i) before processing; then run both jobs. Every job finishes within its (1+\delta) window.

For a fixed calibrated model and fixed query budget, the policy uses (O(1)) working memory and (O(1)) operations per window, for (O(k)) total time. It stores only the current prediction and machine bit. Storing the full prediction tape instead costs (O(k)) bits, but is unnecessary. It uses exactly (q) forecast calls and (q) prediction bits; query time/fee (cq) and one-time model cost (A) are included in (1)–(3). It performs at most (k) migrations, each of delay δ, and has zero deadline failures. The randomized robust policy needs one random bit per window, (O(1)) state, and no forecasts. The deterministic robust policy needs no randomness or forecasts.

The executable check [`verify_v1.py`](verify_v1.py) enumerates all workload bits and independent forecast-noise bits for (k\le6), and confirms (1) exactly using rational arithmetic. It prints one parameter fixture. It verifies only finite instances of this separable model; the proof above, not enumeration, establishes the theorem for all (k).

## Result comparison and interpretation

The diagnostic has a sharp but narrow conclusion. In the robust regime, an untrusted paid bit gives no minimax improvement because a worst-case predictor can erase its correlation with the workload; the optimal policy declines the query. In the calibrated regime, the exact marginal value of each queried bit is the migration-time saving \(\delta(1/2-\varepsilon)\), and the forecast is worth buying precisely when that saving exceeds its price, with the one-time predictor cost amortized by (3). This is an exact acquisition-versus-recourse frontier for independent deadline pairs.

This is not a broad new scheduling capability. It has no interaction between windows, no heterogeneous capacities, no multidimensional vector packing, no uncertain duration, no dynamic arrivals/departures within a packing, and no nontrivial forecast-learning procedure. Its exact threshold follows from separability. It does show why a desired broad theorem must distinguish a stochastic/calibrated improvement from an adversarial guarantee and must put prediction acquisition in the objective.

## Broad model still open here; blockers and decisive next gate

The relevant candidate model is: a stream of n jobs arrives with known demand vectors \(s_j\in[0,1]^d\), departs after unknown lifetimes \(\ell_j\), and must remain assigned to a capacity-one vector bin while active. The goal is to minimize active-bin time. At arrival, the algorithm may acquire a learned lifetime forecast \(\hat\ell_j\) for fee \(c_j\). It may migrate jobs, and every migration adds a restart delay C to that job's active time. The adversary controls the arrival stream and, in the untrusted regime, forecast outputs. Write \(\mu=\ell_{\max}/\ell_{\min}\) for the ratio of largest to smallest lifetime. A meaningful quality regime would need an explicit acquired/certified law or error budget, for example weighted lifetime error

\[
E=\sum_{j=1}^n \lVert s_j\rVert_\infty\,|\ell_j-\hat\ell_j|,
\]

and a separate query/training cost \(A+\sum_j c_jQ_j\). This is only a proposed specification; no general guarantee for it is proved.

The missing technical step is a migration-accounting invariant for a feasible vector packing: every forecast-triggered reassignment must either pay its C-delay from a certified reduction in active-bin time or charge the discrepancy to E, while the total discrepancy charging does not create a new factor in the worst-case O(\min\{\sqrt C,\mu\})-type benchmark. The scalar migration-delay frontier and the vector Any-Fit bounds above do not supply this cross-term. Future attacks would need to (a) define forecasts for the arrival/departure trajectory, not only item lifetime; (b) maintain vector-capacity feasibility at every event; (c) charge migration restart time and query/training fees in the same objective; (d) prove a robust bound against an adaptive forecast-error pattern; and (e) build an instance family lower-bounding the joint information, active-time, and migration-delay frontier. No such invariant or matching lower bound was found in this finite assignment.
