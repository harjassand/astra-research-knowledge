# Black-box feedback-policy certification has an inverse-risk lower bound

## Interface

Fix a resettable feedback policy \(\pi\) and an operational horizon of \(H\) transitions. One independent experiment runs the whole policy from the stated initial law through all \(H\) transitions and returns a trajectory. The safety event is

\[
F_H=\{\text{at least one forbidden state is visited by time }H\}.
\]

Assume only that independent runs under this fixed policy are identically distributed. No parametric transition law, simulator likelihood ratio, known rare-event mechanism, or inward barrier is supplied. We count the full trajectory as one Bernoulli observation \(Y=1_{F_H}\), even if more telemetry is returned. The lower-bound family below makes all safe trajectories observationally identical, so extra telemetry does not help on the no-event path.

## Proposition (exact zero-failure obstruction)

Let \(p=\Pr(F_H)\), let \(0<\alpha<1\), and let \(U_N(Y_1,\ldots,Y_N)\) be any one-sided \((1-\alpha)\)-valid upper confidence bound:

\[
\inf_{p\in[0,1]}\Pr_p\{p\le U_N\}\ge1-\alpha.
\]

For any target \(\varepsilon\in(0,1)\), if

\[
N < \frac{\log\alpha}{\log(1-\varepsilon)},
\]

then after observing zero failures it is impossible for this valid procedure to certify \(p\le\varepsilon\): necessarily

\[
U_N(0,\ldots,0)\ge\varepsilon.
\]

Equivalently, the zero-failure Clopper–Pearson upper endpoint

\[
U_N^{CP}=1-\alpha^{1/N}
\]

first drops below \(\varepsilon\) at \(N\ge\lceil\log\alpha/\log(1-\varepsilon)\rceil\). For small \(\varepsilon\), this is \(N\sim\log(1/\alpha)/\varepsilon\).

### Proof

Suppose instead that \(U_N(0,\ldots,0)<\varepsilon\). Under the admissible model with failure probability \(p=\varepsilon\), the event of seeing all zeros has probability \((1-\varepsilon)^N\). On that entire event the claimed upper bound is below the true value, so the confidence procedure fails. Its noncoverage probability is therefore at least \((1-\varepsilon)^N\). Uniform \(1-\alpha\) coverage requires \((1-\varepsilon)^N\le\alpha\), which is exactly the stated threshold. This argument only uses the zero-failure path; randomization by the confidence procedure cannot evade coverage because conditioning on an all-zero sample leaves its output distribution the same under each member of this Bernoulli family. ∎

## Horizon and compute consequences

For an i.i.d. per-transition hazard \(q\), failure during one length-\(H\) operation is

\[
p_H(q)=1-(1-q)^H.
\]

To certify \(p_H(q)\le\varepsilon\), a per-transition analysis must resolve

\[
q\le q_*:=1-(1-\varepsilon)^{1/H}\sim\varepsilon/H.
\]

Testing complete independent length-\(H\) trajectories needs

\[
N_{\rm episode}\ge
\left\lceil\frac{\log\alpha}{\log(1-\varepsilon)}\right\rceil,
\qquad
C_{\rm transitions}=H N_{\rm episode}.
\]

Testing individual independent transitions to rule out \(q\ge q_*\) gives the same leading transition count:

\[
N_{\rm transition}\ge
\left\lceil\frac{\log\alpha}{\log(1-q_*)}\right\rceil
=\left\lceil\frac{H\log\alpha}{\log(1-\varepsilon)}\right\rceil.
\]

At \(\alpha=0.05\), \(\varepsilon=10^{-6}\), and \(H=10^6\), the episode-level threshold is 2,995,731 full trajectories, or about \(2.996\times10^{12}\) transition evaluations. This is a lower bound for this unstructured information interface, not for known parametric models or validated rare-event importance samplers.

There is also an acquisition-safety consequence. If these are physical plant trials and the candidate has risk \(\varepsilon\), then at the minimum sample size the probability of at least one calibration failure is \(1-(1-\varepsilon)^N\ge1-\alpha\). Thus a 95%-confidence campaign at the risk boundary itself carries at least a 95% chance of seeing a failure. A simulator, safe test envelope, or stronger structural certificate is needed to separate verification from deployment exposure.

## What feedback changes, and what it does not

The experiment is the complete fixed feedback policy, so its path event already includes all within-episode feedback. The lower bound does not assume an open-loop controller. It also survives full path logging: construct a family in which every no-failure path is the same and only a rare hidden shock changes the path to the failure outcome. After \(N\) no-failure runs, the probability of having seen no distinguishing shock remains \((1-p)^N\).

This is an evaluation/acquisition result. If the controller or policy is changed between episodes, those data no longer automatically estimate one fixed policy's operational risk. A valid adaptive analysis needs a confidence sequence or an explicitly weighted change-of-measure argument and must charge policy search, data reuse, and every simulated or physical transition.

The bound is not an obstacle to the Astra N35 theorem. N35 assumes a supplied reaction network, bounded predictable rates, lattice-compatible initial data, and a certified strict inward drift layer for every admitted mode; it then proves operation-time containment directly. It does not infer the generator or its certificate from rare-event samples. Conversely, the black-box bound does not assume the chemical structure needed by N35.

## Limitations and falsifiers

The proposition assumes independent reset runs under one frozen policy and a model class containing an unstructured Bernoulli hazard. It can be beaten only by adding informative structure or observations: for example, known transition probabilities, a known parametric tail law, a support-complete simulator with computable likelihood ratios, or a supplied safety certificate. Any claimed faster universal calibration on the stated black-box interface is falsified by the two-model (\(p=0\) versus \(p=\varepsilon\)) all-zero-path argument. Correlation across episodes, hidden drift, or policy adaptation invalidates the i.i.d. model and may require more data; it cannot improve the stated universal guarantee without additional assumptions.

This is a direct binomial confidence lower bound closely related to exact binomial intervals, not a novelty claim. Its use here is to close an information/cost mismatch between operation-time certificates and data-driven rare-event evaluation.
