The two finite Euclidean executions are independently certified in [the acquisition record](VERIFIED_FINITE_ACQUISITION.md); they supply no speed theorem or universal optimal-cardinality convergence theorem.

**Checkpoint:** the desired universal maximum-node bound at `q=600(T+1)` remains **UNKNOWN**. The author derivations below prove a uniform bound for the continuum correction of the *actual normalized ring centroids*, an actual discrete bound with a square-root logarithmic loss, and an exact obstruction to deriving a general maximum bound from the gradient-L1 residual norm alone. ## 1. Fixed question, operator and norms

Let sigma be normalized area measure on S², and let `V_T` be the real mean-zero harmonics of degrees 1 through T. Use an L2(sigma)-orthonormal basis `e_(l,m)`, with `lambda_l=l(l+1)` and `M=T(T+2)`. For nodes X and tangent vectors v put

    B_X(P)=N^-1 sum_i P(x_i),
    J_X(v)(P)=N^-1 sum_i grad P(x_i) dot v_i,
    <v,w>_X=N^-1 sum_i v_i dot w_i,
    A_X(P,Q)=N^-1 sum_i grad P(x_i) dot grad Q(x_i).

The adjoint is `J_X*P=(grad P(x_i))_i`. Therefore the unique correction of minimum average squared Euclidean length satisfying `J_X v=-B_X` is

    u_X=-A_X^-1 B_X,             v_i=grad u_X(x_i).       (E1)

In orthonormal harmonic coordinates, `A_X=(N^-1 DD^t)` where column blocks of D are the sampled tangent gradients. Formula (E1) agrees with `D^t(DD^t)^-1(-sum_i e(x_i))` used in the exact executable control. The factors N cancel. Changing harmonic basis does not change v.

Two residual norms must be distinguished:

    epsilon_G(B)=sup_P |B(P)| / integral |grad P|,
    zeta(B)=sup_P |B(P)| / ||grad P||_2
           =sqrt(sum_(l,m) |B(e_(l,m))|²/lambda_l).       (E2)

The accepted optimal-order LP proof uses `epsilon_G`. Euclidean energy naturally uses `zeta`, and `zeta<=epsilon_G`. Neither is simply the normalized L2 norm of the harmonic mean.

The initializer is exactly the one in Sections 3–4 of [the accepted author proof](../cycle10/CENTROID_LINF_NEWTON.md): northern ring j has z interval `[1-j²/q²,1-(j-1)²/q²]`, `s_j=2j-1` equal longitude wedges, then reflection across the equator. There are `N=2q²` equal-area cells. Each c_i is the *normalized ambient average of its cell*, not the midpoint in z and not a freely chosen almost-design. Set `h=12/q`. Every cell lies in `B_h(c_i)`.

The requested optimal-order improvement would establish, uniformly in T at `q=600(T+1)`,

    max_i |grad(A_C^-1 B_C)(c_i)| <= C T/q²,             (E3)

with a numerical constant C, and then establish an appropriate invariant under later Euclidean Newton corrections and rational resets. A bound for the continuum potential `(-Delta)^-1 B_C` alone proves neither part.

## 3. Exact general-residual obstruction

Here is a self-contained lower bound for the continuum Euclidean projection. It is an obstruction to the implication

    epsilon_G(B)<=1  =>  ||grad(-Delta)^-1 B||_infinity<=C

with C independent of T. It is **not** a counterexample to (E3), because its B is not asserted to be the actual centroid residual.

Define the finite partial Green polynomial

    F_T(s)=sum_(l=1)^T [(2l+1)/lambda_l] P_l(s),
    G_T(x,y)=F_T(x dot y).

The Legendre equation and the identity
`sum_(l=0)^T(2l+1)P_l=P'_(T+1)+P'_T` imply exactly

    F'_T(s)=[1+s-P_T(s)-P_(T+1)(s)]/(1-s²).            (E7)

To bound the last two terms, the Laplace integral representation gives

    |P_n(cos theta)|
      <=pi^-1 integral_0^pi exp[-n sin²(theta)sin²(phi)/2] dphi
      <=sqrt(pi/2)/[sqrt(n)sin(theta)]
      <2/[sqrt(n)sin(theta)].                          (E8)

The second inequality follows by symmetry, `sin(phi)>=2phi/pi` on `[0,pi/2]`, and extending the Gaussian integral to infinity. Thus for `T>=128` and

    theta_* = asin(8/sqrt(T)),   theta in [theta_*,pi/3],

both Legendre terms in (E7) have modulus at most 1/4. In this annulus,

    F'_T(cos theta) >=1/[2(1-cos theta)].

At the north pole x_0, differentiate in the ambient x direction. In spherical coordinates y=(theta,phi), the azimuthal component of the y-gradient of this differentiated kernel is

    e_phi dot grad_y(partial_x G_T(x_0,y))
                                  =-sin(phi) F'_T(cos theta).

Choose the bounded tangent field

    w(y)=-sign(sin phi) e_phi

on the annulus and zero elsewhere. Values on its measure-zero boundaries are immaterial. Let `B_w(P)=integral w dot grad P`. Then `epsilon_G(B_w)<=1`, while its Euclidean continuum solution u has

    partial_x u(x_0)
      =integral w dot grad_y(partial_x G_T) d sigma
      >=(1/(2pi)) log[(1/2)/(1-sqrt(1-64/T))]
      >=log(T/128)/(2pi).                              (E9)

The last step uses `1-sqrt(1-x)<=x`. Everything uses the actual sharp cutoff through degree T. No smoothed spectral cutoff, asymptotic kernel assumption, or finite ODE calculation enters. This proves a logarithmic *lower* bound for this general projection norm; it does not prove a matching upper bound or assert that logarithms occur on the centroid RHS.

## 4. Exact longitude cancellation for the actual initializer

Write `p=1/q`, `r_j=(j-1/2)p` and `z_j=1-r_j²`. Northern centroid longitudes are

    phi_(j,k)=(2k+1)pi/s_j,    s_j=2j-1.

Every centroid in a ring has the same latitude. The ring average of `exp(i m phi)` is zero unless `s_j` divides m, and is the real number `(-1)^(m/s_j)` otherwise. Therefore:

* all sine harmonic moments vanish exactly;
* for a cosine mode with azimuth m>0, only rings with odd `s_j>=3` dividing m can contribute;
* the first-ring centroid is the exact pole, so it contributes zero to every m>0 mode;
* reflection across the equator also kills every mode with `l+m` odd.

In particular m=1, m=2, and every positive power of two has zero moment. The nonzero alias rings satisfy `j<=(m+1)/2` and are near the poles.

The cone containing ring j is convex; its average and its normalization stay in that cone. Thus `sin(theta_(c_j))<=sqrt(2)j/q`. For the normalized real cosine harmonic,

    |e_(l,m)(theta,phi)|
      <=sqrt[2(2l+1)] (l sin theta)^m/m!.              (E10)

One derivation is `P_l^m=(-1)^m(1-z²)^(m/2)P_l^(m)(z)` and

    |P_l^(m)(z)| <=P_l^(m)(1)
                    =(l+m)!/[2^m m!(l-m)!].

The normalized Gegenbauer Laplace integral has a nonnegative probability weight and an integrand of modulus at most one on `[-1,1]`, proving this endpoint bound; differentiation of the Legendre generating function supplies the displayed factorial. Bounding each of the 2m factorial factors by 2l proves (E10). These facts also follow directly by expanding the same integral.

Since `m!>=(m/exp(1))^m` and `sqrt(2)exp(1)<5`, the amplitude on an alias ring is at most `sqrt[2(2l+1)](5l/q)^m`. The combined northern/southern weight of ring j is `s_j/q²`, and the sum of the contributing odd divisors is at most m². Hence

    |B_C(e_(l,m))| <=[sqrt(2(2l+1)) m²/q²](5l/q)^m,
                         m>=3;                        (E11)

all other nonzonal entries vanish as described above.

For `a=5T/q<=1/120`, use the addition theorem and `sqrt(2)(2l+1)/sqrt(lambda_l)<5`. The nonzonal part of the *continuum* velocity therefore satisfies

    ||grad u_0,nonzonal||_infinity
      <=(5T/q²) sum_(m>=3) m² a^m
      <=50(T/q²) a³.                                  (E12)

Indeed the sum is `a³(9-11a+4a²)/(1-a)³<=10a³`. This is an actual initializer calculation, not a statement about arbitrary small moment vectors.

## 5. Zonal bound including the normalization of every centroid

This section proves the author lemma

    |B_C(P_l)|<=6/q²   for even 2<=l<=T, q>=600(T+1). (E13)

Odd l vanish by reflection. In (E13), P_l is the ordinary Legendre polynomial, not its L2 normalization. Importantly, the nodes in (E13) are the actual normalized centroids.

### 5.1 An exact normalization remainder

For a northern ring, put r=r_j, `g=2-r²`, `s=r sqrt(g)` and `F(r)=r² sqrt(2-r²)`. Its ambient mean is

    m_z=1-r²-p²/4,
    m_perp=sinc(pi p/(2r)) [1/(rp)]
                        integral_(r-p/2)^(r+p/2) F(t) dt.

On `[0,1]`, `|F''|<=9` and

    F''''=-12g^-1/2-39r²g^-3/2-42r^4g^-5/2-15r^6g^-7/2,
    |F''''|<=108.

Taylor averaging and `|sinc(alpha)-1+alpha²/6|<=alpha^4/120` give

    m_perp=s+p² b+E_perp,
    b=F''/(24r)-pi² s/(24r²),
    |b|<=1/r,             |E_perp|<=(3/10)p^4/r³.       (E14)

The coefficient follows from `sqrt(2)<3/2`, `pi²<10`:
the three remainder coefficients are at most `9/160`, `5/32`, and `5/64`, whose sum is less than 3/10. These estimates are valid even at j=1, where `r=p/2` and sinc is zero.

Set

    d(r)=[4/g+pi² g]/24,       0<d(r)<=1,
    a(z)=z[4/(1+z)+pi²(1+z)]/24.

The dot product of `(s,1-r²)` with the order-p² change in (E14) is exactly `-d(r)p²`. Also `|m-(s,1-r²)|²<=5p^4/r²`. Thus

    |m|²=1-2d(r)p²+E_2,       |E_2|<=6p^4/r².

For `e=|m|²-1`, `|e|<=26p²<1/10` and `e²<=5p^4/r²`. The latter is checked by dividing the square of `2p²+6p^4/r²` by `p^4/r²` and using `r>=p/2`:

    4r²+24p²+36p^4/r² <=4+168p²<5.

The Taylor remainder of `(1+e)^-1/2` is at most e² on this interval. Consequently the true normalized centroid latitude satisfies

    z_(C,j)=z_j-p²/4+p² a(z_j)+R_j,
    |R_j|<=9p^4/r_j².                                 (E15)

No uncharged real minimization, asymptotic equality, or replacement by raw z midpoints is used.

### 5.2 The accumulated normalization remainder is small

Let `zeta_j=z_j-p²/4+p²a(z_j)`. For j>=2, both `zeta_j` and `z_(C,j)` are in `(0,1)` and their sine of polar angle is at least `2r_j/3`.

For the surrogate, `a(z)<=1` and `r_j>=3p/2` give `1-zeta_j>=2r_j²/3`. For the actual centroid, `F(t)>=t²` and `sinc(pi p/(2r_j))>=2/3` show `m_perp>=2r_j/3`; normalization can only increase that transverse coordinate. Thus the full segment between the two latitudes obeys the same sine lower bound.

The endpoint derivative bound and the trigonometric Bernstein inequality give

    |P'_l(z)|<=min(l²,l/sqrt(1-z²)),

so on those segments it is at most `min(l²,2l/r_j)`. The first actual centroid is z=1, and its surrogate is at most `1+p²`; there `|P'_l|<=2l²`. This last bound follows from the positive Gegenbauer integral outside 1 and `arcosh(1+p²)<=2p`, since `lp<=1/600`.

Weights in the northern normalized quadrature are `2r_j p=(2j-1)p²`. Put t=lp and split the half-integer sum at `K=2/t`. Equations (E15) and the derivative bounds show

    sum_j (2j-1)p² |P_l(z_(C,j))-P_l(zeta_j)|
      <=72l²p^4
         +18l²p^4 sum_(j>=2) (j-1/2)^-1
                              min[1,2/(t(j-1/2))]
      <=[126+18 log(2/t)]t²p²
      <p²/1000.                                       (E16)

The head sum is at most `1+log K`, and the remaining tail is at most 2. The final expression increases with t on `(0,1/600]`; at its endpoint it is at most `270p²/360000`, using `log1200<8`.

### 5.3 A finite midpoint contour identity

For a rational function f analytic in `0<=Re r<=1`, with only polynomial growth on the vertical boundaries, the midpoint Abel–Plana identity is

    p sum_(j=1)^q f((j-1/2)p)-integral_0^1 f(r)dr
      =-i integral_0^infinity
          [f(iy)-f(-iy)-f(1+iy)+f(1-iy)]
                              /[exp(2pi q y)+1] dy.    (E17)

This identity follows by integrating the half-integer summation kernel on the upper and lower rectangles of the strip. Its poles at `(j-1/2)/q` have the midpoint residues; subtract the limiting constant kernel in each half-plane, leaving the factors `1/(exp(2pi q y)+1)` on the vertical sides. The horizontal remainders tend to zero because those factors decay exponentially against polynomial growth. The oriented vertical sides give the difference and sign in (E17). Equivalently apply the half-integer Abel–Plana formula at length q and rescale. For example `f(r)=r²` gives `-1/(12q²)`, fixing the sign; this example is not the proof of the identity.

Use (E17) with

    f(r)=2r P_l(1-r²-p²/4+p²a(1-r²)).

Its only possible poles are at `r=+-sqrt(2)`, outside the closed strip, and its growth is polynomial. The following bounds make the constant in (E17) explicit.

At r=iy, the argument of P_l is real and no larger than

    1+y²+p²(1+y²)² <=cosh[4(y+p)].

Here `a(1+y²)<=(1+y²)²`. The last inequality follows by comparing the terms in `cosh(4t)-1` to `t²+t^4+t^6` with t=y+p. The Legendre integral representation then gives

    |f(iy)-f(-iy)|<=4y exp[4l(y+p)].

Its contribution to (E17) is at most `8/(25q²)`: `exp(4lp)<2` and `2pi q-4l>5q`.

At r=1+iy, write `z=y²-2iy` and `xi=z-p²/4+p²a(z)`. Since `|1+z|>=1`,

    |a(z)|<=|z|+|z|²,
    asinh|xi|<=p²/4+8y,
    |Re xi|<=p²/4+2p²y+11y² exp(2y).                  (E18)

For the middle inequality, `|z|<=y²+2y`, hence
`|xi|<=p²/4+(1+y)^4-1`; also `(1+y)^4-1<=exp(4y)-1<=sinh(8y)`. The last inequality in (E18) follows by expanding `|z|+|z|²` and bounding its cubic and quartic terms by multiples of `y² exp(2y)`.

The complex Laplace representation gives `|P_l(w)|<=exp[l asinh|w|]`. Cauchy's formula on radius `1/l` gives `|P'_l(w)|<=exp(1)l exp[l asinh|w|]`. Since l is even, `P_l(conjugate xi)=P_l(-conjugate xi)`. The segment between xi and `-conjugate xi` stays in the disk of radius `|xi|`. Therefore

    |f(1+iy)-f(1-iy)|
      <=4 exp(lp²/4) exp(10ly)
                 [y+3lp²/4+6lp²y+33ly²].             (E19)

Use `exp(lp²/4)<2`, `2pi q-10l>5q`, and integrate the constant, linear and quadratic terms against `exp(-5qy)`. Their total is bounded by

    8[1/(25q²)+3l/(20q³)+6l/(25q^4)+66l/(125q³)]
                                                      <0.33/q².

Together with the previous boundary, (E17) is less than `1/q²` in modulus.

### 5.4 Comparing the surrogate integral to area measure

Substitute z=1-r². The surrogate integral is

    integral_0^1 P_l(phi(z)) dz,
    phi(z)=z-p²/4+p²a(z).

On `[0,1]`, `0<a'(z)<3/2`, so phi is increasing with `phi'>=1`. Its endpoints are `-p²/4` and `1+p²(a(1)-1/4)`; the total length outside `[0,1]` is `p²a(1)<p²`. On this enlarged interval `|P_l|<2`, because `arcosh(1+p²)<=2p` and `lp<=1/600`. After changing variables to phi(z), the two added endpoint integrals contribute at most `2p²`, and the Jacobian error is at most `3p²/2`. Thus

    |integral_0^1 P_l(phi(z)) dz-integral_0^1 P_l(z) dz|
                                                    <4p².

Combining this with (E16), (E17)–(E19) proves (E13), with room in the constant 6. This completes the normalization and quadrature calculation for the actual centroid nodes.

## 6. What follows for the continuum and actual discrete corrections

The zonal coefficient is `B_C(e_(l,0))=sqrt(2l+1)B_C(P_l)`. From (E13) and the addition theorem,

    ||grad u_0,zonal||_infinity
      <=(6/q²) sum_(l<=T) (2l+1)/sqrt(lambda_l)
      <=18T/q².

Together with (E12), this gives the complete author continuum bound

    ||grad u_0||_infinity <=19T/q²,
    u_0=-(-Delta)^-1 B_C.                              (E20)

The continuum correction after a reset within delta of each centroid changes by at most `2delta sqrt(M)`, since the reset residual has `epsilon_G<=2delta`. This statement changes the RHS of the continuum equation; it does not control the change in `A_X`.

There is also a useful bound for the *actual* discrete correction. Equations (E11), (E13) imply

    zeta(B_C)² <=109(1+log T)/q^4,
    zeta(B_C) <=11 sqrt(1+log T)/q².                    (E21)

For the zonal part, use `(2l+1)/lambda_l<=3/l`, giving at most `108(1+log T)/q^4`. For the aliases, the additional part is at most

    [6(1+log T)/q^4] sum_(m>=3) m^4 a^(2m)
      <=[600 a^6(1+log T)/q^4] <(1+log T)/q^4.

The series bound follows because successive terms have ratio at most `4a²`; its first term is `81a^6`. Thus (E6) gives the fully discrete, actual-centroid bound

    max_i |v_(C,i)|
      <=(209/13)(T+1)sqrt(1+log T)/q².                 (E22)

One can separate the continuum part more sharply. Let Lambda be the diagonal Laplacian matrix and

    H=Lambda^-1/2(A_C-Lambda)Lambda^-1/2.

Equation (E4) says `||H||_2<=6/19`. Hence

    max_i |v_(C,i)|
      <=19T/q²+(66/13)(T+1)sqrt(1+log T)/q².           (E23)

Indeed `||(I+H)^-1-I||_2<=6/13`, and point evaluation of the energy-normalized gradient costs at most sqrt(M). This is a rigorous square-root logarithmic *upper* loss from the available energy argument. It is not a matching lower bound for the initializer, nor evidence that the loss is necessary.

At fixed `q=600(T+1)`, neither (E22) nor (E23) yields a T-independent constant in (E3). Their usefulness for any finite degree does not change that quantifier.

## 7. A complete Euclidean route with logarithmic oversampling

The new estimates do close a weaker-cardinality acquisition proof using only exact Euclidean linear solves. This is a mathematically consequential control; it does not improve the accepted `N=O(T²)` LP result.

Set

    L=ceil(log_2(T+1)),    k=ceil(sqrt(1+L)),
    q=600(T+1)k,          N=2q²=O(T² log(T+1)),
    h=12/q,              delta=2^-24(T+1)^-8,
    I=20+10ceil(log_2(T+1)).                            (E24)

Acquire the same cells and rational centroid reset. Since `sqrt(1+log T)<=k`, (E21) and the reset estimate give

    zeta_0<=11k/q²+2delta <=Z_0:=12k/q².               (E25)

For the last inequality use `k<=T+1` and
`2delta q²/k=720000*2^-24*k*(T+1)^-6<1`. The first cell/cost construction is unchanged except for the charged extra factor k² in N.

Use the exact minimum-Euclidean correction at each step, the same rational Cayley update, and the same deterministic distinct bounded-height reset. While nodes and paths stay in the anchor balls, (E5)–(E6) give

    ||v||_X²<2 zeta²,             max_i|v_i|<2(T+1)zeta.

Globally `|grad P|_infinity<=(T+1)||grad P||_2`. For a fixed axis a, the trigonometric Bernstein bound gives

    ||L_a²P||_infinity<=T||L_aP||_infinity
                                <=T(T+1)||grad P||_2.

Taking the great-circle axis for each tangent direction bounds the spherical Hessian operator norm by the same quantity. If `max|v_i|<=1`, the Cayley parameter-to-angle error is at most `|v_i|³/12`. Thus its full remainder after subtracting the exact linear equation, averaged over nodes, has H1-dual norm at most

    (T+1)² ||v||_X².

The reset adds at most `2delta` in this norm by the local gradient sampling bound. Therefore

    zeta_next<=2(T+1)² zeta²+2delta.                   (E26)

Because `2(T+1)² Z_0=24/(360000k)<1/4`, the inductive basin gives

    zeta_j<=4^-j Z_0+(8/3)delta.                       (E27)

The precision floor also remains below `1/[8(T+1)²]`, so the quadratic term is always at most zeta/4. The total allotted distance from a true centroid is

    delta+sum_(j<I)[2(T+1)zeta_j+delta]
      <=(8/3)(T+1)Z_0
             +[1+I+(16/3)(T+1)I]delta.

The first term divided by h is `32(T+1)k/(12q)=1/225`. Since `I<=30(T+1)` and `k<=T+1`, the precision contribution divided by h is less than
`10000*2^-24*(T+1)^-4<0.001`. Total travel is less than `0.01h`; this closes the simultaneous path and sampling induction. Every maximum step is less than one. No assumption of Newton convergence is inserted before the basin calculation.

At the specified I, (E27) is below `beta_T/[16(T+1)]`, with `beta_T=1/[2048(T+1)^5]`. Consequently the final normalized harmonic mean is at most beta_T/16, and the feature Gram matrix is at least `(13/19)I` by the same rotation sampling gate. Distinctness and exact rational unit length follow from the accepted reset and Cayley identities.

All operations can be exact rational operations in the unnormalized rational harmonic basis. The derivative normal matrix is full rank by (E4); Gaussian elimination supplies its inverse action in polynomial bit time. A straightforward step uses `O(NM²+M³)` rational arithmetic operations in addition to harmonic evaluation/reset costs. Rational inputs have the existing `O(T log(T+1))` basis height and `O(log(T+1))` reset coordinate height; sums of distinct denominators and intermediate elimination are charged polynomially, rather than assumed to have single-term bit length. There are O(log(T+1)) steps. The output contains O(T² log(T+1)) directions with O(log(T+1)) coordinate bits. This corollary uses no numerical LP proposal and no standard LP primitive, but it has an extra logarithmic node factor and large constants.

## 8. Exact unresolved optimal-order boundary

The first unresolved lemma is (E3) for the actual discrete inverse. A sufficient stronger result would be a T-uniform bound for the Neumann action of

    E_C=Lambda^-1(A_C-Lambda)

in a function norm that dominates `||grad u||_infinity` and contains the actual continuum potential from (E20). The energy norm supplies only (E23). Simply replacing `A_C` by Lambda, invoking a smooth spectral cutoff, or asserting that an L2 spectral perturbation is small in the gradient maximum norm is not a proof.

Even an independently gated proof of (E3) would settle only the initial correction. Later Euclidean Newton residuals are quadratic retraction errors plus resets; they are no longer the original cell-centroid quadrature functional. Exact longitude cancellation need not remain true after the canonical discrete solve moves points. A full optimal-order theorem needs a closed residual class or a different invariant controlling those later inverse actions. The LP argument already supplies such a product displacement bound for arbitrary current residuals; (E9) explains why its replacement by a particular Euclidean inverse is a substantive issue.

**Resumable next step:** independently reconstruct (E13), especially the finite half-integer contour identity, the exact normalized centroid remainder (E15), and the complex endpoint bounds. Then investigate `E_C` on the actual zonal-plus-polar-alias majorant from (E11), (E13), rather than on arbitrary residuals. A viable proof must bound the entire discrete inverse and identify whether the resulting majorant is closed under the next Cayley remainder. A counterexample must use these legal normalized centroid nodes or a later specified trajectory; an arbitrary bounded-field RHS such as (E9) does not qualify.
