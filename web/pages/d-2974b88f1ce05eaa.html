# Exact boundary of fibre-affine nonlinear Schur descent

Worker: hessian_bridge12, cycle12, 7 October 2026. Status: a characteristic-zero structural reduction and exact certificates; no HC4 resolution and no literature-wide priority claim.

## Question and legal interface

Can the published Meng–Yang five-variable potential be reduced to a four-variable constant-Hessian counterexample by a polynomial change that fixes the two base variables and is affine-linear in the three dual variables, followed by elimination of the polynomial unit pivot `t=A`?

The allowed transformations in this note are exactly

\[
 (x,y,Y)\longmapsto (x,y,V(x,y)Y+d(x,y)),\qquad
 V\in\mathrm{GL}_3(k[x,y]),\quad \det V\in k^\times,
\]

whose first coordinate is `t=A+delta(x,y)`. The residual two coordinates may have arbitrary polynomial base-dependent linear changes, translations, and shears by `t`. Elimination means the partial Legendre critical value at a **constant** conjugate level `sigma`. General nonlinear changes in the residual dual variables, changes mixing the base and dual variables, and non-coordinate symplectic reductions are outside this interface.

Throughout, `k` has characteristic zero. Statements about singleton geometric fibres and properness are over `C`.

## Input polynomials

Put `u=1+xy`, `v=xy`, `Y=(p,q,r)^T`, and

\[
\begin{aligned}
A&=u^3p+3xu^2q-x^3r,\\
B&=y^2u(4+3xy)p+
  (y+3xy^2(4+3xy))q+(2x-3x^2y)r,\\
f_{L,M,N}&=LA^2+MA+NB,\qquad LN\ne0.
\end{aligned}
\]

The known family has constant Hessian determinant `8LN^4`. Our reduction below does not require recomputing that five-variable determinant.

The Bezout identity

\[
 (1-3v+6v^2)u^3-x^3 y^3(6v^2+15v+10)=1
\]

makes the row defining `A` unimodular. One determinant-one completion is

\[
U=\begin{pmatrix}
u^3&3xu^2&-x^3\\
0&1&0\\
-y^3(6v^2+15v+10)&0&1-3v+6v^2
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

With `(t,rho,eta)^T=UY`, direct polynomial substitution gives

\[
 B=\beta_t t+\gamma_1\rho+\gamma_2\eta,
\]

where

\[
\begin{aligned}
\beta_t&=y^2(6v^2+15v+4),\\
\gamma&=(-yP(v),xQ(v)),\\
P(v)&=18v^5+81v^4+120v^3+60v^2-1,\\
Q(v)&=(v+1)(v+2).
\end{aligned}
\]

## Theorem 1: every permitted completion has the same two-variable coefficient interface

Every permitted completion of `A` differs from `U` by a matrix

\[
 VU^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\h&C\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad C\in\mathrm{GL}_2(k[x,y]),\quad \det C\in k^\times.
\]

This follows immediately from the common first row: its product with `U^{-1}` is `(1,0,0)`. Including all translations, write the new coordinates as

\[
t'=A+\delta,
\qquad r'=hA+C\binom{\rho}{\eta}+b.
\]

Set `G=gamma C^{-1}` and `beta'=beta_t-Gh`. In these coordinates,

\[
 f=L(t'-\delta)^2+(M+N\beta')(t'-\delta)+NG r'-NGb.
\]

The unit-pivot equation for `f-sigma t'` has the polynomial solution

\[
t'^*=\delta+\frac{\sigma-M-N\beta'}{2L}.
\]

Its critical value is therefore exactly

\[
\psi_\sigma=h_\sigma(x,y)+NG_1(x,y)r'_1+NG_2(x,y)r'_2,
\]

\[
h_\sigma=-\frac{(\sigma-M-N\beta')^2}{4L}-NGb-\sigma\delta.
\]

Thus changing the completion, translations, or `t`-shears never removes the two-variable coefficient map `G`. They only change it by a residual invertible polynomial linear gauge and change the base potential. A constant determinant of `C` can be normalized to1 by a constant residual rescaling; it adds no new collision possibilities.

## Theorem 2: the descended HC4 problem is exactly a plane Keller problem in this class

For every base polynomial `h` and every two-variable `G`,

\[
\operatorname{Hess}\psi=
\begin{pmatrix}
 *&N(DG)^T\\
 NDG&0
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
\det\operatorname{Hess}\psi=N^4(\det DG)^2.
\]

Consequently nonzero constant Hessian determinant is equivalent to `det DG` being a nonzero constant. Here we use the characteristic-zero polynomial domain: a polynomial whose square is a nonzero constant is itself a nonzero constant.

Under this Keller assumption the gradient has the exact form

\[
\nabla\psi(z,r)=(\nabla h(z)+N(DG(z))^Tr,\ NG(z)),\qquad z=(x,y).
\]

It is noninjective if and only if `G` is noninjective. One direction follows from the last two outputs and invertibility of `DG`. For the other, if `G(z_+)=G(z_-)` at distinct bases, choose any common first output `a` and set

\[
r_\pm=(N(DG(z_\pm))^T)^{-1}(a-\nabla h(z_\pm)).
\]

These are distinct full source points with the same gradient. The inverse matrix is polynomial in the base because its determinant is a nonzero constant.

The gradient has a polynomial inverse if and only if `G` has one. If `G^{-1}` is known, first recover `z=G^{-1}(Z/N)`, then recover `r` by the displayed inverse matrix. Conversely a polynomial inverse of the full gradient supplies a polynomial right inverse of `G` by evaluating the first output block at zero; that right inverse is dominant, and equality on its dense image proves it is also a left inverse.

This is a structural equivalence, not an independence or novelty claim for the plane conjecture. It explains why residual-affine elimination returns to JC2 rather than transporting the known three-variable obstruction directly.

## Theorem 3: the coefficient maps are precisely a normalized singleton-zero class

The pinned prior gives a polynomial `C0 in SL2(k[x,y])` with

\[
\gamma C_0=(y,2x).
\]

Its exact entries and determinant are independently checked in `verify_fibre_affine_reduction.py`; no numerical interpolation is used. Hence all determinant-one residual gauges give exactly

\[
G=(y,2x)D,\qquad D\in\mathrm{SL}_2(k[x,y]).
\]

In particular,

\[
(G_1,G_2)=(x,y),\qquad G(0)=0,
\qquad \det DG(0)=-2.
\]

The origin is the whole reduced zero fibre. The Keller condition, if attained, must be `det DG=-2` everywhere.

Conversely, let `G` be any polynomial pair with `(G1,G2)=(x,y)` and `det DG(0)=-2`. Write `G=(y,2x)D0`, possible because each component lies in `(x,y)`. Then `det D0(0)=1`. Since `det D0-1` vanishes at0 and the ideals agree, choose polynomials `a,b` such that

\[
\det D_0-1=aG_1+bG_2.
\]

The row `(y,2x)` has syzygy column `n=(-2x,y)^T`. Set

\[
D=D_0+n(b,-a).
\]

The row product remains `G`, and a two-column determinant expansion gives

\[
\det D=\det D_0-aG_1-bG_2=1.
\]

This is a constructive polynomial completion, valid in every degree. There is no additional restriction hidden in the original toric coefficient row.

Over `C`, a Keller map with zero fibre exactly `{0}` has a reduced zero fibre by its nonzero Jacobian; thus its component ideal equals `(x,y)`. After normalizing its Jacobian to `-2`, it lies in this class. Therefore the all-degree residual-linear problem is exactly the singleton-zero subclass of the plane Keller problem.

A finite map in this class cannot be a counterexample: finite etale fibres have constant degree, while the zero fibre has degree1. Hence any successful member would necessarily be nonproper and have a singleton zero fibre. We have not proved that such a nonproper Keller map is impossible.

## Decisive obstruction, nearest prior, and missing lemma

The parent/root and hessian_check12 independently prove a stronger affine obstruction: for `lambda A²` plus **any** fibre-linear polynomial and any base polynomial, `lambda!=0`, the positive-degree Hessian coefficients span all15 symmetric directions. Thus affine reparametrization plus quadratic repair cannot produce a constant scalar or mixed Hessian entry. Our smaller independently checked family certificate is

\[
\det K=2^{22}3^{13}7 L^{10}N^5,
\]

for the15 positive monomial rows recorded in `hesse_span_certificate.json`. `N!=0` is sufficient for this particular minor, not necessary for the stronger property. The all-degree analytic extension belongs to root's joint work and is not our separate discovery.

The new deduction isolated here is the **universality of the affine-dual completion** and the **constructive converse for its maximal-ideal coefficient class**. The determinant and toric normalizer are prior. The reviewed repository sources do not state this exact converse; literature-wide novelty is unresolved, and no claim of priority is made.

The load-bearing missing lemma is now precise:

> Does a complex polynomial map `G=(G1,G2)` with `det DG=-2` and `(G1,G2)=(x,y)` necessarily have a polynomial inverse?

Proving that statement would close every permitted affine-dual completion in all degrees. A counterexample to it would already refute JC2 and, by Theorem 2, produce HC4. The certificate does not settle the lemma. General transformations nonlinear in the residual dual variables may escape this equivalence and remain a separate search space.

## Downstream use and next falsifiable step

Use the three exact gates before expanding any new completion: normalize its residual gauge; compute `det DG`; check whether it actually retains a collision. An all-degree residual-linear search can be described directly as the singleton-zero plane Keller problem, and a candidate must demonstrate nonproperness. The base potential cannot repair failure of `det DG` or create a collision when `G` is injective.

The next independent step is to audit Theorem 3 against known singleton-fibre Keller criteria and to test a genuinely nonlinear residual-dual transformation against the full Hessian chain rule. Such a chart must leave the affine-dual interface, have a polynomial critical equation, keep a nonzero constant reduced Hessian determinant, and retain a collision. A numerical rank, a formal critical solution, or a collision on only one source slice is insufficient.
