Abstract

Multi-metric learning techniques are designed to enhance optimization processes, such as imputing missing data in high-dimensional, multimodal datasets, where multiple local distance functions are defined. Here, we first outline the theoretical foundations of multi-distance metric learning and relevant concepts from group theory. Convergence results, followed by experimental evaluations on the LINCS dataset, including comparisons with two other methods are presented. Finally, an ablation study to illustrate how incorporating each additional metric contributes to improved overall performance is provided.

1 Single-metric and multi-metric learning

Metric tensor (or simply metric) is an additional structure on a manifold (such as a surface) that allows defining distances and angles, just as the inner product on a Euclidean space allows defining distances and angles there. More precisely, a metric tensor at a point p of a manifold, \(\mathcal {M}\), is a bilinear form defined on the tangent space at p, say \(T_p\) (that is, a bilinear function that maps pairs of tangent vectors to real numbers), and a metric tensor on the manifold consists of a metric tensor at each point p that varies smoothly with p.

The metric tensor, in general, is a function \(g_{ij}\) through which the distance between any two points in a given space can be computed. Its components can be viewed as multiplication factors which must be placed in front of the differential displacements \(dx_i\) in a generalized Pythagorean theorem:

$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 = g_{11} dx_1^2 +g_{12} dx_1\, dx_2 + g_{22} dx_2^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (1)

In Euclidean space, \(g_{ij}=\delta _{ij}\), the Kronecker delta function, reproducing the usual form of the Pythagorean theorem.

A single-metric learns a metric tensor, \({\textbf{M}}\), such that distances are measured as

$$\begin{aligned} \text {dist}^2(x_i, x_j) = \Vert {\textbf{L}} (x_i-x_j)\Vert _F^2= \Vert x_i-x_j\Vert ^2_{{\textbf{M}}} \equiv (x_i-x_j)^\top \, {\textbf{M}} \, (x_i - x_j), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2)

where \({\textbf{L}}\) is a linear transformation, \({\textbf{M}}\in {\mathbb {R}}^d \times {\mathbb {R}}^d\) forms a symmetric and positive definite matrix, and \({\textbf{M}} = {\textbf{L}}^\top {\textbf{L}}\), i.e. \({\textbf{L}}\) forms a Cholesky decomposition for metric tensor \({\textbf{M}}.\) Matrix \({\textbf{M}}\) is referred to as the Mahalanobis distance coming from the dot-product on \({\mathbb {R}}^d\), which is defined as follows:

Definition 1

(Matrix Mahalanobis Distance) Let \(d \in \mathbb {N}\) and \( \textbf{M}\in \mathcal {S}_d(\mathbb {R})_0^+\), where \(\mathcal {S}_d(\mathbb {R})_0^+\) is the set of positive semidefinite matrices of dimension d. The Mahalanobis distance corresponding to the matrix \(\textbf{M}\) is \(d_{\textbf{M}}: \mathbb {R}^d\times \mathbb {R}^d \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\), given by

$$\begin{aligned} d_{\textbf{M}}(x,y):= \sqrt{(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})^\top \textbf{M}~(\textbf{x}-\textbf{y})}, \end{aligned}$$

for \(\textbf{x}, \textbf{y}\in \mathbb {R}^d\).

Two additional properties of Mahalanobis distances over normed spaces, on top of coincidence, symmetry and triangular inequality are: 

                (i)

Homogeneousness: \(d(ax,ay)= |a|\, d(x, y)\), for \(a\in {\mathbb {R}}\), \(x,y\in {\mathbb {R}}^d\),

                (ii)

Translation invariance: \(d(x, y) = d(x + z, y + z)\), for \(x,y,z\in {\mathbb {R}}^d\).

There are several classical approaches for finding such a metric tensor where the metric is given by the inverse covariance matrix of the training data; Metric tensor in this case involves within mode relations as well as cross-mode clusters, i.e. if \({\textbf{S}}_{\text{ w }}\) and \({\textbf{S}}_{\text{ c }}\) denote the within mode and cross-mode clusters, respectively, then \({\textbf{M}} ={\textbf{S}}_{\text{ w }}^{-1} {\textbf{S}}_{\text{ c }} {\textbf{S}}_{\text{ w }}^{-1}\) [19]. Learning a single global metric tensor is not compatible with the nature of many problems. Therefore, the ideas of learning local metric tensor(s) [19, 36, 37] have been developed which yielded to multi-metric learning.

The tensor product (also called outer product) of vectors forms a higher-order tensor. Given K vectors \(\textbf{u}^{(1)} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1}\), \(\textbf{u}^{(2)} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_2}, \dots , \textbf{u}^{(K)} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_K}\), their tensor product yields a K-th-order tensor \(\varvec{\mathcal {X}} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1 \times N_2 \times \cdots \times N_K}\) with elements:

$$\begin{aligned} x_{i_1 i_2 \dots i_K} = u^{(1)}_{i_1} u^{(2)}_{i_2} \cdots u^{(K)}_{i_K}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (3)

This operation is denoted as \(\mathcal {X} = {\textbf{u}}^{(1)} \otimes {\textbf{u}}^{(2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes {\textbf{u}}^{(K)}\), where \(\mathcal {X}\) is a rank-1 tensor.

The mode-n product (also called tensor-matrix product) is a fundamental operation in tensor algebra that multiplies a tensor by a matrix along a specific mode [23]. Given an Kth-order tensor \(\mathcal {X} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1 \times N_2 \times \cdots \times N_K}\) and a matrix \({\textbf{U}} \in \mathbb {R}^{J \times N_n}\), the mode-n product \(\mathcal {Y} = \mathcal {X} \times _{\text {n}}{\textbf{U}}\) yields a tensor \(\mathcal {Y} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1 \times \cdots \times N_{n-1} \times J \times N_{n+1} \times \cdots \times N_K}\) with elements \( y_{i_1 \dots i_{n-1} j i_{n+1} \dots i_K} = \sum _{i_n=1}^{N_n} x_{i_1 i_2 \dots i_K} \, u_{j i_n}. \)

The mode-n product satisfies several important properties. For distinct modes, the products commute: \(\mathcal {X} \times _m {\textbf{A}} \times _n {\textbf{B}} = \mathcal {X} \times _n {\textbf{B}} \times _m {\textbf{A}}\) for \(m \ne n\). For the same mode: \(\mathcal {X} \times _n {\textbf{A}} \times _n {\textbf{B}} = \mathcal {X} \times _n ({\textbf{BA}})\). The result is equivalent to matrix multiplication of the mode-n unfolding: \(\mathcal {Y} = \mathcal {X} \times _n {\textbf{U}} \iff {\textbf{Y}}_{(n)} = {\textbf{U}} {\textbf{X}}_{(n)}\). These operations form the foundation for tensor decompositions such as CANDECOMP/PARAFAC (CP) decomposition [13, 18] and Tucker [35], which are widely used in multiway data analysis.

The CP decomposition approximates a tensor as a sum of rank-one tensors. For an Kth-order tensor \(\mathcal {X} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1 \times N_2 \times \cdots \times N_K}\), the CP decomposition with R components is \( \mathcal {X} \approx \sum _{r=1}^R \lambda _r \cdot {\textbf{u}}_r^{(1)} \otimes {\textbf{u}}_r^{(2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes {\textbf{u}}_r^{(K)}, \) where \(\lambda _r \in \mathbb {R}\) are weights, and \({\textbf{u}}_r^{(n)} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_n}\) are factor vectors for mode n.

The Tucker decomposition [35] provides a more flexible tensor representation by introducing a core tensor that captures interactions between components across different modes. For an Kth-order tensor \(\mathcal {X} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_1 \times N_2 \times \cdots \times K_N}\), the Tucker decomposition is:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {X} \approx \mathcal {G} \times _1 {\textbf{U}}^{(1)} \times _2 {\textbf{U}}^{(2)} \times _3 \cdots \times _K {\textbf{U}}^{(K)}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (4)

where \(\mathcal {G} \in \mathbb {R}^{R_1 \times R_2 \times \cdots \times R_K}\), with \(R_i\le N_i, \forall i=1, \ldots , K\), is the core tensor and \({\textbf{U}}^{(n)} \in \mathbb {R}^{N_n \times R_n}\) are the factor matrices for each mode n. Key properties of Tucker decomposition include flexibility, interaction capture, and dimensionality reduction. The core tensor \(\mathcal {G}\) captures multi-way interactions between components and when \(R_n < N_n\), it provides compression [3].

The CP decomposition can be viewed as a special case of Tucker decomposition where the core tensor \(\mathcal {G}\) is super-diagonal (\(g_{r_1 r_2 \dots r_K} = \lambda _r\) if \(r_1 = r_2 = \cdots = r_K = r,\) and 0 otherwise) and all modes have the same number of components (\(R_1 = R_2 = \cdots = R_K = R\)).

In terms of associtivity properties of Tucker product [14, 23], one may note the mode-wise associativity of the mode-n product for a fixed mode n with respect to matrix multiplication, i.e.,

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathcal {X} \times _n A) \times _n B = \mathcal {X} \times _n (BA), \end{aligned}$$

                    (5)

provided that the dimensions of A and B are compatible. This property follows directly from the definition of the mode-n product as a matrix multiplication on the mode-n unfolding of \(\mathcal {X}\). For distinct modes \(m \ne n\), the mode-wise products commute, hence cross-mode commutativity,

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathcal {X} \times _m A) \times _n B = (\mathcal {X} \times _n B) \times _m A. \end{aligned}$$

                    (6)

Hence, the order of applying mode transformations is irrelevant.

Although the mode-wise products commute, the Tucker product as a whole is not globally associative in the same sense as matrix multiplication since each mode product operates on a different dimension of \(\mathcal {X}\).

We utilize a regularized Tucker decomposition form of a tensor to generalize the definition of Mahalanobis distance over the space of tensors [5]:

Definition 2

(Tensor Mahalanobis Distance) Let \(\mathcal X\in \mathbb {R}^{N_1\times N_2\times \cdots \times N_K}\). Consider the multilinear transformation \( \varphi :\mathbb {R}^{N_1\times N_2\times \cdots \times N_K}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^{N_1\times N_2\times \cdots \times N_K}\), with

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi (\mathcal X)= \mathcal X\times _1 {\textbf{L}}^{(1)}\times _2 {\textbf{L}}^{(2)}\cdots \times _K {\textbf{L}}^{(K)}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (7)

where the square matrices \({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )}\in \mathbb {R}^{N_\ell \times N_\ell }\), for \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\), are called the \(\ell \)-mode matrices. The squared Mahalanobis distance can be computed as:

$$\begin{aligned} d_M(\mathcal X_i, \mathcal X_j)&:= \big \Vert \varphi (\mathcal X_i)- \varphi (\mathcal X_j)\big \Vert _F^2= \big \Vert (\mathcal X_i -\mathcal X_j)\times _1 {\textbf{L}}^{(1)} \cdots \times _K {\textbf{L}}^{(K)}\big \Vert _F^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (8)

In other words, finding Mahalanobis distance of two tensors is equivalent to finding local matrix Mahalanobis distances over each mode of the tensor.

2 Algebraic foundation

2.1 Reductive groups and algebraic group actions

In this section, we summarize key concepts from group theory needed to understand the Kempf-Ness theorem. We start by introducing the representation theory of reductive groups [27, 31]. Examples of such application can be found in [4, 28], to name but a few. Let G be a general linear group. The formal definition of reductive group is given as:

Definition 3

(Reductive Group) A reductive group is a linear algebraic group G, over an algebraically closed field F, that satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:

The radical of the connected component \(G^0\) of the unit element of G is an algebraic torus;

the unipotent radical of the group \(G^0\) is trivial; or

the group \(G^0\) is a product of closed normal subgroups \(H_1\) and \(H_2\) that are a semi-simple algebraic group and an algebraic torus, respectively.

Intuitively, reductivity can be thought as the property of a linear algebraic group G the guarantees a representation for the group G.

For a real (or complex) vector space X, the general linear group, \(\text {GL}(X)\), and special linear group, \(\text {SL}(X)\), are reductive groups and so are their products. In general, \(\text {GL}_d(X)\) is the set of \(d\times d\) invertible matrices over X, together with the matrix multiplication operation and \(\text {SL}_d(X)\) is a subset of \(\text {GL}_d(X)\) consisting of those elements whose determinants are unitary. An \(n_1\times n_2\times n_3\) real tensor can be thought of as an element in

$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal{X}}:=X_{n_1}\otimes X_{n_2}\otimes X_{n_3} \cong \mathbb {R}^{n_1\times n_2\times n_3}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (9)

where \(X_{n_1}\), \(X_{n_2}\) and \(X_{n_3}\) are vector spaces of dimension \((n_1,n_2,n_3), \) which itself is the representation of the reductive group

$$\begin{aligned} {\mathcal {G}}:=\text {GL}(X_{n_1})\times \text {GL}(X_{n_2})\times \text {GL}(X_{n_3}). \end{aligned}$$

                    (10)

Lemma 4

Let \( \psi _{\text{ l }}:\; {\mathcal {G}}\times {\mathcal {X}}\rightarrow {\mathcal {X}},\) be defined as

$$\begin{aligned} \psi \big ((A_1,A_2,A_3),\;\mathcal {X}\big ) := (A_1,A_2,A_3)\cdot \mathcal {X} := \mathcal {X}\times _1 A_1 \times _2 A_2 \times _3 A_3. \end{aligned}$$

                    (11)

Then \( \psi _{\text{ l }}\) is a left group action of \({\mathcal {G}}\) on \({\mathcal {X}}\).

Proof

We must check the two group action axioms. Let \(e=(I_{I_1},I_{I_2},I_{I_3})\in {\mathcal {G}}\) be the identity. By definition of the mode product and the identity matrix, \( e\cdot \mathcal {X} = \mathcal {X}\times _1 I_{I_1}\times _2 I_{I_2}\times _3 I_{I_3} = \mathcal {X},\) so the identity property holds.

To verify the compatibility, let \(g=(A_1,A_2,A_3)\) and \(h=(\widehat{A}_1,\widehat{A}_2,\widehat{A}_3)\) be elements of \({\mathcal {G}}\). Using the associativity property of the mode-\(n\) product \((\mathcal {Y}\times _n B)\times _n C=\mathcal {Y}\times _n (CB)\) for each mode \(n\), we compute

$$\begin{aligned} g\cdot \big (h\cdot \mathcal {X}\big )&= g\cdot \big (\mathcal {X}\times _1\widehat{A}_1\times _2\widehat{A}_2\times _3\widehat{A}_3\big )\\&= \big (\mathcal {X}\times _1\widehat{A}_1\times _2\widehat{A}_2\times _3\widehat{A}_3\big ) \times _1 A_1\times _2 A_2\times _3 A_3\\&= \mathcal {X}\times _1 (A_1\widehat{A}_1)\times _2 (A_2\widehat{A}_2)\times _3 (A_3\widehat{A}_3)\\&= (gh)\cdot \mathcal {X}, \end{aligned}$$

where \(gh=(A_1\widehat{A}_1,\;A_2\widehat{A}_2,\;A_3\widehat{A}_3)\) is the product in \({\mathcal {G}}\). Hence the compatibility (associativity) axiom holds, and \(\psi _{\text{ l }}\) is a left action. \(\square \)

Similarly, one may define \(\phi _{\text{ r }}:{\mathcal {X}}\times {\mathcal {G}}\rightarrow {\mathcal {X}}\) by

$$\begin{aligned} \phi _{\text{ r }}( \mathcal X, (A_1,A_2,A_3)):= \mathcal {X}\cdot (A_1,A_2,A_3):=\mathcal {X}\times _1 A_1^{-1}\times _2 A_2^{-1}\times _3 A_3^{-1}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (12)

Because each \(A_i\) is invertible, one checks \((\mathcal {X}\cdot g)\cdot h=\mathcal {X}\cdot (gh), \) so \(\phi _{\text{ r }}\) is a right group action of \({\mathcal {G}}\) on \({\mathcal {X}}\).

Therefore, the group \({\mathcal {G}}\) acts by linear transformations defined in Eq. (11), meaning that \({\mathcal {X}}\) is indeed a representation of the reductive group \({\mathcal {G}}\).

As mentioned above, a three-way tensor of the size \(n_1\times n_2\times n_3\) is an element in the representation \({\mathcal {X}}\cong \mathbb {R}^{n_1\times n_2\times n_3}\) of the reductive group \({\mathcal {G}}\). For two coupled tensors, having one mode in common, we get the representation

$$\begin{aligned} (X_{n_1}\otimes X_{n_2}\otimes X_{n_3})\oplus (X_{n_3}\otimes Y\otimes Z), \end{aligned}$$

                    (13)

of the group

$$\begin{aligned} \text {GL}(X_{n_1})\times \text {GL}(X_{n_2}) \times \text {GL}(X_{n_3})\times \text {GL}(Y)\times \text {GL}(Z). \end{aligned}$$

                    (14)

Using the above framework, two coupled tensors can be identified with the representation

$$\begin{aligned} (X_{n_1}\otimes X_{n_2}\otimes X_{n_3})\bigoplus \limits _{\ell =1}^3 \left( S^2 X_{n_\ell } \otimes X_{n_\ell }\right) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (15)

of the group \({\mathcal {G}}\times {\mathcal {G}}\), with \({\mathcal {G}}\) given in Eq. (10). These reformulation of the problem induces metrics with which the sparse matrices could be optimally completed. For the remainder of the section, we assume that the data under consideration lies in a representation of a reductive group G.

2.1.1 Minimal solution using a proper group action and Kempf–Ness theorem

In this section, we outline the properties and results of The Kempf-Ness theorem [29] for reductive groups acting on \({\mathbb {R}}\), with the group action defined in Sect. 2.1. It shows that there is essentially a unique minimal solution for the main problem we later propose in Sect. 3.

The classical Kempf–Ness theorem establishes a fundamental connection between algebraic geometry and symplectic geometry for complex reductive groups, relating closed orbits in a representation space to minima of a norm function invariant under a maximal compact subgroup. In many applications, however, one works with real reductive Lie groups acting on real vector spaces.

In this setting, a closely analogous result holds: the orbit of a vector is closed if and only if the squared norm function, defined with respect to a K-invariant inner product, attains a global minimum. Moreover, the set of minimizers of this function coincides with a single orbit under the maximal compact subgroup. This result, often referred to as the Real Kempf–Ness theorem, allows one to transfer geometric insights from the complex case to real representation spaces [9, 10, Theorem 1.1].

A vector \(v \in V\) is stable if and only if the function

$$ \varphi : G \rightarrow \mathbb {R}, \quad \varphi (g) = \Vert g \cdot v \Vert ^2 $$

attains a minimum. Equivalently, v is stable if its G-orbit is closed [22].

Theorem 5

(Complex Kempf–Ness [22]) Let G be a complex reductive group with maximal compact subgroup \(K \subset G\) and let V be a G-representation with K-invariant Hermitian norm. For a nonzero vector \(v\in V\), if \(\varphi (g) = \Vert g \cdot v\Vert ^2\) admits a critical point, then it is an absolute minimum. Moreover, the set of critical points forms a single K-orbit.

Theorem 6

(Real Kempf–Ness) Let G be a real reductive Lie group with Cartan decomposition \(G = K \exp (\mathfrak p)\), where \(K\) is a maximal compact subgroup of \(G\). Suppose G acts linearly on a finite-dimensional real vector space V preserving a K-invariant inner product. For nonzero \(v\in V\), define \(\varphi (g) = \Vert g\cdot v\Vert ^2\). Then 

                      1.

The orbit \(G \cdot v\) is closed in V.

                      2.

\(\varphi \) attains a global minimum on G.

Furthermore, if these conditions hold, the set of minimizers of \(\varphi \) is exactly a single K-orbit in \(G \cdot v\) [9, 10, Theorem 1.1].

The Kempf-Ness theorem can be thought of as a non-commutative duality theory paralleling the linear programming duality given by Farkas lemma [26] which corresponds to the commutative world. The theorem, which is a result of the geodesic convexity of the mapping \(\varphi \) implies that there is a “unique” metric, which is the Euclidean metric “after” the change of coordinates given by the group action, here g.

To avoid a degenerated case, in the absence of any critical point, one may choose a slightly smaller group G, e.g. \(\text {SL}\), special linear group, as opposed to \(\text {GL}\), the group \({\mathbb {G}}_m\), along with its products which are called algebraic tori group, or utilize a regularization that is compatible with the theory setup. Therefore, the choice of G does indeed determine the optimal metric. It is noteworthy that there exist arbitrary change of coordinates that may be used in order to normalize the tensor data; however, they could change certain characteristics such as rank of the tensor which is of main importance.

2.1.2 Determining optimal metric

We now consider the reductive group \({\mathcal {G}}\) which acts on the set \({\mathcal {X}}\) by the group action defined in Eq. (11). For a non-zero vector \({\textbf{v}}\in \mathbb {R}^m\), if there is any critical point for the real-valued map, \(\varphi (g)=\Vert g\cdot {\textbf{v}}\Vert \), for \(g\in {\mathcal {G}}\), by Theorem (5) it has to be a minimum. However, it may not be straightforward to either verify the existence of a critical point, or to find it. We now study this general result for specific case of symmetric tensors and matrices.

Next, we assume m data points \({\textbf{y}}_1,{\textbf{y}}_2,\dots ,{\textbf{y}}_m \in X_n\), with respective mean 0 and an invertible covariance matrix, \({\textbf{M}}\). We now consider the matrix whose columns are created by these data points, i.e. \({\textbf{Y}}=[{\textbf{y}}_1 \cdots {\textbf{y}}_m]\in {\mathcal {Y}}:= X_n\otimes X_m\), together with the mapping \(\varphi :\text {SL}(X_n)\rightarrow \mathbb {R},\) defined by \(\varphi ({\textbf{G}})=\Vert {\textbf{G}}\cdot {\textbf{Y}}\Vert ^2\). In Lemma (7) and the following Remark (8), we show that the function \(\varphi \) has a critical point, namely \(\bar{{\textbf{G}}}=\textbf{M}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\), which is unique by Theorem (5). If the data points \({\textbf{y}}_1,{\textbf{y}}_2,\dots ,{\textbf{y}}_m\) are evenly distributed, the metric induced by matrix \({\textbf{M}}\) is indeed the Mahalanobis distance. It should be noted, however, that defining the Mahalanobis distance does not require evenly distributed data; such a distribution simply guarantees that the metric behaves consistently in all directions.

For the following Lemma, we consider the action of subgroup \(\text {SL}(X)\subseteq \text {GL}(X)\) on the set X by multiplication. We let \({\mathcal {Y}}:=X_n\otimes X_m\cong {\mathbb {R}}^{n\times m}\) and we consider a non-zero element \({\textbf{Y}}\in {\mathcal {Y}}\), which assumes an SVD form, i.e. \({\textbf{Y}}={\textbf{U}} {\mathbf{\Sigma }}{\textbf{V}}^\top \), for some orthogonal matrices \({\textbf{U}}\in \text {SO}_n\), \({\textbf{V}}\in \text {SO}_m\) and some diagonal matrix \({\mathbf{\Sigma }}\in {\mathbb {R}}^{n\times m}\). We now have the following lemma:

Lemma 7

(Critical points of the \(\text {SL}\) group action) Let \({\textbf{Y}}\in {\mathcal {Y}}\), where \(rank ({\textbf{Y}})\) \(=n<m,\) and its singular value decomposition \({\textbf{U}}{\mathbf{\Sigma }}{\textbf{V}}^\top \) be given. Given \(\varphi :\text {SL}(X_n)\rightarrow {\mathbb {R}}\), with \(\varphi ({\textbf{G}})= \Vert {\textbf{G}}\cdot {\textbf{Y}}\Vert _F^2, \) then \(\bar{{\textbf{G}}}\) with \(\bar{{\textbf{G}}}:={\mathbf{\Lambda }}{\textbf{U}}^\top \), where \({\mathbf{\Lambda }}=\root n \of {\det ({\textbf{Y}})}{\mathbf{\Sigma }}_n^{-1}\) and square matrix \({\mathbf{\Sigma }}_n\) (consisting of non-zero singular values \(\{\sigma _i\}_{i=1}^n\)), is a critical point for the norm function \(\varphi \), and is a unique absolute minimum, up to some normalization factor.

Proof

First, since \(rank ({\textbf{Y}})=n<m\), therefore, \(\sigma _i\)’s and \(\det ({\textbf{Y}})\) are non-zero. Considering the function \(\varphi ({\textbf{G}}) = \Vert {\textbf{G}}\cdot {\textbf{Y}}\Vert _F^2\) which acts by multiplication, i.e. \(\varphi ({\textbf{G}}) =\Vert {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}\Vert ^2_F=\operatorname {Tr}\left( ({\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}})^\top {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}})\right) .\) One may use the fact that trace is invariant under cyclic permutations, to apply the cyclic property, that is,

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi ({\textbf{G}}) =\operatorname {Tr}\left( {\textbf{Y}}^\top {\textbf{G}}^\top {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}\right) = \operatorname {Tr}\left( {\textbf{G}}^\top {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}{\textbf{Y}}^\top \right) = \operatorname {Tr}\left( {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}{\textbf{Y}}^\top {\textbf{G}}^\top \right) . \end{aligned}$$

                    (16)

Since the \(\operatorname {Tr}(\cdot )\) is a linear operator, hence it commutes with the derivative, i.e. \(\partial \operatorname {Tr}({\textbf{X}})= \operatorname {Tr}(\partial {\textbf{X}})\) for some matrix \({\textbf{X}}\). Moreover, \(\partial {\textbf{X}}^\top = (\partial {\textbf{X}})^\top \). Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \varphi ({\textbf{G}})}{\partial {\textbf{G}}} =\operatorname {Tr}\left( \frac{\partial }{\partial {\textbf{G}}} ( {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}{\textbf{Y}}^\top {\textbf{G}}^\top ) \right) =2\operatorname {Tr}\left( {\textbf{G}} {\textbf{Y}}{\textbf{Y}}^\top \right) =0. \end{aligned}$$

                    (17)

The the Lie algebra of \(\text {SL}_n\), namely \({\mathfrak {sl}}_n\), equals the space of n-square matrices with trace zero. Therefore \(\bar{{\textbf{G}}}{\textbf{Y}}\) is a critical point for \(\varphi \) if and only if \(\operatorname {Tr}({\textbf{G}}\bar{{\textbf{G}}}{\textbf{Y}} {\textbf{Y}}^\top \bar{{\textbf{G}}})=0\), \(\forall {\textbf{G}}\in {\mathfrak {sl}}_n\), if and only if \(\bar{{\textbf{G}}}{\textbf{Y}} {\textbf{Y}}^\top \bar{{\textbf{G}}}=\lambda {\textbf{I}}_n\) for some non-zero \(\lambda \in {\mathbb {N}}.\) Since \({\textbf{Y}}\) is non-zero, \(\lambda \ne 0\). One may write:

3 The metric tensor-based imputation problem

Let data tensor \(\mathcal X\in \mathbb {R}^{N_1\times N_2\times \cdots \times N_K}\) be given.

For any fixed mode, we assume that the following symmetric multi metric tensors are given:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal S^{(k)}\in S^2X_{N_k}\otimes V_k, \end{aligned}$$

                    (21)

such that they are coupled with an incomplete tensor \(\mathcal X\). Here, for \(k=1,\ldots , K\), \(S^2X_{N_k}\)’s denote the symmetric power of \(X_{N_k}\) and \(V_k\)’s are some arbitrary vector spaces over \({\mathbb {R}}\).

The symmetric tensors \(\mathcal S^{(k)}\), defines local metrics within the k-mode of the tensor. Any prior information about the elements of the k-mode of the tensor is included in the tensor \(\mathcal S^{(k)}\), which are non-negative definite. For each fixed \(k=1,\ldots , K\), the \(\ell\text{-th} \) mode matricization of tensor \(\mathcal S^{(k)}\) is a symmetric matrix denoted by \({\textbf{S}}^{(k)}_{(\ell )}\). The space of symmetric \(N_k\times N_k\) matrices can be identified with the space of symmetric tensors \(S^2X_{N_k}\subseteq X_{N_k}\otimes X_{N_k}\).

Let \(\text {GL}(\cdot )\) and \(\text {SL}(\cdot )\) denote the general linear group and special linear group, respectively. As shown in Sect. 2, \(\text {GL}(X_{N_k})\) acts on a symmetric matrix \({\textbf{B}}\) by the action defined as:

$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf{A}}\cdot {\textbf{B}}\rightarrow {\textbf{A}}{\textbf{B}}{\textbf{A}}^\top , \end{aligned}$$

for \({\textbf{A}}\in \text {GL}(X_{N_k}) \) and \({\textbf{B}} \in X_{N_k}\otimes X_{N_k}\). Suppose that the only known entries of \(\mathcal X\) are at positions

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega _{N_1\cdots N_K}=((i_{11},i_{21},\cdots , i_{N1}),(i_{12},i_{22},\cdots , i_{N2}),\dots ,(i_{1\,m},i_{2\,m},\cdots , i_{Nm})). \end{aligned}$$

This constraint can be written as \(\omega _{N_1\cdots N_K}(\mathcal Z)=\nu _{m}\) where \(\nu _m\in \mathbb {R}^m\) is some fixed vector, and

$$\begin{aligned} \omega _{N_1\cdots N_K}:{\mathcal {X}}\rightarrow \mathbb {R}^m, \end{aligned}$$

maps a tensor \(\mathcal B\in {\mathcal {X}}\) to the vector

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathcal B_{i_{11},i_{21},\ldots , i_{N1}},\mathcal B_{i_{12},i_{22},\ldots , i_{N2}},\dots ,\mathcal B_{i_{1\,m},i_{2\,m},\ldots , i_{Nm}})^\top \in \mathbb {R}^m. \end{aligned}$$

One may use the metric tensors \(\mathcal S^{(k)}\) as regularization of the tensor completion problem of tensor \(\mathcal X\).

3.1 The optimization problem

Let \(\mathcal X\in {\mathbb {R}}^{N_1\times \cdots \times N_K}\). For any fixed mode, say \(\ell \), \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\), there exists a multi-metric tensor denoted by \(\mathcal S^{(\ell )}\). The goal is to find the unknown tensor \(\mathcal Z\in \mathbb {R}^{N_1\times \cdots \times N_K}\), that matches the known entries of tensor \(\mathcal X\) and the unknown entries are predicted using the multi-metric tensor. The objective function to minimize is therefore:

$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} \min \limits _{\Gamma } \quad&\, F(\mathcal Z):=d_M(\mathcal X, \mathcal Z) +\sum \limits _{\ell =1}^K \varrho _\ell \, \sum \limits _{j=1}^{n_\ell }\, ({\textbf{D}}_\ell )_{j,j}\, \operatorname {Tr}\left( {\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )}\, {\textbf{S}}^{(\ell )}_{(j)}\, {\textbf{L}}^{{(\ell )^\top }}\right) , \\ \text {s.t.} \quad&\omega _{N_1\cdots N_K}(\mathcal Z)=\nu _{m}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$

                    (22)

where the distance function \(d_M\) refers to the tensor Mahalanobis distance given in Definition 2, with local metric tensors \({\textbf{L}}^{(k)}\in \text {SL}_{N_k},\) for \(k=1, \ldots , K\). The variable set is given by \(\Gamma =\{\mathcal Z,{\textbf{L}}^{(1)}, {\textbf{L}}^{(2)}, \ldots {\textbf{L}}^{(K)}\}\), and for some regularization parameters \(\varrho _\ell \). The penalty term \(\sum \limits _{\ell =1}^K J_\ell ({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )})\) may be added using penalty functions \(J_\ell (\cdot )\), for \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\) to impose any additional properties, such as orthogonality, sparsity, strict low rank condition and so on. Here, \({\textbf{S}}^{(\ell )}_{(j)}\) is the j-th mode matricization of metric tensor \(\mathcal S^{(\ell )}\). Moreover, the objective function given in Problem (22) also minimizes all the number of layers added to \(\mathcal S^{(\ell )}\), denoted as \(n_\ell \), for each layer. Additional transformations \({\textbf{D}}_\ell \) for \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\), which are diagonal matrices with unit determinant and positive entries on the diagonal, are also adopted. If entries of the metric tensors \(\mathcal S^{(\ell )}\) are partially observed, certain constraints are adopted in addition to the one which assumes that all the metrics are nonnegative definite.

The optimization algorithm follows an alternating minimization scheme. Local metrics, \({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )}\), \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\), is being optimized while other two are held fixed. As penalty functions for these distance matrices, one may impose the nuclear norm (also known as the trace norm), which provides a tight convex relaxation of matrix rank. The nuclear norm of a higher-order tensor \(\mathcal {X} \in \mathbb {F}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_D}\), as defined in [17], can be expressed in two equivalent ways. Its dual formulation is \( \Vert \mathcal {X}\Vert _*:= \max \left\{ \langle \mathcal {X}, \mathcal {Y} \rangle : \mathcal {Y} \in \mathbb {F}^{n_1 \times \cdots \times n_D}, \Vert \mathcal {Y}\Vert _2 = 1 \right\} ,\) where \(\Vert \cdot \Vert _2\) denotes the tensor spectral norm. Equivalently, the tensor nuclear norm can be defined as

$$ \Vert \mathcal {X}\Vert _*:= \inf \left\{ \sum _{r=1}^{R} |\lambda _r| \;:\; \mathcal {X} = \sum _{r=1}^{R} \lambda _r\, u_r^{(1)} \otimes \cdots \otimes u_r^{(D)}, \; \Vert u_r^{(d)}\Vert = 1 \right\} , $$

where \(u_r^{(d)} \in \mathbb {F}^{n_d}\) for \(d=1,\dots ,D\), and \(\otimes \) denotes the tensor product. This definition generalizes the matrix nuclear norm to higher-order tensors and provides a tight convex relaxation of the tensor rank. In analogy with the matrix case, where the nuclear norm is the convex envelope of matrix rank over the spectral norm unit ball, the tensor nuclear norm serves as a convex surrogate for tensor rank while avoiding dependence on mode-wise matricization. Minimizing the tensor nuclear norm promotes representations with a small number of rank-one components, yielding an effective and theoretically grounded framework for low-rank tensor recovery.

It is shown that for matrices and under certain conditions, rank minimization problem can be translated to solving the convex optimization problem of minimization of the trace norm over the given affine space [11, 12, 30]. One may generalize the definition of the trace norm for matrices to tensors as a convex combination of the trace norms of all matricization along each mode (also see [1, 24]).

4 Existence of the solution

In this section, we outline the conditions under which Problem (22) has in fact the solution and that it converges to a stationary point. For any proper, lower semi-continuous function \({\mathfrak {f}}: {\mathbb {E}} \rightarrow (-\infty , \infty ]\), we let \(\partial _L {\mathfrak {f}}: {\mathbb {E}} \rightarrow 2^{{\mathbb {E}}}\) denote the limiting subdifferential of \({\mathfrak {f}}\); see [32, Definition 8.3]. We consider the following minimization problem:

$$\begin{aligned} \min \limits _{\Gamma } \big \{F(\mathcal Z)\equiv f(\mathcal Z)+h(\mathcal Z)+g_1({\textbf{L}}^{(1)})+ g_2({\textbf{L}}^{(2)})+\cdots +g_K({\textbf{L}}^{(K)})\big \}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (23)

where: 

                (i).

The functions \({\mathfrak {f}}: {\mathbb {E}} \rightarrow (-\infty , \infty ]\), where \({\mathfrak {f}}=f,h,g_\ell \), \(\ell =1,\ldots , K\), are proper semi-continuous and subdifferentiable over their domain,

                (ii).

The function f is a continuously differentiable convex function over \(\text {Dom}(h) \times \text {Dom}(g_1) \times \text {Dom}(g_2) \times \cdots \times \text {Dom}(g_K)\).

We consider the following assumption:

Assumption A

Considering Problem (22), with penalty functions \(J(\cdot )\), 

                  (i)

\(J: \mathbb {R}^{N_{1} \times \cdots \times N_K } \rightarrow \left( -\infty , \infty \right] \) are proper and lower semi-continuous such that \(\inf _{\mathbb {R}^{N_{1} \times \cdots \times N_{K} }}J > -\infty \),

                  (ii)

\(F(\mathcal Z): \mathbb {R}^{N_{1} \times \cdots \times N_{K}} \rightarrow \mathbb {R}\) is differentiable and \(\inf _{\mathbb {R}^{N_{1} \times \cdots \times N_{K}}} F > -\infty \).

                  (iii)

The gradients \(\nabla F(\mathcal Z)\) is Lipschitz continuous with moduli \(L_F\), i.e., 

$$\begin{aligned} \Vert \nabla F(\mathcal Z^1) - \nabla F(\mathcal Z^2)\Vert _F^2 \le L_F\Vert \mathcal Z^1 - \mathcal Z^2 \Vert _F^2, \quad \text {for all} \quad \mathcal Z^{1}, \mathcal Z^{2}. \end{aligned}$$

For clarity, we note that the objective function \(F(\mathcal Z)\) in Problem (23) is composed of a smooth term and nonsmooth penalties:

$$\begin{aligned} F(\mathcal Z) = f(\mathcal Z) + h(\mathcal Z) + \sum _{\ell =1}^{K} g_\ell ({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )}), \end{aligned}$$

where \(f(\mathcal Z)\) is continuously differentiable and convex, capturing the primary fidelity or reconstruction component of the problem. The functions \(h(\mathcal Z)\) and \(g_\ell ({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )})\) are proper, lower semi-continuous, and possibly nonsmooth; they serve as regularizers on the tensor \(\mathcal Z\) or on individual block variables \({\textbf{L}}^{(\ell )}\).

This decomposition naturally enables block-wise optimization via alternating minimization: each subproblem minimizes a smooth function restricted to a block plus the corresponding nonsmooth penalty. As established under Assumption A and the uniqueness of block minimizers [8], any accumulation point of the iterates is a stationary point of the full objective.

Here, we adopt an alternating optimizer to solve the optimization problem. The ability to employ the alternating minimization method relies on the capability of computing minimizers with respect to each of the blocks. Here, we use the results of Bertsekas [8] stating that if the minimum with respect to each block of variables is unique, then any accumulation point of the sequence generated by the method is also a stationary point. In order to establish the convergence results for Problem (22), we first show that the problem is indeed well-defined, in the most general sense. For any \(\ell =1,2,\ldots , K\), we consider the Euclidean space \({\mathbb {E}}={\mathbb {E}}_1\times {\mathbb {E}}_2\times \cdots \times {\mathbb {E}}_K,\) and the set of linear transformation \(\{f_\ell \}_{\ell =1}^K\), where \(f_\ell : {\mathbb {E}}_\ell \rightarrow {\mathbb {E}}\), mapping all but \(\ell \)-th block to zero. We now have the following lemma:

Lemma 9

(Well-definedness) Suppose \(F:{\mathbb {E}}\rightarrow (-\infty , \infty ]\) is proper and closed. Further, assume that F has bounded level sets, i.e., for any \(\mu \in \mathbb {R}\), the set \( Level (F,\mu ):= \{\mathcal Z \in {\mathbb {E}}: F(\mathcal Z) \le \mu \}\) is bounded. Then: 

                  (i)

The function F attains its minimum, i.e., there exists \(\mathcal Z^* \in {\mathbb {E}}\) such that \(F(\mathcal Z^*) = \inf _{\mathcal Z \in {\mathbb {E}}} F(\mathcal Z)\).

                  (ii)

For any \(\bar{\mathcal Z}\in \text {Dom}(F)\) and each \(\ell =1,2,\dots ,K\), the problem 

$$\begin{aligned} \min _{{{\textbf{Y}}}_\ell \in {\mathbb {E}}_\ell } F\Big (\bar{\mathcal Z} + f_\ell ({{\textbf{Y}}}_\ell - \bar{{\textbf{Z}}}_\ell )\Big ) \end{aligned}$$

                    (24)

 possesses a minimizer.

Proof

(i): To show the existence of a minimizer of F, we let \({\widehat{\mathcal Z}} \in \text {Dom}(F)\) be arbitrary. Consider the level set

$$ \text {Level}\big (F, F({\widehat{\mathcal Z}})\big ) = \{\mathcal Z \in {\mathbb {E}}: F(\mathcal Z) \le F({\widehat{\mathcal Z}})\}. $$

By assumption, this set is bounded. Since F is closed, the level set is also closed. In a finite-dimensional space, any set that is both closed and bounded is compact by the Heine–Borel theorem. Therefore, \(\text {Level}(F, F({\widehat{\mathcal Z}}))\) is compact. By the Weierstrass extreme value theorem, a continuous function attains its minimum over a compact set, which implies that

$$ \min _{\mathcal Z \in \text {Level}(F, F({\widehat{\mathcal Z}}))} F(\mathcal Z) $$

exists. Since any global minimizer must lie in some level set, it follows that F has at least one minimizer over \({\mathbb {E}}\).

(ii) To show the existence of a minimizer for (24)), we deine

$$ G_\ell ({{\textbf{Y}}}_\ell ):= F\Big (\bar{\mathcal Z} + f_\ell ({{\textbf{Y}}}_\ell - \bar{{\textbf{Z}}}_\ell )\Big ). $$

Since F is proper and closed, and \(f_\ell \) is linear, \(G_\ell \) is also proper and closed. Moreover, the level sets of \(G_\ell \) are bounded because they are affine images of the bounded level sets of F. By the same argument as above, any closed and bounded level set in finite dimensions is compact. Therefore, the minimum of \(G_\ell \) over \({{\textbf{Y}}}_\ell \in {\mathbb {E}}_\ell \) exists by the Weierstrass theorem, proving that a minimizer for (24) exists for each mode \(\ell \). \(\square \)

Theorem 10

(Convergence) Suppose Assumption A holds. Then, the sequence

$$ \Gamma _k = (\mathcal Z_k, {\textbf{L}}^{(1)}_k, {\textbf{L}}^{(2)}_k, \ldots , {\textbf{L}}^{(K)}_k) $$

generated by the alternating minimization algorithm from any starting point converges to a stationary point of Problem (22).

Proof

We organize the proof in several steps:

(i) Well-definedness and boundedness: By Lemma 9, Problem (22) is well-defined, and each block minimization subproblem possesses at least one minimizer. Further, under Assumption A, the level sets of F are bounded. Hence, the iterates \(\{\Gamma _k\}\) remain in a compact set.

(ii) Non-increasing sequence of objective values: At iteration k, the alternating minimization updates one block at a time by minimizing F with respect to that block while keeping other blocks fixed. Therefore, for each block update,

$$ F(\Gamma _{k+1}) \le F(\Gamma _k), $$

and the sequence of objective values \(\{F(\Gamma _k)\}\) is non-increasing and bounded below. This ensures convergence of the objective values.

(iii) Existence of accumulation points: Since \(\{\Gamma _k\}\) lies in a compact set (bounded level sets) and the objective is non-increasing, there exists at least one accumulation point of the sequence.

(iv) Stationarity of accumulation points: Assume that each block minimization has a unique minimizer, as required by Proposition 2.7.1 in [8] (see also Theorem [14.3] in [6]).Then, any accumulation point \(\Gamma ^*\) satisfies the optimality condition for each block:

$$ 0 \in \partial _L F|_{\text {block } \ell }(\Gamma ^*), \quad \forall \ell = 1,\dots ,K, $$

where \(\partial _L\) denotes the limiting subdifferential [32, Definition 8.3]. Hence, \(\Gamma ^*\) is a stationary point of the full objective \(F(\mathcal Z)\).

Combining Steps (i)–(iv), one may conclude that the sequence generated by alternating minimization converges to a stationary point of Problem (22). \(\square \)

The sequence \(\{\Gamma _k\}\) generated by alternating minimization produces non-increasing objective values and remains in a bounded set. As established in Theorem 10, any accumulation point of this sequence is a stationary point of \(F(\mathcal Z)\).

Lastly, it is noteworthy that the only result available on the rate of convergence of the alternating method under general convexity assumptions (and not strong convexity) is the result in [7] showing a sublinear rate of convergence, and that the multiplicative constant depends on the minimum of the block Lipschitz constants. However, this result is limited in the sense that it only holds for unconstrained problems with a smooth objective function, therefore does not apply to our proposed constrained problem.

5 Experimental results

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we employ the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS) dataset [33], a large-scale perturbational genomics resource which provides large-scale gene expression profiles of multiple human cell lines exposed to diverse chemical, genetic, and pharmacological perturbations.Footnote 1 The LINCS project was designed to systematically characterize cellular responses to external perturbations and to uncover functional relationships among drugs, genes, and diseases. Owing to its large scale and diversity of perturbational conditions, the dataset is particularly suitable for studying drug–target–disease interactions and evaluating tensor-based machine learning frameworks.

Specifically, the target tensor in this work is constructed using Level 5 data from the Broad Institute LINCS Phase 1 L1000 collection [15]. The L1000 platform directly measures the expression levels of 978 carefully selected “landmark” genes and computationally infers the remaining transcriptome, thereby enabling high-throughput gene expression profiling at reduced experimental cost. Level 5 data correspond to consensus gene expression signatures generated by aggregating multiple experimental replicates using a moderated z-score procedure, which improves robustness and reduces experimental noise. Each signature captures the differential transcriptional response of a specific cell line under a given perturbation condition, including information such as compound identity, dosage, and treatment duration.

In our framework, these processed gene expression signatures are organized into a multidimensional tensor representation that preserves the intrinsic multi-way relationships among drugs, genes, cell lines, and disease-related entities. This tensorized representation provides a natural setting for tensor completion, representation learning, and downstream predictive analysis.

To generate the similarity tensors \(\mathcal {S}^{(i)}\) for each mode, as defined in Eq. (21), the drug similarity tensor is constructed by integrating two drug-related similarity matrices from the previous study [4], complemented by an additional similarity layer obtained from ChemicalChecker [16]. The correspondence between LINCS compounds and their DrugBank [38] counterparts is established through a curated mapping dictionary to ensure consistent indexing across datasets. The overall construction procedure follows the methodology introduced in [4].

The gene similarity tensor is constructed using the intersection of genes shared across all available datasets. In total, five distinct sources contribute to this tensor: two similarity matrices adopted from prior work, one matrix derived from the NTD-DR dataset [21], one from STRING [34], and one from RegNetwork [25].

Finally, the cell line similarity tensor is derived from the attribute-based similarity matrix provided in the LINCS Kinativ Kinase Inhibitor Bioactivity Profiles dataset.Footnote 2 The resulting tensor has dimensions \(6780 \times 70 \times 1104\), corresponding respectively to drugs, cell lines, and genes. More formally, let \(\mathcal {X} \in \mathbb {R}^{6780 \times 70 \times 1104}\) denote the target tensor, where each entry \(X_{i,j,k}\) represents the expression level of gene k measured in cell line j after treatment with drug i. Each tensor entry represents the expression level of a particular gene in a specific cell line following treatment with a given drug.

To evaluate the proposed framework, we compare its performance against representative methods from the two principal categories of tensor completion approaches: (i) methods that do not leverage auxiliary or side information, and (ii) methods that incorporate such information when available, but are inherently constrained either by the limited number of modalities they can couple or by their ability to utilize only a single side-information matrix per mode.

In particular, we select HaLRTC [24] as a representative convex optimization–based low-rank tensor completion method without side information, and CTRC [20] as a representative decomposition-based coupled tensor completion approach that can incorporate auxiliary information through latent factorization.

CTRC [20]: Coupled Tensor Ring Completion represents a tensor ring decomposition–based model designed for coupled tensor completion. The method employs a block coordinate descent optimization scheme to iteratively refine the factor matrices and achieve convergence to a locally optimal solution.

HaLRTC [24]: High-Accuracy Low-Rank Tensor Completion formulates the completion problem as a convex optimization task under a tensor-specific trace norm regularization. The trace norm serves as a tight convex surrogate for the tensor rank, thereby enabling the recovery of low-rank tensor structures from incomplete observations with theoretical guarantees of optimality and stability (Table 1).

5.1 Ablation study on mode-specific metric tensors

We conducted an ablation study to evaluate the contribution of each mode-specific metric tensor to the proposed tensor metric learning (TML) framework. Each mode \(\ell \in \{1,2,3\}\) is associated with a metric tensor parameterized by a regularization weight \(\varrho _\ell \), as defiend in Problem 22, which controls the influence of learned mode-wise geometry relative to the Euclidean baseline. Setting \(\varrho _\ell = 0\) effectively disables learning in that mode, forcing the model to revert to a standard Euclidean distance in the corresponding latent subspace. By selectively activating or deactivating these parameters, we can isolate the contribution of each mode’s learned geometry to the overall tensor reconstruction performance.

Specifically, we evaluated four configurations: with all \(\varrho _i=0\), corresponding to conventional tensor completion without metric learning and with enabaling one more at a time. For each setting, we report the Relative Standard Error (RSE) and Explained Variance under different missing rates.

As summarized in Table 2, when all \(\varrho _i=0\), the model reduces to standard tensor completion under the Euclidean metric, producing relatively high reconstruction errors (RSE of 0.2256 and 0.4190 for \(10\%\) and \(20\%\) missing rates, respectively). Introducing a learned metric in the first mode (\(\varrho _1 \ne 0\)) already yields a significant improvement, reducing the RSE by more than half. This confirms that even a single mode’s metric adaptation enhances the model’s ability to capture meaningful pairwise relationships—such as correlations among drugs, targets, or experimental conditions—within that specific mode.

Enabling additional metrics for the second and third modes leads to further consistent improvements. When both \(\varrho _1\) and \(\varrho _2\) are active, the RSE decreases to 0.0680 for \(10\%\) missing data and 0.1080 for \(20\%\), with explained variance exceeding 0.98. Finally, when all three metrics are learned jointly, the model achieves nearly perfect reconstruction (RSE \(\approx 0.008\) and 0.0113, explained variance \(> 0.9999\)), indicating that the three mode-specific metrics complement one another and jointly capture the underlying manifold geometry of the tensor data.

In the context of the LINCS dataset, each tensor mode corresponds to a distinct data dimension—typically drugs (or perturbagens), cell lines, and gene expression signatures. Enabling or disabling a mode in our framework effectively determines whether side information associated with that mode (e.g., drug similarity, cell line ontology, or gene co-expression) contributes to the learned metric tensor.

When a mode is enabled (i.e., \(\varrho _\ell \ne 0\)), the algorithm incorporates its corresponding similarity structure to guide the tensor completion process, allowing the model to better capture biologically meaningful relationships. Conversely, when a mode is disabled (\(\varrho _\ell = 0\)), the completion relies solely on the Euclidean distance, ignoring the rich contextual information from that mode. As shown in Table 2, enabling additional modes leads to progressive improvements in reconstruction accuracy, highlighting the complementary value of multi-mode side information in modeling complex biological interactions within the LINCS dataset.

These results demonstrate the essential role of mode-wise metric learning in improving tensor completion accuracy. Each learned metric tensor contributes distinct structural information: for instance, the first mode may encode sample-level relationships, the second mode captures feature correlations, and the third mode reflects temporal or contextual dependencies. By integrating them in a unified geometric framework, the proposed TML model effectively adapts the distance computation to the intrinsic structure of the data, yielding substantial gains over the Euclidean baseline.

6 Conclusion

In this manuscript, we introduced a simultaneous multi-metric learning framework and demonstrated its application in tensor analysis. The performance of the proposed method has been compared against alternative approaches. Other related tensor methods have been previously developed using metric learning approaches [4, 5]. The group action defined in our framework, grounded in the Kempf–Ness theorem and results from group representation theory, provides a theoretical foundation for identifying critical points that minimize the objective function. Our experimental results, including evaluations on the LINCS dataset and an ablation study, further illustrate how incorporating multiple local distance metrics enhances performance and robustness across high-dimensional, multimodal data. As future work, one may consider developing a theoretical framework to use metric learning approaches to better approximate the rank of a tensor, as a continuation of the work in [2].
