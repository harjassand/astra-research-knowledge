T17 — Signed rough-number correlation and the pointwise short-interval barrier
Frozen record: v1 (append-only)
Status: internally derived exact identities; finite-tested; target theorem unresolved.

1. Target and honest scope

The ambitious target is an unconditional pointwise short-interval prime number theorem: for every fixed epsilon>0, uniformly for x sufficiently large and x^(1/2+epsilon) <= H <= x^0.99,

    pi(x+H)-pi(x) = (1+o_epsilon(1)) H/log x.

This would improve the pointwise exponent substantially and approach the square-root scale. It is a target, not a theorem established here. The task asked for a signed arithmetic correlation that keeps the prime-factor parity information that a nonnegative sieve discards. I derive two exact representations and test the classical composite-heavy counterexample against them. The representations identify the missing correlation but do not prove a new prime-distribution theorem.

The target concerns intervals in the integers and has no progression modulus. There are therefore no exceptional Dirichlet characters in the statement or proof attempt. Any extension to progressions would need to state its modulus range and isolate a possible exceptional real character; nothing here gives that extension.

2. Primary frontier check as of 2026-10-09

The published Baker–Harman–Pintz theorem says every sufficiently large x has a prime in [x,x+x^0.525]. The primary journal page is Baker, Harman, Pintz, “The Difference Between Consecutive Primes, II,” Proc. London Math. Soc. 83 (2001), 532–562, doi:10.1112/plms/83.3.532.

Guth–Maynard, “New large value estimates for Dirichlet polynomials,” Annals of Mathematics 203 (2026), 623–675, proves a pointwise asymptotic for pi(x+y)-pi(x) when x^(17/30+epsilon) <= y <= x^0.99. Their Corollary 1.3 gives y/log x plus O_epsilon(y exp(-(log x)^(1/4))). Corollary 1.4 gives the same asymptotic for all but O(X exp(-(log X)^(1/4))) starting points x in [X,2X], for y >= X^(2/15+epsilon). The primary source is https://annals.math.princeton.edu/2026/203-2/p06 and the authors’ arXiv v2 is https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.20552v2, last revised 2026-04-07. This is the strongest verified pointwise asymptotic found in the checked primary sources; the almost-all exponent is a different quantifier.

Runbo Li’s arXiv v8, “The number of primes in short intervals and numerical calculations for Harman’s sieve,” https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04458v8, last revised 2025-10-16, states that [x-x^0.52,x] contains a prime for all sufficiently large x and gives nontrivial lower and upper constants for lengths x^(theta+epsilon), 0.52 <= theta <= 0.525. The arXiv record has no journal reference. I treat the 0.52 claim as a preprint claim, not as independently established literature. Its proof uses a detailed Harman-sieve decomposition, Type I/II arithmetic estimates and extensive numerical integral evaluation (paper §§1–6, especially Theorems 1–2); the arXiv record links C++ ancillary files. The accepted BHP exponent remains the conservative published baseline.

The parity literature already supplies both a clean obstruction and successful extra-correlation mechanisms. Selberg’s sequence a_n=1+lambda(n) is explicitly identified by Ford–Maynard as a sequence with a_p=0 that satisfies arbitrarily long Type I information. Ford–Maynard, “On the theory of prime producing sieves,” arXiv:2407.14368v1, §§1–2 and 9–10, gives a general Type I/Type II framework. Their Theorem 2.1 says that for each gamma<1 some positive Type II width is still too small: bounded nonnegative a_n can obey both stated Type I and Type II estimates and vanish on every prime. Their Theorem 2.2 gives a necessary-and-sufficient combinatorial criterion for an asymptotic to follow from their particular Type I/II hypotheses. This is close prior art to any generic “bilinear correlation breaks parity” claim; that claim would be a rediscovery unless it supplies a materially new correlation or an application outside their scope.

Friedlander–Iwaniec’s “Asymptotic sieve for primes,” Annals 148 (1998), 1041–1065, arXiv:math/9811186, adds a bilinear axiom to classical sieve data and explains how it can break parity. Their companion “The polynomial X^2+Y^4 captures its primes,” Annals 148 (1998), 945–1040, arXiv:math/9811185, verifies the relevant inputs and proves an asymptotic for primes a^2+b^4. This is a genuine successful parity-sensitive method in a specially structured sequence, not a general pointwise short-interval theorem.

Matomäki–Merikoski–Teräväinen, “Primes in arithmetic progressions and short intervals without L-functions,” arXiv:2401.17570v1, Theorem 1.2, proves at least (1/40)x^(39/40)/log x primes in (x-x^(39/40),x] for sufficiently large x by a sieve detecting primes in multiplicatively structured sets. This is a separate parity-breaking/sieve route, but the interval is much longer than current pointwise records.

3. First obstruction: a nonnegative local sieve model can be composite-only

Let Omega(n) count prime factors with multiplicity and let lambda(n)=(-1)^Omega(n). It is completely multiplicative. Define a_n=1+lambda(n). Then a_n is 0 or 2 for n>=2, and a_p=0 for every prime p. Its positive support consists entirely of composites.

For every positive integer d and X>=1, complete multiplicativity gives the exact formula

    sum_{n<=X, d|n} a_n
      = floor(X/d) + lambda(d) L(floor(X/d)),
    L(y) := sum_{m<=y} lambda(m).

Thus, for every fixed d, the prime number theorem for lambda, L(y)=o(y), gives the same main term X/d as the uniform sequence a_n=1. More strongly, the classical zero-free-region estimate L(y) << y exp(-c sqrt(log y)) implies that for each fixed delta>0 and D<=X^(1-delta),

    sum_{d<=D} |sum_{n<=X,d|n}a_n - floor(X/d)|
       << X log X exp(-c sqrt(delta log X))
       = o_A(X/log^A X)

for every fixed A>0. This is the standard Selberg parity model: even very strong congruence/divisibility information alone is compatible with no prime support. The input about L(y) is an analytic theorem, not a finite computation. The code below checks the exact finite formula only.

This falsifies the tempting inequality “small divisor-distribution error forces positive prime mass” before any attempt to use it. It also warns against assuming a generic signed cancellation estimate after rough-number restriction: that restriction is precisely where the prime signal lives.

4. Exact parity detector on rough numbers

Fix X and z>(2X)^(1/3). For X<n<=2X, define roughness by P^-(n)>z, where P^-(n) is the least prime factor. If n is rough, it has at most two prime factors with multiplicity: three factors would give n>z^3>2X. Thus the rough integers in this dyadic block are either primes or semiprimes, including squares.

Since lambda(p)=-1 and lambda(pq)=+1, including p=q, the exact identity is

    1_{n prime} = 1_{P^-(n)>z} (1-lambda(n))/2,   X<n<=2X.

For any interval I contained in (X,2X], put

    R_0(I;z) = sum_{n in I} 1_{P^-(n)>z},
    R_1(I;z) = sum_{n in I} 1_{P^-(n)>z} lambda(n).

Then

    pi(I) = (R_0(I;z)-R_1(I;z))/2.

If S_2(I;z) counts semiprimes pq in I with z<p<=q, including squares, then separately

    R_0(I;z) = pi(I)+S_2(I;z),
    R_1(I;z) = -pi(I)+S_2(I;z).

This exposes the lost bit exactly: the unsigned rough count combines primes and semiprimes, while the Liouville sign separates them. The formula alone is not a prime theorem; estimating R_1 to the prime main-term scale is as hard as the missing parity information.

For Re(s)>1, the two finite-cutoff sequences also have distinct Euler products:

    sum_{P^-(n)>z} n^(-s)
      = zeta(s) product_{p<=z}(1-p^(-s)),
    sum_{P^-(n)>z} lambda(n)n^(-s)
      = zeta(2s)/zeta(s) product_{p<=z}(1+p^(-s)).

The second product is a multiplicative Fourier coefficient for the nontrivial character of Omega(n) mod 2. It is a genuinely different representation from the divisor-class sieve data in Section 3, but the character itself is classical. No new analytic estimate follows merely from writing this Euler product.

5. Exact bilinear route and the prime-power range

The von Mangoldt function obeys the exact Dirichlet-convolution identity

    Lambda = mu * log,
    Lambda(n) = sum_{d|n} mu(d) log(n/d).

For any weight c_n and interval I this gives

    sum_{n in I} c_n Lambda(n)
       = sum_{dm in I} mu(d)c_{dm}log(m).

This signed bilinear form is absent from the nonnegative local sieve model. A fully explicit Vaughan decomposition, with mu_{<=U}(d)=mu(d)1_{d<=U}, mu_{>U}=mu-mu_{<=U}, Lambda_{<=V}(n)=Lambda(n)1_{n<=V}, and Lambda_{>V}=Lambda-Lambda_{<=V}, is

    Lambda = Lambda_{<=V}
           + mu_{<=U}*log
           - (mu_{<=U}*1)*Lambda_{<=V}
           + (mu_{>U}*1)*Lambda_{>V}.

The final term contains a Type II bilinear form after dyadic grouping. The decomposition is exact but does not estimate that form. In a short interval, the interval constraint dm in I couples the two variables; replacing it by an independent rectangular sum incurs boundary errors that must be quantified at the chosen U,V and H. A bound adequate on [X,2X] is not automatically uniform for each I.

Prime powers also need explicit treatment. Exactly,

    sum_{n in I} Lambda(n)/log(n)
      = pi(I) + sum_{k>=2, p^k in I} 1/k.

For I contained in (X,2X], the second term is O(sqrt(X) log X), by counting all prime powers <=2X. Hence it is o(H/log X) when H>=X^(1/2+epsilon). This is why the selected target has a strict fixed epsilon above 1/2. The error does not justify a claim at H=sqrt(X) times arbitrary subpolynomial factors.

6. What a short-interval correlation estimate would have to say

Choose a fixed alpha with 1/3<alpha<1/2 and set z=X^alpha. The same exact parity detector applies throughout (X,2X]. On a long interval such as (X,2X], the prime number theorem and the standard semiprime convolution give the coefficients

    R_0(I;z) = (1 + log((1-alpha)/alpha) + o(1)) H/log X,
    R_1(I;z) = (-1 + log((1-alpha)/alpha) + o(1)) H/log X.

The semiprime coefficient follows by decomposing pq with X^alpha<p<=sqrt(2X) and evaluating the standard integral

    integral_alpha^(1/2) dt/(t(1-t)) = log((1-alpha)/alpha).

For short intervals, these same coefficients are a natural prediction only if the semiprime count is also uniform at that scale. A PNT for primes in I alone does not supply that local semiprime asymptotic: its convolution samples prime intervals of length H/p, which can be much shorter than the original H. Thus this coefficient calculation is a diagnostic under an additional semiprime-uniformity premise, not a consequence of the target theorem. If matching short-interval estimates for R_0 and R_1 were proved, subtracting them would give coefficient 2 and then pi(I)~H/log X. If the signed rough sum is only bounded by O(H/log X) with an unspecified constant, it need not distinguish zero prime mass from the correct prime main term. A useful new principle would need a uniform signed estimate with the exact main coefficient and error o(H/log X), together with the needed rough-count estimate. No such estimate is derived here.

The lower cutoff for ordinary inclusion-exclusion from interval remainder terms is too small to guarantee at most two prime factors: elementary interval counting only controls divisor moduli to a scale tied to H, whereas z>x^(1/3) is needed for the exact parity truncation. This is the concrete obstruction in the attempted transfer. Harman-type methods obtain more arithmetic information by Type I/II estimates and Buchstab switching, as in Li’s preprint, but the route below reproduces no improvement over it.

7. Finite experimental evidence

Run `python3 work/theory/t17_prime_parity/parity_identities.py`. It uses only the standard library and writes no files. The saved output is `finite_checks.json`.

At N=100,000 it checks 17,984 primes through 200,000 and finds a_p=0 for all of them under a_n=1+lambda(n). For ten sample divisors it compares the exact weighted count on multiples of d with floor(N/d); the observed relative errors range from about 0.18% to 4.62%. These finite errors are not an asymptotic proof of Type I distribution.

For z=59, z^3>200,000. On (100000,200000], the script obtains 13,450 rough integers and signed rough sum -3,334; their half-difference is exactly 8,392, the directly counted primes. It repeats the exact check on two shorter subintervals. It also verifies Lambda=mu*log through 5,000 and the stated Vaughan identity through 2,000 for U=17,V=23, with floating-point residuals below 4e-15. These are finite identity checks only; no cancellation estimate or asymptotic claim is tested.

8. Exact gap and next mathematical gates

The main gap is a uniform estimate for the signed rough sum R_1(I;X^alpha), with its main term at scale H/log X, in every interval I of length H>=X^(1/2+epsilon). Establishing that estimate appears to contain the desired prime theorem itself. The alternate mu-log/Vaughan route moves the same gap into Type II bilinear cancellation with a short-interval product constraint. I have not proved either estimate, and the finite checks do not support it.

The objective of inventing a new correlation principle that crosses the obstruction and proves a new consequential prime theorem is therefore NOT ACHIEVED. The outputs are candidate exact identities, a counterexample that invalidates local-sieve-only inequalities, and finite validation of the identities. They are not a prime-distribution breakthrough or a novelty claim.

If the investigation continues, the next decisive gates are: (i) compare any proposed short-interval signed bound term-by-term with Ford–Maynard’s Type II ranges and Selberg-like fake sequences; (ii) prove a semiprime bound with constants at the H/log X scale and uniformity in the starting point; (iii) preserve the interval constraint in dyadic bilinear decomposition, recording the boundary error relative to H/log X; and (iv) only then derive a pointwise prime conclusion. A progression variant additionally needs uniformity in q, treatment of a possible exceptional real character, and explicit dependence of the remainder on q. None of these are completed here.
