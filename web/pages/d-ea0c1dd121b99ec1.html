1. Introduction and background
In this paper, we present various tree formulas of a ﬁnite Markov chain, and make con-
nections with random spanning trees and mean ﬁrst passage times in the Markov chain.
Most results are known from previous work, but a few formulas and statements, e.g. the
combinatorial interpretation of Kemeny’s constant in Corollary 1.4, appear here for the ﬁrst
time. We oﬀer a probabilistic and combinatorial approach to these results, encompassing the
closely related results of Leighton and Rivest [61, 60] as well as Wilson’s algorithm [100, 85]
for generation of random spanning trees.

Section 3 recalls the short combinatorial proof of this result due to Ventcel and Freidlin

It was also formulated by Shubert [89] and Solberg [90] in the language of graph theory,

name Markov chain tree theorem was ﬁrst coined by Leighton and Rivest [61, 60], where

Later Anantharam and Tsoucas [4], Aldous [3] and Broder [15] provided probabilistic
arguments by lifting the Markov chain to its spanning tree counterpart. A method to generate
random spanning trees, the Aldous-Broder algorithm, was devised as a by-product of their
proofs: see Lyons and Peres [69, Section 4.4]. See also Madry et al. [49, 70] for development on
fast algorithms to generate random spanning trees. Recently, Biane et al. [9, 10] studied the
factorization of a polynomial associated to that spanning tree-valued Markov chain. Gursoy
et al. [38] extended the Markov chain tree theorem in the max algebra setting.

The purpose of this work is to provide combinatorial and probabilistic meanings of these
tree formulas, without appeal to linear algebra. The formulas (1.7)-(1.9) can be proved by
purely combinatorial arguments. As an example, a combinatorial proof of the harmonic
tree formula (1.9) is given in Section 2. In addition, the Green tree formula (1.7) and the
harmonic tree formula (1.9) are closely related to Wilson’s algorithm, whose original proof
[100] is combinatorial. In fact,
• the Green tree formula (1.7) is derived from the harmonic tree formula (1.9), together
with standard theory of Markov chains;
• the harmonic tree formula (1.9) is a consequence of the success of Wilson’s algorithm;
• Wilson’s algorithm follows from the Green tree formula (1.7) by a probabilistic argu-
ment due to Lawler [58].
These arguments are presented in Sections 2 and 5. We show in Subsection 4.2 that even the
formula (1.4) can be deduced from the Markov chain tree theorem.

algorithm for sampling cycle-rooted spanning forests, which will be discussed later.
For any ﬁnite path (x0,x 1,...,x l) in a directed graph, its loop erasure (u0,u 1,...,u m) is

6. Loose ends and further references
6.1. Spanning trees and other models. Spanning trees are closely related to various
mathematical models. The connection between uniform spanning trees and loop-erased ran-
dom walks has been discussed in Section 5. Here we list two more examples.
• Temperley [93, 94] established a bijection between spanning trees of a square grid
and perfect matchings/dimer coverings of a related square grid. This bijection was
extended to general planar graphs by Burton and Pemantle [17], and Kenyon et al.
[53]. As an analog of Kirchhoﬀ’s matrix tree theorem for enumerating spanning
trees, Kasteleyn-Temperley-Fisher’s theory [47, 95] expresses the number of dimer
conﬁgurations in a graph in terms of the determinant of the Kasteleyn matrix . We
refer to Wu [101] and Kenyon [51, Section 3] for further development.
• Dhar et al. [30, 71] constructed a bijective map between spanning trees of a graph
and recurrent sandpiles on that graph. This map is not unique, and an alternative
bijection was provided by Cori and Le Borgne [27]. See J´ arai [42] for development
on sandpile models. Recently, Kassel and Wilson [46] has developed a new approach
to computing sandpile densities of planar graphs by using a two-component forest
formula of Liu and Chow [64].
