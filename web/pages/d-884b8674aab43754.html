Abstract
The Approximate polynomial satisfiability problem (APS), introduced by Guo, Saxena, and
Sinhababu (CCC 2018), asks whether the zero vector lies in the Zariski closure of the image
of a given polynomial map. Specifically, for a field k with algebraic closure K, the problem
asks whether0∈ f(Kn) for a polynomial mapf= (f 1, . . . , fm) withf i∈k[X 1, . . . , Xn].
APS is a natural topological analogue of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, namely the question of
whether a given system of polynomial equations has a common zero. APS captures several
problems in algebraic complexity, including border rank, hitting sets for border classes, and
null-cone membership; it is known to beNP-hard and inPSPACE.
We show that APS lies in the Counting Hierarchy ( CH) over both the rationals and finite
fields, substantially improving the known PSPACE upper bound. Our proof builds on a
recent breakthrough due to Andrews, Garg, and Schost (FOCS 2026) on deciding Hilbert’s
Nullstellensatz in CH. As a corollary, our result improves the complexity of certifying hitting
sets for border classes from PSPACE to CH. Such hitting sets play a role in explicit construc-
tions and Noether-normalization problems arising in Geometric Complexity Theory, where a
longstanding difficulty has been that algorithmic constructions for VP can be substantially
harder than the analogous problem forVP.
We also give a polynomial-time reduction of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz to APS, valid in any
characteristic. In characteristic zero, we give a reduction of APS to the decision problem for
the existential theory of real closed fields. Overall, our results place approximate polynomial
satisfiability closer in complexity to exact polynomial feasibility and as a byproduct give
improved complexity bounds for several problems arising in approximative complexity.

1 Introduction
Solving systems of polynomial equations is one of the basic algorithmic problems in algebra
and has connections to a remarkably broad range of computational questions. The problem of
determining whether a given collection of polynomials with coefficients in k has a common zero
in K = k, the algebraic closure of k, is called Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, denoted HNk. Besides
its intrinsic importance in computational algebraic geometry, HNk serves as a tool to address
manyalgebraic existenceproblems. A striking example is tensor rank: deciding tensor rank over
a field k has the same computational complexity as HNk; in fact, tensor-rank computation and
polynomial-system solvability over arbitrary integral domains are known to be polynomial-time
equivalent [ SˇS18, Shi16]. Similarly, fixing the graph of an arithmetic circuit and varying its
scalar labels gives a parameterisation of the polynomials computed by that circuit [ Raz08]. This
readily transforms questions about whether thereexistsa computation of a prescribed form into
solving a system of polynomial equations in the circuit parameters which is useful in proving the
existence of hard polynomial families [ CKW11, SY10] as well as designing blackbox identity testing
algorithms [Agr05, HS80].
Approximative ComplexityIn this work, we are concerned with approximative or border
complexity. In many classical questions in algebraic complexity, one must pass from objects
admitting an exact representation of bounded complexity to limits of such objects. One of the
earliest and most influential examples illustrating the power of approximative complexity comes
from matrix multiplication. Bini, Capovani, Lotti, and Romani [ BCRL79, BLR80] showed that
allowing arbitrarily small errors can reduce the complexity of bilinear algorithms, and used this to
obtain faster matrix multiplication algorithms. This led to the systematic study ofborder rank: a
tensor has border rank at most r if it belongs to the closure of the set of tensors of rank at most r.
Lickteig’s work in the 1980s developed the algebraic geometry of these closures and used it to
prove lower bounds on border rank [ Lic84, Lic85]. Studying closure of polynomial maps has been a
central theme in algebraic complexity: they occur in approximative circuit complexity, in the factor
theorem of B¨ urgisser [B¨ ur04], and especially in Geometric Complexity Theory, where Mulmuley
and Sohoni [ MS01, MS08] developed a framework to prove algebraic circuit lower bounds via
non-containment of orbit closures. For instance, the determinant-versus-permanent [Agr07] problem
can be phrased as the problem of separating the orbit closure of the determinant polynomial from
the orbit closure of an appropriately padded permanent polynomial. This reformulation makes the
problem amenable to tools from algebraic geometry, invariant theory, and representation theory, and
has generated a substantial body of work on the geometry of algebraic computation [ Lan17, BI25].
This distinction between exact polynomial maps and their closures leads directly to the problem
studied in this paper. Let k be either Q or a finite field Fq, and let K =k be an algebraic closure
of k. The approximate polynomial satisfiability problem APSk asks whether0 ∈ f(Kn) for a
polynomial map f = (f1,...,f m) with fi∈k [X1,...,X n]. The latter condition is a weakening
of the requirement0 ∈f(K n), i.e., that f1,...,f m have a common zero. It is known that APSk
lies in PSPACE [GSS19], while a celebrated result of Koiran [ Koi96] shows that HNQ lies in the
polynomial hierarchy, under the generalised Riemann hypothesis. Recently it was shown that HNk
lies in the counting hierarchy unconditionally when k is either the rationals or a finite field [ AGS26].
Our main result shows that APSk lies in the counting hierarchy. The proof naturally divides into
two steps. In the first step we give a randomised polynomial-time reduction of APSk to a decision
problem (called the multivariate resultant valuation MRVk problem) concerning the resultant of

of polynomials f = (f1,...,f m)∈k [X1,...,X n]m is said to be algebraically dependent if there
exists a nonzero polynomial A(Y1,...,Y m)∈k [Y1,...,Y m] such that A(f1,...,f m) = 0. We call A

The computational complexity of checking whether a given set of polynomials are algebraically
dependent crucially depends on the characteristic of the field where the coefficients come from.
Over fields of characteristic zero, and more generally in sufficiently large characteristic, the Jacobian
criterion reduces algebraic independence testing to polynomial identity testing, giving a randomised
polynomial-time algorithm [ BMS13]. In small positive characteristic the Jacobian criterion fails
because ofinseparability. Guo, Saxena, and Sinhababu showed that over finite fields algebraic
independence testing lies in AM∩coAM , substantially improving the previously known upper
bound [GSS19].
Kayal [Kay09] investigated the complexity of computing the annhilating polynomial and showed
that is NP-hard to test if the constant term of the annhilating polynomial is zero and #P-hard to
evaluate the constant term of the annhilator modulo a given prime p. Both these results strongly
suggest that the annhilating polynomial is unlikely to be computed by small algebraic circuits. Guo,
Saxena, and Sinhababu further showed that0 ∈f(Kn) if and only if every annihilator off vanishes
at the origin: A(0) = 0 for every A∈I f. Thus algebraic independence testing asks whether there
are any algebraic relations among f1,...,f m, whereas APS asks whether all such relations vanish
at0. We strengthen Kayal’s result to show that this problem is NPk-hard by showing a reduction
from HNk.
1.1 Applications of APS
Besides being a natural analogue of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, APS is versatile in understanding
approximative complexity. Here we survey a few such applications.
Hitting sets and Noether normalisation.Let C be a family of n-variate polynomials. A
finite set H⊆k n is ahitting setfor C if every nonzero f∈ C is nonzero at some point of
H. Constructing explicit hitting sets is a long-standing open problem in complexity theory and
is intimately connected with algebraic circuit lower bounds. A classical result of Heintz and
Schnorr [HS80] shows that a random set of points from a sufficiently large finite grid in kn is a
hitting set with high probability. However, certifying whether a set of points from kn is a hitting
set is a non-trivial task: since arbitrary constants from k are allowed to appear in such circuits in
Valiant’s model, there are potentially uncountably many polynomials computable bysmallalgebraic
circuits, so it is not possible simply to enumerate all polynomials computed by such circuits. To

an instance of Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz. This observation gives a PSPACE procedure to certify a
hitting set and using Koiran’s theorem, a PH construction assuming GRH. In view of the recent
result HNk∈CH [AGS26], the same certification can now be performed unconditionally in the

A natural question that arises in this context is the complexity of certifying hitting sets for the
border class VP. A polynomial in VP is not known to admit small algebraic circuits: it is, however,
a limit of polynomials having small circuits. A striking example of the power of approximative
complexity is the case of width-2 algebraic branching programs. It is known that this model is not
universal, i.e. there exist explicit polynomials that cannot be computed by width-2 ABPs [ AW16].
However, the corresponding border class is known to be universal [ BIZ18]. Thus, a set that is a
hitting set for every member of an approximating family need not be a hitting set for its limit,
and the preceding discussion about certifying hitting sets for VP does not directly translate to
the analogous question for VP. The work of Forbes and Shpilka [ FS18] and Guo, Saxena, and
Sinhababu [GSS19] addressed precisely this issue. Forbes and Shpilka [ FS18] gave a PSPACE
upper bound to certify hitting sets for VP via the notion ofrobust hitting sets. Guo, Saxena, and
Sinhababu [GSS19] subsequently generalised the PSPACE bound to hold over arbitrary fields, via
a reduction to APS. Both works [ FS18, GSS19] raise the question of whether the complexity of
certifying hitting sets for VPcan be improved toPH.
Hitting sets also arise in Mulmuley’s formulation of Noether normalisation in Geometric
Complexity Theory [Mul17]. Mulmuley shows [ Mul17] that for any explicit families of varieties
(and specifically for the determinantal variety ∆[det,m]), the construction of aseparating explicit
system of parametersreduces to the construction of hitting sets. Mulmuley observed a substantial
gap between the complexity of the underlying algebraic objects and the then-known complexity of
constructing such normalizing maps, calling this theGCT chasm. The PSPACE upper bounds for
certifying hitting sets due to Forbes–Shpilka and Guo–Saxena–Sinhababu substantially narrowed
this gap. Our result further mitigates the GCT chasm toCH.
Null Cone ProblemA basic closure problem in computational invariant theory isnull-cone
membership. Let a reductive algebraic groupGact linearly on a vector spaceV. The null cone is
NG(V) :={v∈V: 0∈ G·v}.
This definition has several equivalent interpretations which make the problem particularly rich. In
classical invariant theory,NG(V ) is precisely the common zero set of all homogeneous G-invariant
polynomials of positive degree. Thus null-cone membership is a highly structured analogue of
polynomial-system feasibility, where the defining equations are given only implicitly through the
group action rather than explicitly as an input system.
The Hilbert–Mumford criterion says that v∈N G(V ) if and only if there is a one-parameter
subgroup λ : Gm→G such that limt→0λ(t)·v = 0. Thus membership in the null cone is already a
statement about algebraic degeneration: although no group element sends a nonzero v exactly to
zero, a suitable one-parameter family may do so in the limit.
Over C, there is a complementary analytic formulation. If cap(v) := infg∈G∥g·v∥ , then
v∈ NG(V ) if and only if cap(v) = 0. This optimisation-theoretic point of view has been
algorithmically fruitful. B¨ urgisser, Garg, Oliveira, Walter, and Wigderson [ BGO+18] showed

3 Encoding and Sampling Conventions
In this section we discuss the representation of field elements and polynomials, as well as the
random-sampling conventions used in the complexity bounds below.
Field elements.We denote elements of Q by pairs of binary integers. A finite field Fq, where
q =pe, is represented as Fp[U]/(µ(U)), with p prime and µ a specified monic irreducible polynomial
of degree e. Writing u for the residue class of U, a field element is represented in the power basis
1,u,...,u e−1. We denote the total input length by N. Thus the number of variables and monomials,
the coefficient bit lengths, and the logarithms of the degrees are polynomially bounded in N. Over
Q, clearing the denominators of each input polynomial does not change its APS status. We also
assume that all polynomials in an instance of APSk are non-constant: identically zero polynomials
may be deleted without changing the APS status, while a nonzero constant polynomial makes the
instance negative.
