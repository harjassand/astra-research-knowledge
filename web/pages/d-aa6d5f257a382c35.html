# Fracture-aware transport guarantees in microstructured porous solids

**Date:** 2026-10-08 (Australia/Brisbane)  
**Status:** conditional reduced-model theorem and exact counterexample; no nature-level validation or priority claim.

## Verdict

The sharp missing capability in the literature slice examined here is a **post-damage lower guarantee for transport through a mechanically loaded microstructure**. Recent fracture mechanics work can either (i) infer permeability from stiffness/closure in a calibrated family of rough fractures, or (ii) resolve flow through an already-open fracture with a coupled porous-flow model. Recent architected-fracture studies add programmable crack paths and distributed energy dissipation. In the papers inspected, none gives a certificate of the form “after every mechanically admissible crack/damage set up to energy budget (B), the remaining flow between specified ports is at least (Q_*).”

I attack that interface in two steps. First, an exact lubrication-flow counterexample shows that normal stiffness, porosity, and even the *histogram* of apertures do not determine permeability: rearranging the same two aperture values from streamwise to cross-stream bands changes conductance by an unbounded factor as the aperture contrast grows. Second, a finite hydraulic-network theorem couples measured throat conductances to per-link fracture energies and gives a verifiable sufficient (and in a restricted parallel-corridor model, sharp) design budget for retaining a target flow after damage.

The theorem is a standard electrical-network path/cut argument applied to a specified fracture–flow reduction. It is useful as a design certificate, but it is **not** a new continuum-fracture theorem, a validated material law, a demonstrated architecture, or a priority-cleared discovery. The physical question remains whether a printed or natural microstructure can be reduced to a monotone edge-deletion model with reliable post-damage edge conductances and rupture costs.

## What current primary work establishes—and leaves open

**Rough fracture stiffness-to-permeability scaling.** He et al. (2025) simulate three-dimensional rough rock-fracture contact with a bonded-particle DEM, vary RMS roughness and shear offset, and compute normal specific stiffness from closure. They then obtain flow from the simulated aperture field using a parallel-plate finite-difference model. Their fitted relation rescales stiffness and permeability across their chosen roughness and offset family. The authors explicitly exclude the high-stiffness/low-porosity asymptote because disconnected void space makes their closure-based/cubic-law permeability overpredict flow. Their simulations also show old paths closing while new paths activate. This pinpoints connectivity and evolving damage as the load-bearing gap; it does not refute their within-family collapse. The inspected study is numerical, not a measured stiffness-to-flow law for natural fractures. [He et al., *JGR: Solid Earth* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1029/2024JB030633).

**Flow through a fixed fracture in porous material.** Rivas et al. (2025) couple a Theory of Porous Media model to a phase-field representation and verify velocity and pressure predictions against a microfluidic chip. Their calibration uses a straight, already-open channel; the phase field is stationary during the flow calculation. Their physical chip is about (200\,\mu\mathrm m) thick, and PIV verifies the predicted free-flow/porous-flow transition. This is strong model/experiment work for an existing channel, but it does not validate crack propagation, evolving throat failure, or post-fracture transport guarantees. [Rivas et al., *Archive of Applied Mechanics* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00419-025-02892-7).

**Fracture-aware architecture and dissipation.** The 2024 damage-programmable metamaterial study combines crack guidance, shielding, and bridging; the 2025 dual-bond study uses sequential weak-bond fracture and plastic hinges to distribute dissipation across a specimen. These are strong mechanical baselines. Their reported design objective is crack trajectory/resistance and mechanical energy absorption; the coupled hydraulic lower-bound question addressed here is distinct. [Wang et al., *Nature Communications* (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-51757-0); [Meng et al., *Nature Communications* (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-62007-2).

The paper-level boundary is narrow: a broad priority search has not been completed. The novelty claim is therefore **UNKNOWN**. The defensible finding is that the specific path/cut certificate below addresses an interface not supplied by these inspected demonstrations.

## Counterexample: same compliance and aperture statistics, very different transport

Consider a thin Newtonian fluid slit of length (L), width (W), viscosity \(\mu\), and aperture field (h(x,y)\), with (h\ll L,W\), no slip at the walls, and creeping flow. The lubrication equation has local in-plane conductance proportional to \(h^3/(12\mu)\).

Compare two layouts with the same aperture histogram: aperture (b) occupies area fraction \(\varphi\), and aperture \(a\in(0,b)\) occupies fraction \(1-\varphi\).

* **Streamwise bands:** each aperture band runs from inlet to outlet, so the bands conduct in parallel:
  \[
  G_{\parallel}=\frac{W}{12\mu L}\big(\varphi b^3+(1-\varphi)a^3\big).
  \]
* **Cross-stream bands:** every flow path crosses the two aperture regions in series:
  \[
  G_{\mathrm{series}}=\frac{W}{12\mu L}\left(\frac{\varphi}{b^3}+\frac{1-\varphi}{a^3}\right)^{-1}.
  \]

Thus
\[
\frac{G_{\parallel}}{G_{\mathrm{series}}}
=\big(\varphi b^3+(1-\varphi)a^3\big)
  \left(\frac{\varphi}{b^3}+\frac{1-\varphi}{a^3}\right).
\]
At \(\varphi=1/2\), with \(r=b/a\), this is
\[
\frac14(r^3+2+r^{-3}).
\]
For (b/a=10), the exact ratio is (250.50025). As (r\to\infty), it grows as (r^3/4); there is no finite stiffness-only or histogram-only permeability multiplier.

In an independent-column normal-compliance model, every patch of a given aperture class follows the same local normal traction–closure curve, and the global normal force is the area sum. The two layouts then have the same full global normal force–closure curve—not merely the same tangent stiffness—because they contain the same fractions of the two patch classes. They also have the same mean aperture, porosity, and aperture histogram. Yet their hydraulic conductances differ by the ratio above. This is an exact counterexample for that explicitly named local-compliance/lubrication model. Lateral elastic coupling or anisotropic contacts in a real fracture can break the equality of mechanical curves and must be measured; the counterexample does not assert that natural rock surfaces obey independent columns.

At fixed pressure drop, hydraulic power dissipation is \(\mathcal P=\Delta p\,Q=G(\Delta p)^2\), so the streamwise design transmits and dissipates 250.5 times as much power in the (r=10) fixture. At fixed required throughput, the pressure and power needed are inversely proportional to (G). The dissipation comparison therefore depends on the operating control (fixed \(\Delta p\) versus fixed \(Q\)); “more dissipation” is not intrinsically a better design.

This construction intentionally changes directional connectivity and is outside the isotropic Gaussian-roughness family in He et al. It does **not** disprove their equation for the family they simulated. It does show why any transfer to general microstructures needs a connectivity- or directional-flow observable rather than only a scalar normal stiffness, mean aperture, or one-point aperture statistics.

## Conditional fracture–transport certificate

Reduce the measured microstructure to a finite graph \(G=(V,E)\) with inlet (s), outlet (t), hydraulic throat conductance \(g_e>0\), and irreversible occlusive-damage cost \(c_e>0\) for each throat/bridge edge. For a long rectangular slit segment (e), a first model is
\[
g_e=\frac{w_e h_e^3}{12\mu\ell_e},\qquad c_e=\Gamma_e A_e,
\]
where (w_e,h_e,\ell_e) are throat geometry, and \(\Gamma_e A_e\) is its calibrated fracture-surface work. In a design certificate, use a measured lower bound \(\underline g_e\) on post-load conductance and a measured lower bound \(\underline c_e\) on the energy needed to occlude/fail that edge. The simple cubic slit law is a benchmark, not a premise if pore-resolved Stokes conductance is available.

For a route \(P\), define its series conductance
\[
g(P)=\left(\sum_{e\in P}\frac{1}{\underline g_e}\right)^{-1}.
\]
Choose pairwise edge-disjoint (s\)-to-(t) routes (P_1,\ldots,P_m) satisfying (g(P_i)\ge G_*\). Define their certificate failure budget
\[
B_{\mathrm{cert}}=\sum_{i=1}^m \min_{e\in P_i}\underline c_e.
\]

**Theorem (robust route certificate).** Suppose a damage event only deletes/occludes edges, and any admissible event with total occlusion energy below (B) deletes edges of total cost at most that budget. If (B<B_{\mathrm{cert}}), then the post-damage effective conductance satisfies
\[
G_{\mathrm{eff}}(G\setminus D)\ge G_*.
\]
Consequently, at pressure drop \(\Delta p_{\max}\), the model guarantees \(Q\ge G_*\Delta p_{\max}\). If the graph consists exactly of the listed disjoint routes with no additional bypasses, then (B_{\mathrm{cert}}) is the exact minimum edge-deletion cost that can reduce conductance below (G_*).

**Proof.** If (D) hit every (P_i), it would contain at least one edge from each route. Edge-disjointness and additive costs imply its cost is at least \(\sum_i\min_{e\in P_i}\underline c_e=B_{\mathrm{cert}}\). Thus (B<B_{\mathrm{cert}}) leaves some complete route (P_i). Its series conductance is at least (G_*). In a passive resistor/Stokes network, adding available edges cannot lower effective conductance (Rayleigh monotonicity), so the full surviving network has conductance at least that of the intact route. In the exact-union case, choosing the cheapest edge from each route costs (B_{\mathrm{cert}}), removes every route, and makes the conductance zero. \(\square\)

This is deliberately a **certificate**, not an optimal-design algorithm. The candidate must supply measured or validated bounds \(\underline g_e,\underline c_e\), and the selected routes must survive the damage model. The generic max-flow/min-cut intuition alone is insufficient for a nonzero conductance target; the per-route series-resistance check supplies the quantitative lower bound.

### Worked reduced-model fixture

Take three edge-disjoint corridors. Each corridor contains two rectangular slit segments in series, each with (w=0.5\,\mathrm{mm}), (h=50\,\mu\mathrm m), \(\ell=1\,\mathrm{mm}\), and water-like \(\mu=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{Pa\,s}\). The cubic-slit model gives
\[
g_e=5.2083\times10^{-12}\;\mathrm{m^3\,Pa^{-1}\,s^{-1}},\qquad
g(P_i)=2.6042\times10^{-12}\;\mathrm{m^3\,Pa^{-1}\,s^{-1}}.
\]
For target \(G_*=2.5\times10^{-12}\;\mathrm{m^3\,Pa^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\), each surviving route clears the target. If each corridor has one calibrated (1\,\mu\mathrm J) occlusion edge and a more costly companion edge, then \(B_{\mathrm{cert}}=3\,\mu\mathrm J\). Any damage budget strictly below this leaves (Q\ge G_*\Delta p\); at (3\,\mu\mathrm J), the three cheap edges can be removed and the exact-union network is disconnected. These are constructed parameters for a transparent arithmetic example, not measurements from a paper or specimen.

## Strong baselines and what this adds

1. **Closure/permeability scaling:** use He et al.’s calibrated roughness/offset relation, or their full simulated aperture field and parallel-plate flow solve. This is more informative than scalar stiffness and is the strongest relevant numerical baseline in the inspected literature. It already handles the in-family response well; the authors themselves mark low-porosity disconnected paths as the failure region. The proposed certificate adds a lower-bound question after a specified admissible damage budget, rather than replacing their aperture solver.
2. **Coupled continuum model:** use the TPM/phase-field model and microfluidic velocity comparison of Rivas et al. for a fixed open fracture in a porous plate. It has direct experiment/model comparison for the flow field. The new certificate addresses edge loss after evolving mechanical damage, an interface their fixed-channel experiment does not test.
3. **Pore-resolved flow:** solve Stokes flow on the segmented 3D geometry after each measured damage state. This is the strongest equally informed flow predictor if the full geometry is acquired. The path certificate is cheaper to verify and can give a worst-case guarantee, but may be very conservative and does not infer geometry, damage, or material law.
4. **Architecture-only toughening:** damage-programmable and dual-bond fracture metamaterials are strong mechanical comparators for crack resistance and dissipation. A fair coupled comparison must match mass/solid fraction, initial normal stiffness, pressure/flow target, notch/load, and tested damage budget, then measure conductance after each event. Fracture-energy improvement alone does not imply retained transport.

The result does not beat these baselines on predictive accuracy, and no experiment has established an advantage. It supplies a verifiable response guarantee in a specified reduced model, where an average-aperture law or initial-stiffness fit cannot provide one.

## Decisive tests and falsifiers

**Test A: connectivity versus stiffness.** Fabricate two thin microfluidic plates with the same area fractions of (b=100\,\mu\mathrm m) and (a=10\,\mu\mathrm m) gaps, but put the (b)-bands streamwise in one plate and cross-stream in the other. Use the same material, thickness, normal loading and inlet/outlet length. Calibrate and match the normal force–closure curves; image aperture distributions; measure \(Q/\Delta p\) in the laminar regime. The model predicts a conductance ratio of (250.50025), subject to finite-width edge effects and manifold losses. A substantially smaller ratio with verified geometry would falsify the independent-slit approximation for the chip (for example, side leakage or lateral coupling), not the algebra. If stiffness alone predicts both layouts within measurement error despite that verified conductance separation, the claimed stiffness-only equivalence is falsified.

**Test B: post-fracture route guarantee.** Fabricate a porous plate with three separated, imaged flow corridors and calibrated sacrificial occlusion/bridge sites; introduce notches at preregistered sites to control failure order. On separate same-batch coupons, acquire each route’s pressure-flow conductance and each bridge’s energy-to-occlude distribution. Then apply quasi-static loading with pressure maintained, measuring force/displacement, crack events by microscopy or DIC, (Q(t)), and \(\Delta p(t)\). The predicted gate is (Q/\Delta p\ge G_*) until cumulative measured occlusion energy reaches \(B_{\mathrm{cert}}\). A lower conductance before that gate, given intact imaged route and calibrated lower bounds, falsifies the reduced network assumptions or certificate implementation. Failure of the prediction after those assumptions are directly verified falsifies the theorem’s stated interface; it should not be recast as a nature-level discovery.

The strongest control is a plate with the same solid fraction, initial stiffness, mean aperture, total initial conductance and notch position, but a single nonredundant flow route. A second control is pore-resolved Stokes simulation on the **measured post-damage geometry**. Compare area-normalized transport, initial crack-initiation load, dissipated fracture energy, and the worst post-damage \(Q/\Delta p\), not only peak load or work to complete failure.

## Costs, conditioning, and model–nature boundary

**Fabrication and scale.** Test A needs two-level slit geometry, repeated channels, a calibrated normal loading fixture, inlet/outlet manifolds, and low-pressure tubing; a 10–100 \(\mu\mathrm m\) aperture scale and millimetre-to-centimetre length are a coupon experiment, not direct evidence for bulk rock or a thick 3D scaffold. Rivas et al.’s 200-\(\mu\mathrm m\)-thick chip shows that microfluidic fabrication/PIV at this scale is feasible, while also documenting why full 3D depth effects matter. Transfer to a 3D specimen requires volumetric tomography or a validated nondestructive pore map plus load-induced registration.

**Acquisition and sensing.** At minimum, acquire inlet/outlet pressure, volumetric flow, normal force/displacement, and damage timing. PIV or fluorescent tracer imaging identifies route activation; microscopy or micro-CT resolves slit/edge connectivity. A scalar stiffness/impedance proxy is not enough to assign a path lower bound. Destructive post-mortem imaging can calibrate a model but cannot by itself certify a live in-service structure.

**Conditioning and computation.** Slit conductance scales as (h^3): to first order, relative conductance error is (3\,\delta h/h+\delta w/w-\delta\ell/\ell-\delta\mu/\mu) for independent small signed errors. A 10% aperture uncertainty can therefore create roughly 30% local conductance uncertainty before accounting for connectivity; a near-closed throat can dominate a series route. Use conservative \(\underline g_e\) and propagate segmentation/calibration error. Fracture costs require mode-, rate-, environment-, and fatigue-specific calibration. Once a graph is supplied, checking proposed routes is linear in the number of edges; finding a globally optimal architecture under multiple fracture and flow constraints is a separate combinatorial design problem and is not included. Pore-resolved Stokes solves and 3D tomography may dominate runtime and experiment cost. No dollar or instrument-time values are invented.

**Nature boundary.** The theorem assumes laminar Newtonian flow, fixed port topology, additive per-edge conductances, monotone damage represented as throat deletion/occlusion, conservative lower bounds on each surviving route’s post-load conductance, additive rupture costs, and no unmodeled global closure of intact edges. Real fractures can both open and close paths; rough contact, multiphase flow, slip, gouge/debris, dissolution, inertia, fatigue, plasticity and pressure feedback can all violate these assumptions. Rivas et al. validate a particular fixed-fracture flow transition; He et al. simulate damage/closure and explicitly encounter disconnected pore error. Neither source validates this graph reduction in nature.
