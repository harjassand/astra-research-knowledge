## G1. Normalization and exact finite class

Let the covariate space be a singleton, let `F={0,+1,-1}`, let independent fair signs have sum `S`, and fix `n>=1`. The definition in Block–Rakhlin–Sellke, Eq. (5), is exactly

    R_eta(F)=n^{-1} E max_{f in F} [S f-eta n f^2].

Thus maximizing the three values gives

    R_eta(F)=n^{-1} E (|S|-eta n)_+,
    R_eta(F/2)=n^{-1} E (|S|/2-eta n/4)_+.

The map `ell(u)=u/2` is centered and is a contraction. At `eta=0` the second quantity is exactly one half of the first. At positive eta the thresholds differ by a factor of two. The finite class is symmetric, measurable, bounded, contains zero and is Donsker; it is not convex. No translation, adaptive data choice or hidden enlargement is present.

## G2. Exponential ratio, including the small-eta regime

For `0<eta<=1/4`, the first normalized integrand is at most one, so the simple-walk Chernoff bound gives

    R_eta(F)<=P(|S|>eta n)<=2 exp(-eta^2 n/2).

Assume `k=n/2+3 eta n/8` is integral. Then `S=3 eta n/4` is a legal endpoint and the contracted integrand divided by n equals `eta/8`. Put `p=k/n`. Under `Binomial(n,p)`, k is a mode: `floor((n+1)p)=k`, since `np=k` and `0<p<1`. Its probability is at least `1/(n+1)`. Changing its parameter to one half therefore gives

    P_{1/2}(S=3 eta n/4)>=exp[-n D(p||1/2)]/(n+1).

For `x=2p-1=3 eta/4`, integrating the derivative `atanh(x)` yields the convergent expansion

    D((1+x)/2||1/2)=sum_{j>=1} x^(2j)/[(2j)(2j-1)]
                  <=x^2/2+x^4/[12(1-x^2)].

Because `eta^2<=1/16`, its coefficient after division by eta squared is at most

    9/32+81/(192*247)<3/10.

The contraction numerator is consequently at least

    eta exp[-(3/10) eta^2 n]/[8(n+1)].

Dividing by the previous upper bound proves the advertised lower bound

    R_eta(F/2)/R_eta(F)
       >=eta exp(eta^2 n/5)/[16(n+1)].

The denominator is strictly positive: the all-equal signs produce `n(1-eta)>0` before normalization. For `eta=1/4` and n divisible by 32 the endpoint is integral and the ratio lower bound is `exp(n/80)/[64(n+1)]`.

The claimed legal small-eta family also reconstructs. For integer `h>=4`, set

    n_h=64h 2^h,  eta_h=1/(8h).

Here `k=(32h+3)2^h` is integral. Since `log 64<6`, `log h<=h-1`, and `log 2<1`, one has `log n_h<5+2h<=4h^2`; hence `eta_h<=(4 sqrt(log n_h))^{-1}`. The lower bound becomes

    exp[2^h/(5h)]/[128h(n_h+1)].

Its logarithm divided by `log n_h=O(h)` tends to infinity. Thus even arbitrary fixed powers of n, and in particular the recent positive theorem's `sqrt(log n)(1+1/eta_h)` factor, cannot repair this example without changing the class assumptions. Within that theorem's stated class conditions, convexity is the missing condition.

## G3. Convex completion changes the offset functional

The convex hull consists of constant functions in `[-1,1]`. Completing the square shows that its optimizer is `clip(S/(2 eta n),-1,1)`. The feasible constant `S/n` lies in the interval and produces `(1-eta)S^2/n` before division by n. The unconstrained optimum is `S^2/(4 eta n)`. Using `E S^2=n` gives

    (1-eta)/n<=R_eta(conv F)<=1/(4 eta n).

For the preceding eta range the lower bound is positive. Combining it with G2 proves

    R_eta(conv F)/R_eta(F)
       >=(1-eta) exp(eta^2 n/2)/(2n).

At eta zero the convex hull leaves the linear supremum unchanged. The positive-eta difference therefore is an actual cost of this statistical completion, even in one dimension. Applying a convex-class theorem to the completed class does not give its certificate in terms of the original discrete offset functional.

## G4. Actual same-data regression controls

Let the labels be independent fair signs at the same covariate. The risk of a constant t is `1+t^2`; zero is the best candidate in both libraries. Its empirical risk is `1-2t S/n+t^2`.

Comparison of t=0 with t=+/-1 gives the thresholds `S/n=+/-1/2`; choosing zero at ties gives excess risk `P(|S|>n/2)`. Comparison with t=+/-1/2 gives thresholds `+/-1/4` and excess risk `(1/4)P(|S|>n/4)`. These are exact population expectations of data-driven estimators, not a universal ordering between estimators.

The statement that shrinking can worsen risk is also analytically verifiable, without a numerical sweep. For n divisible by 16, the endpoint `S=3n/8` belongs to the smaller-threshold event. Setting `p=11/16` and `x=3/8`, the same mode argument and series yield

    D(11/16||1/2)<=9/128+81/42240<3/40.

Thus the shrunk estimator's expected excess risk is at least `exp(-3n/40)/[4(n+1)]`, whereas the original estimator's risk is at most `2 exp(-n/8)`. Their ratio is at least `exp(n/20)/[8(n+1)]` and eventually exceeds one. For other n the reverse ordering can hold; O4 does not assert otherwise.

The two-step procedure in Liang–Rakhlin–Sridharan Eq. (2) first chooses a proper ERM g and then minimizes over `star(F,g)`. For each possible `g in {0,+1,-1}`, the union of its segments to all other library points is exactly `[-1,1]`. The second-stage minimizer is `S/n`, with expected excess risk `E S^2/n^2=1/n`. A direct sample-mean algorithm uses the identical labels and O(n) additions plus division, giving the same answer. This is the competent control for the completed-class repair. The supplied finite library and the population used to evaluate the example are separate from acquiring scientifically correct hypotheses or a noise law.
