Abstract
    

In this paper, we prove that output-sensitive sparse polynomial GCD computation over finite fields is NP-hard under BPP many-one reduction. More precisely, for two sparse univariate polynomials f,gf,g with finite field coefficients, there exists no randomized algorithm to compute gcd⁡(f,g)\gcd(f,g), which is polynomial-time in the sizes of f,g,gcd⁡(f,g)f,g,\gcd(f,g) under the standard complexity assumption NP⊈BPP\mathrm{NP}\nsubseteq\mathrm{BPP}.
This settles the open problem posed as Challenge 5 in Davenport and Carette [2009] in the finite field setting.
Furthermore, we show that the Roots of Unity Detection problem over finite fields is NP-hard; that is, determining whether the GCD of a sparse univariate polynomial and xn−1x^{n}-1 has nonzero degree is NP-hard.
  




1 Introduction




The greatest common divisor (GCD) for polynomials is one of the core and most extensively studied problems in computer algebra von zur Gathen and Gerhard [2013].
For two dense degree-DD univariate polynomials over a field, the fast Euclidean algorithm can be used to compute their GCD using O⁡(D​log2​D​log⁡log⁡D)O(D\,\log^{2}D\,\log\log D) field operations [von zur Gathen and Gerhard, 2013, Chapter 11].




In practical computations and in computer algebra systems like Maple and Mathematica, polynomials are typically represented in a sparse form SparsityChallenges,Roche2018, meaning that only the nonzero coefficients and their corresponding exponents are stored in lists.
Consider a sparse univariate polynomial ff of degree DD, containing TT nonzero terms, and whose coefficients have a maximum absolute value of HH. Its size is tightly bounded by




Size​(f)=O⁡(T⁡(log⁡D+log⁡H))=O⁡(T​log⁡(D​H)).\text{Size}(f)=O(T(\log D+\log H))=O(T\log(DH)).



So a sparse polynomial-time algorithm should have bit complexity that is polynomial in TT, log⁡D\log D, and log⁡H\log H.




There exist no sparse polynomial-time GCD algorithms, because the GCD of two sparse polynomials can have a size that is exponential in the sizes of the input polynomials, as shown below.



Example 1 (Schinzel [2002]).



Let pp, qq be distinct primes and
f=xp​q−1,g=xp+q−xp−xq+1=(xp−1)​(xq−1)f=x^{pq}-1,g=x^{p+q}-x^{p}-x^{q}+1=(x^{p}-1)(x^{q}-1). Then we have the following irreducible decompositions:
xp−1=(x−1)​Φpx^{p}-1=(x-1)\Phi_{p},
xq−1=(x−1)​Φqx^{q}-1=(x-1)\Phi_{q},
xp​q−1=(x−1)​Φp​Φq​Φp​qx^{pq}-1=(x-1)\Phi_{p}\Phi_{q}\Phi_{pq},
where Φn\Phi_{n} is the nn-th cyclotomic polynomial.
Thus




gcd⁡(f,g)\displaystyle\gcd(f,g)
=(x−1)​Φp​Φq=(xp−1)​Φq=xp​Φq−Φq\displaystyle=(x-1)\Phi_{p}\Phi_{q}=(x^{p}-1)\Phi_{q}=x^{p}\Phi_{q}-\Phi_{q}



which contains 2​q2q terms, assuming q<pq<p.





This implies that the strongest guaranty we can provide is output-sensitive complexity; in other words, the algorithm’s running time must explicitly depend on the size of the GCD.




There exist many breakthrough algorithms for GCD, both output-sensitive and output-insensitive,
including methods based on sub-resultant computation Collins [1967]; Brown and Traub [1971]; Hong and Yang [2024],
methods based on interpolations Zippel [1979]; van der Hoeven and Lecerf [2021]; Hu and Monagan [2016]; Huang and Gao [2025]; Kaltofen and Trager [1990], and modular methods based on Hensel lifting Moses and Yun [1973].
Recent research has continued to advance GCD computation, focusing on improvements such as the half-GCD algorithm van der Hoeven [2025]. Additionally, constant-time variants of the GCD algorithm have been developed, which are particularly valuable in cryptographic applications where resistance to timing attacks is essential Bernstein and Yang [2019].
In many cases, these algorithms perform quite effectively, as demonstrated by their use in popular software systems such as Maple and Mathematica.
However, the complexity of all these methods depends on the degrees of the input polynomials; therefore, they are not polynomial-time algorithms.




The following example shows that, even for output-sensitive complexity, the procedure to compute the GCD might be exponential.



Example 2.



Let p>qp>q be two distinct primes, and
f=xp−1,g=xq−1f=x^{p}-1,g=x^{q}-1.
Then their GCD can be uniquely written as





gcd⁡(f,g)=x−1=A​f+B​g.\displaystyle\gcd(f,g)=x-1=Af+Bg.



We can show that TB>p−qT_{B}>p-q; that is, even if Tgcd⁡(f,g)T_{\gcd(f,g)} is small, computing gcd⁡(f,g)\gcd(f,g) might require handling polynomials AA and BB that are exponential in the sizes of f,g,gcd⁡(f,g)f,g,\gcd(f,g).





Determining whether two sparse univariate polynomials over ℚ{\mathbb{Q}} possess a non-trivial GCD is NP-hard [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 3.3]; this result has also been generalized to polynomials over finite fields Gathen et al. [1993]; Kaltofen and Koiran [2005].
This brings us directly to the following problem [Davenport and Carette, 2009, Challenge 5] about the output-sensitive sparse polynomial GCD computation.





Open Problem 1.
Find an algorithm for computing gcd⁡(f,g)\gcd(f,g) that is polynomial-time in ‖f‖0\|f\|_{0}, ‖g‖0\|g\|_{0}, and ‖gcd⁡(f,g)‖0\|\gcd(f,g)\|_{0}.





For finite fields, we make Open Problem 1 more explicit as follows.





Open Problem 2.
For f,g∈𝔽q​[x]f,g\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x], if there exists an algorithm for computing gcd⁡(f,g)\gcd(f,g) that is polynomial in TT, log⁡D\log D, and log⁡q\log q,
where




D=max⁡(deg⁡(f),deg⁡(g)),T=max⁡(‖f‖0,‖g‖0,‖gcd⁡(f,g)‖0).D=\max(\deg(f),\deg(g)),T=\max(\|f\|_{0},\|g\|_{0},\|\gcd(f,g)\|_{0}).




Here, GCD is assumed to be monic to ensure its uniqueness.




1.1 Main Results




In this paper, we settle Open Problem 2 by proving that sparse polynomial GCD computation is NP-hard under bounded-error probabilistic polynomial-time (BPP) reduction.
Precisely, we have the following conditional obstruction.



Theorem 3.



If there were an algorithm that, for every input pair f,g∈𝔽q​[x]f,g\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x] satisfying




deg(f),deg(g)≤D,|supp(f)|,|supp(g)|,|supp(gcd(f,g))|≤T,\deg(f),\deg(g)\leq D,\qquad|\operatorname{supp}(f)|,\ |\operatorname{supp}(g)|,\ |\operatorname{supp}(\gcd(f,g))|\leq T,



computes the sparse GCD in bit complexity that is polynomial
in TT, log⁡D\log D, and log⁡q\log q, then NP⊆BPP\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}.
Thus, under the standard complexity assumption NP⊈BPP\mathrm{NP}\nsubseteq\mathrm{BPP}, no
such algorithm exists.





Open Problem 1 remains unsolved in the settings of integers.
Even for the finite field setting, we may further ask whether the problem is NP-hard? or PSPACE-hard? Or is the decision of whether a sparse polynomial is the GCD of two sparse polynomials NP-complete?




Furthermore, we show that the Roots of Unity Detection problem is NP-hard; that is, given a sparse univariate polynomial ff of degree bounded by DD, determining whether ff vanishes at a root of unity whose order is at most DD is NP-hard.
Equivalently, deciding whether deg⁡gcd⁡(f,g)>0\deg\gcd(f,g)>0 is NP-hard for g=xn−1g=x^{n}-1 and a sparse polynomial ff.



Theorem 4.



If there were an algorithm that, for f∈𝔽q​[x]f\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x] and g=xn−1g=x^{n}-1 satisfying




deg⁡f≤Dn≤D|supp⁡(f)|≤T,\deg f\leq D\qquad n\leq D\qquad|\operatorname{supp}(f)|\leq T,



decides whether deg⁡gcd⁡(f,g)>0\deg\gcd(f,g)>0 in bit complexity that is polynomial
in TT, log⁡D\log D, and log⁡q\log q, then NP⊆BPP\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}.
Thus, under the standard complexity assumption NP⊈BPP\mathrm{NP}\nsubseteq\mathrm{BPP}, no
such algorithm exists.





Theorem 4 strengthens the previously known result that deciding whether two sparse polynomials are co-prime is NP-hard [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 3.3].
Note that Theorem 4 is not output-sensitive, so it cannot be deduced from Theorem 3, since gcd⁡(xn−1,f)\gcd(x^{n}-1,f) could have exponential size, as shown in Example 1.
Theorem 4 states that determining whether there exists a unit root that is a zero of ff is NP-hard, whereas [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 4.1] shows that deciding whether there exists a unit root that is not a zero of ff is NP-hard. Therefore, Theorem 4 cannot be inferred from [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 4.1].

1.2 Related Work




Most of the classic NP-hardness results concerning GCD computation are due to Plaisted Plaisted [1977]; Zippel [1979]; Plaisted [1984]. These include the NP-hardness of determining whether the GCD of sparse integer polynomials is nontrivial [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 3.3], as well as the NP-completeness of deciding whether xN−1x^{N}-1 fails to divide a given sparse polynomial [Plaisted, 1984, Theorem 4.1].
Non-trivial GCD is shown to be NP-hard over finite fields Gathen et al. [1993].
Deciding the square-freeness of a sparse polynomial in ℤ⁡[x]{\mathbb{Z}}[x] is NP-hard Karpinski and Shparlinski [1999].
The Co-prime test of sparse polynomials over large finite fields is CoNP-hard Kaltofen and Koiran [2005].




For univariate polynomials represented by straight-line programs, an algorithm that runs in time polynomial in Size​(f,g)\text{Size}(f,g) and DD is provided in Kaltofen [1988].
Filaseta, Granville, and Schinzel give a sparse GCD algorithm that is linear in log⁡D\log D when one of the polynomials does not have cyclotomic-factors Filaseta et al. [2008].




Multivariate polynomial GCD computation is central to algebraic and symbolic computation.
The mainstream approach reduces the multivariate case to univariate computations via modular methods or Hensel lifting, followed by interpolation or lifting to recover the true GCD.
Representative contributions include Brown’s interpolation-based algorithm for dense polynomials [Brown, 1971], the EZ-GCD algorithm using Hensel lifting for sparse polynomials by Moses and Yun [Moses and Yun, 1973],
Zippel’s probabilistic sparse GCD algorithm based on sparse interpolation and the Schwartz–Zippel lemma [Zippel, 1979].
Additionally, Kaltofen’s algorithm for polynomials given by straight-line programs [Kaltofen, 1988], extends naturally to the multivariate case. Likewise, the black-box GCD algorithm of Kaltofen and Trager [Kaltofen and Trager, 1990] also applies to multivariate polynomials.
The parallel sparse GCD algorithm of Hu and Monagan combining Kronecker substitution with Ben-Or–Tiwari sparse interpolation [Hu and Monagan, 2016].
These methods typically run in polynomial time, but often rely on randomization or additional assumptions to achieve deterministic complexity or explicit bit complexity bounds.
An algorithm of bit complexity O~​(n​D​TG​(Tf+Tg)​log2​q​log2​log2​1/δ)\widetilde{O}(nDT_{G}(T_{f}+T_{g})\log^{2}q\log^{2}\log^{2}1/\delta) is given over 𝔽q{\mathbb{F}}_{q} in Huang and Gao [2025].

2 Output-sensitive Sparse Polynomial GCD over Finite Field is NP-hard




In this section, we present the proof for Theorem 3. The proof consists of five steps given in the five subsections, respectively.




We outline the five steps of the proof below.
The first step in Section 2.1 isolates the SAT instance, reducing it to a bounded-clause formula that is either unsatisfiable or, with inverse-polynomial probability, uniquely satisfiable.
The second step in Section 2.2 constructs a suitable prime field and a system of roots of unity whose residue classes encode Boolean assignments.
The third step in Section 2.3 builds sparse polynomials whose common roots among these roots of unity are exactly the satisfying assignments of the isolated formula.
The fourth step in Section 2.4 compresses the whole list of constraint polynomials into one random sparse polynomial while preserving the relevant common roots with high probability.
The final step in Section 2.5 checks the sparse size and degree bounds of the constructed instance and then amplifies the randomized reduction to obtain the claimed BPP consequence.




We use the sparse bit model over variable finite fields: a polynomial over
𝔽q\mathbb{F}_{q} is represented by its nonzero coefficient–exponent pairs, the exponents are written in binary, and the field size is part of the input. The construction below uses only prime fields.




2.1 Boolean Isolation



Definition 5 (Boolean variable and literal).



A Boolean variable xx takes values in {0,1}\{0,1\} (false/true).
A literal is either a variable xx (positive literal) or its negation ¬x\neg x (negative literal).




Definition 6 (Clause and CNF).



A clause is a disjunction (logical OR) of literals.
A formula is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) if it is a conjunction (logical AND) of clauses.




Definition 7 (Satisfiability (SAT)).



Given a CNF formula FF on nn Boolean variables, the SAT problem asks: does there exist an assignment to the variables such that FF evaluates to true?
Such an assignment, if it exists, is called a satisfying assignment.





The Cook–Levin theorem states that SAT is NP-complete. The result was proved independently by
Cook [2023] and Levin [1973].



Theorem 8 (Cook–Levin Theorem).



SAT is NP-complete: every problem in NP can be reduced to SAT in polynomial time, and SAT itself belongs to NP.




Definition 9 (Parsimonious reduction).



A reduction from SAT to a problem PP is parsimonious if it preserves the exact number of satisfying assignments — not only whether a satisfying assignment exists, but also the count.




Definition 10 (Unique-3SAT-style promise problem).



A CNF formula with clause size at most three is called a Unique-3SAT-style instance under the promise that it has either zero or exactly one satisfying assignment. The promise problem asks to distinguish the two cases.





Formulas with more than one satisfying assignment may arise from failed randomized trials and lie outside the promise. For an exact three-literal display, repeated-literal padding is allowed:




(λ)​becomes​(λ∨λ∨λ),(λ∨μ)​becomes​(λ∨μ∨μ),(\lambda)\;\text{becomes}\;(\lambda\vee\lambda\vee\lambda),\qquad(\lambda\vee\mu)\;\text{becomes}\;(\lambda\vee\mu\vee\mu),



which preserves the number of satisfying assignments.




We use the notion of randomized polynomial-time reduction as defined by Valiant and Vazirani in their seminal paper [Valiant and Vazirani, 1985]. This notion allows a one-sided error: no-instances are always mapped to no-instances, while yes-instances are mapped to yes-instances with at least inverse-polynomial probability.



Definition 11 (Randomized polynomial-time reduction [Valiant and Vazirani, 1985]).



A problem AA is randomized polynomial-time reducible to a problem BB if there exists a randomized Turing machine TT running in polynomial time and a polynomial PP such that:


1.

Here |x||x| denotes the bit-length of the encoding of the input instance xx, i.e., the input size.

4 Conclusion




In this paper, we show that sparse polynomial GCD computation over a finite field is NP-hard under BPP reduction.
Taking the contrapositive, for sparse polynomials f,g∈𝔽q​[x]f,g\in{\mathbb{F}}_{q}[x], under the standard complexity assumption
NP⊈BPP\mathrm{NP}\nsubseteq\mathrm{BPP}, there is no algorithm which computes
gcd⁡(f,g)\gcd(f,g) for every finite-field input pair satisfying




deg⁡f,deg⁡g≤D,|supp⁡(f)|,|supp⁡(g)|,|supp⁡(gcd⁡(f,g))|≤T\deg f,\deg g\leq D,\qquad|\operatorname{supp}(f)|,\ |\operatorname{supp}(g)|,\ |\operatorname{supp}(\gcd(f,g))|\leq T



with bit complexity polynomial in TT, log⁡D\log D, and log⁡q\log q.
Equivalently, a positive answer to the question in this bit-complexity model
would imply NP⊆BPP\mathrm{NP}\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}.




We also show that the Roots of Unity Detection over finite fields is NP-hard under BPP reduction. Equivalently, determining whether a sparse polynomial f⁡(x)f(x) is co-prime to xn−1x^{n}-1 is NP-hard under BPP reduction.
