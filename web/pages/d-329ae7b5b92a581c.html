# H16 — focused prior-art and falsification gate for the oscillator H3 construction

Date: 2026-10-10. Sources checked online after reading `CHARTER.txt`, `NETWORK_GATE.txt`, the frozen s41 derivation and residue test, and current h07/h09/s45 reports.

## Decision

The new second-order construction is an explicit, model-specific recovery mechanism, not a desired transfer function supplied to the inverse solver. Under its stated nondegeneracy conditions, H1 plus the cubic Volterra kernel H3 determines a dense symmetric K and unknown positive nodewise cubic coefficients up to hidden signed relabeling. The key high-opposite-pair contraction is algebraically distinct from a generic black-box fit and resolves the orthogonal mode-shape ambiguity left by H1.

The strongest prior-art risk is parallel Wiener–Hammerstein (PWH) identification from Volterra kernels. In fact, the target H3 is exactly the cubic-order input-output kernel of an n-branch PWH system. This means the branch-separation structure is not new in isolation. The sources found do not, however, give the claimed specialized global reconstruction of physical K and all α_i from one-port H1/H3 for this continuous-time symmetric oscillator, nor its bounded-frequency O(n^3) query scheme. So the exact reconstruction is not superseded by an identified existing algorithm in the literature reviewed here, but a novelty claim must be narrowed to the special contraction, physical-coordinate recovery, and finite-resource/stability theorem—not to nonlinear FRF or branch decomposition generally.

Practical capability remains **OPEN**. The O(n^3) count is for exact cubic-kernel oracle samples; it is not yet an end-to-end laboratory sample/time/precision theorem. The numerical residue test uses kernels computed from auditor-known model parameters, and the damping values needed for its well-conditioned near-resonance matrices are very small.

## Target and frozen construction

The physical model is

\[
\ddot q+\gamma\dot q+Kq+\operatorname{diag}(\alpha)q^{\circ3}=e_1u,\qquad y=e_1^Tq,
\]

with K symmetric positive definite, common scalar damping γ, known dimension, one driven/measured coordinate, and unknown positive α_i. Write

\[
Q_r(s)=e_r^T(s^2+\gamma s+K)^{-1}e_1,\quad
K=U\Lambda U^T,\quad a=U^Te_1.
\]

The measured linear transfer is H1(s)=Q_1(s)=Σ_i a_i²/(s²+γs+λ_i). For simple poles and nonzero port residues it identifies λ_i and a_i², but not U: all U with the same first physical row give the same H1.

The cubic Volterra kernel is

\[
H_3(s_1,s_2,s_3)=-\sum_r\alpha_r Q_r(s_1+s_2+s_3)\prod_{j=1}^3Q_r(s_j).
\]

For fixed low frequencies s,t, put c=(s−t)/2 and F_R(s,t)=−H3(R+c,−R+c,t). Since the off-port resolvent entries obey Q_r(z)=−K_{r1}z^{-4}+O(z^{-5}) for r>1, while Q_1(z)=z^{-2}+O(z^{-3}), the port term appears at order R^-4 and hidden-node terms at R^-8. First extract α_1 from lim R^4F_R=α_1Q_1(s)Q_1(t), subtract that known port contribution, then

\[
B(s,t):=\lim_{R\to\infty}R^8\big[F_R-\alpha_1Q_1(s)Q_1(t)Q_1(R+c)Q_1(-R+c)\big]
=\sum_{r>1}\beta_r Q_r(s)Q_r(t),\quad \beta_r=\alpha_rK_{r1}^2.
\]

At n linearly independent low-frequency pairs, H1 supplies V_{ij}=a_i/(s_j²+γs_j+λ_i) and the B samples form Bmat=V^T A V (orientation convention depending on whether V stores modal vectors as columns), where

\[
A=U^T\operatorname{diag}(0,\beta_2,\ldots,\beta_n)U.
\]

If K_{r1}≠0 for all hidden r and the β_r are pairwise distinct and nonzero, eigendecomposition of A recovers the rows of U; the kernel direction is anchored by the H1 port residues a_i, with only hidden sign/permutation gauge left. Then K=UΛU^T and α_r=β_r/K_{r1}². This is global within the promise set, not merely a local fit.

The oscillator extension supplies a useful conditioning idea: as a function of R, F_R is rational with 4n known poles from the oscillator poles in Q(R+c) and Q(−R+c). Recovering its residues at bounded imaginary frequencies near resonances yields its R^-4 and R^-8 coefficients without driving R to infinity. The frozen claim is O(n) residue samples for each of n² low-frequency pairs, hence O(n³) cubic-kernel queries, maximum frequency O(√||K||), and polynomial resource dependence under inverse-polynomial damping, modal-gap, port-overlap, K_{r1}, and β-gap promises. The high-opposite-pair coefficient extraction and O(n³) count do not by themselves prove that stability statement.

## Strongest prior-art comparison: parallel Wiener–Hammerstein identification

The closest structural match is exact at H3 order. Define the branch input-output map

\[
y_3(t)=-\sum_{r=1}^n [Q_r*\{(Q_r*u)^3\}](t).
\]

Its cubic Volterra kernel is exactly the displayed H3. Thus, with the transfer functions Q_r used as both front and back branch filters and the static cubic gain −α_r, the candidate's H3 data form an n-branch parallel Wiener–Hammerstein kernel. H1 can be supplied as a separate linear branch. The full nonlinear oscillator is not generally the same PWH operator: state feedback generates fifth- and higher-order kernels that this static-block surrogate does not reproduce. The identity is nevertheless a strong prior-art discriminator because it shows that H3 branch separation itself sits inside a long-studied model class.

The most directly relevant constructive method found is Usevich, Dreesen and Ishteva, “Low-rank tensor recovery for Jacobian-based Volterra identification of parallel Wiener–Hammerstein systems” ([arXiv:2109.09584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.09584)). It obtains a rank-r tensor representation from specially chosen Jacobian samples of the Volterra polynomial map; the recovered factors encode the front/back filter coefficients and number of branches. Its stated model uses finite-memory FIR blocks, and the recovery is posed as partial tensor recovery/CPD (with alternating least squares suggested). It starts from Volterra kernel information and does not impose the oscillator's shared rational denominator, front=back constraint, symmetry, known H1 spectrum, or coordinate/eigenvector relationship. Consequently it is the strongest algorithmic comparator for branch extraction, but the published assumptions and guarantee do not directly provide the candidate's continuous-time physical K, α recovery or bounded-resonance error bound. Stable rational filters may be approximated by FIR truncations, but no error/conditioning transfer theorem for this problem was found.

Dreesen et al., “Modeling Parallel Wiener–Hammerstein Systems Using Tensor Decomposition of Volterra Kernels” ([arXiv:1609.08063](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.08063)), explicitly derive joint structured CP decompositions of several Volterra-kernel orders and report reconstruction experiments under noise. This is evidence that Volterra-kernel factorization and branch recovery are established, but the method again targets the PWH block model, not reconstruction of a symmetric physical stiffness matrix from one port with a global uniqueness certificate.

Schoukens et al., “Parametric identification of parallel Wiener–Hammerstein systems” ([Automatica DOI 10.1016/j.automatica.2014.10.105](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2014.10.105); author preprint [arXiv:1708.06543](https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.06543)), give an input-output-only identification pipeline: estimate best linear approximations at several excitation levels, decompose dynamics into parallel orthogonal branches, partition front/back LTI blocks, fit static nonlinearities, and finish with joint nonlinear least squares. They prove consistency of the initialization under their excitation/model assumptions and validate a custom PWH device. The same work discusses non-identifiability from arbitrary full-rank changes of branch coordinates, resulting in a coupled MIMO static nonlinearity. That caveat is directly relevant: a generic PWH fit is an I/O model and does not by itself certify the original node coordinates or K. It may fit a model that predicts the same observed response without producing the required physical realization.

**Exact distinction:** existing PWH work strongly overlaps the cubic branch representation and may recover equivalent branch filters given complete Volterra information. The proposed mechanism specializes this representation using H1's poles/residues and an opposite-frequency rational contraction to make a known zero-eigenvector plus a symmetric physical eigendecomposition. I found no source proving that exact specialized inference or its finite-sample/precision claim. Therefore the construction is not “first nonlinear branch identification”; it is a candidate for a restricted one-port physical-parameter reconstruction algorithm.

## Other close physical-system baselines

Peng, Lang and Billings, “Linear parameter estimation for multi-degree-of-freedom nonlinear systems using nonlinear output frequency-response functions” ([Mechanical Systems and Signal Processing DOI 10.1016/j.ymssp.2007.04.009](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymssp.2007.04.009); source manuscript [PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/3533/1/pengzk1.pdf.pdf)), use Volterra/NOFRF relations to estimate linear stiffness and damping in locally nonlinear MDOF systems. The paper's conclusion explicitly assumes known mass distribution and known location of the nonlinear component; its demonstration uses a chain-coupled structure, and it estimates linear parameters rather than the all-node unknown cubic law. It is an important direct precedent for extracting mechanical parameters from nonlinear frequency response, but it does not remove the arbitrary dense K / one scalar port orientation ambiguity under the interface here.

Setio, Jezequel and coauthors' nonlinear-modal FRF methods, and the later response-controlled stepped-sine method of Karaagacli and Ozguven (2021, [DOI 10.1016/j.ymssp.2020.107023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ymssp.2020.107023); [open paper](https://open.metu.edu.tr/bitstream/handle/11511/50718/10.1016%3Aj.ymssp.2020.107023.pdf)), provide a closer experimental comparison. RCT can use single-point excitation and characterize multiple cubic nonlinearities near an isolated mode; the published 5-DOF example measures responses at all DOFs. Its method assumes the mode of interest is sufficiently separated with no internal resonances, and produces amplitude-dependent nonlinear modal parameters/NNM curves. It does not infer all hidden nodewise α_i and an arbitrary dense K from a single measured coordinate. It demonstrates that single-point excitation is physically standard, but not that single-point sensing suffices.

Two scope restrictions prevent overstating the candidate. First, if the mechanical network is a known irreducible Jacobi/tridiagonal chain, the linear one-port spectral measure already determines K: its poles and residues provide the spectral data for classical Jacobi-matrix inverse reconstruction (Hald, “Inverse eigenvalue problems for Jacobi matrices,” 1976, [DOI 10.1016/0024-3795(76)90064-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-3795(76)90064-1)). The nonlinear mechanism matters for arbitrary dense/unstructured K, not ordinary known spring-chain topology. Second, Vanelli and Hendrickx's recurrent-network paper, [arXiv:2412.08472v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.08472v4), already establishes generic local identifiability for known-topology discrete-time node-activation networks with source/sink excitation/sensing (one port in strongly connected graphs). It does not use the continuous-time oscillator or give this global physical-coordinate formula, but it is a close general network-identifiability precedent and cannot be described as feedforward-only based on earlier versions.

## Falsification and finite-resource audit

1. **Exact degeneracies.** If any K_{r1}=0 then that node disappears from this first contraction; sparse networks generally have such zeros. If β_i=β_j, A has a multidimensional eigenspace and this diagonalization does not fix the hidden basis inside it. If a modal port overlap vanishes or eigenvalues repeat, H1 no longer provides the stated modal labels. These are failures of this construction, not impossibility theorems for higher-order kernels.
2. **No generic guarantee from a weakly nonlinear fit.** The extraction is a third-order derivative/Volterra coefficient. A small raw response signal, imperfect phase cycling, actuator/sensor dynamics, drift, finite settling, and deviation from a cubic law can dominate it. None of the cited tensor or modal algorithms supplies the candidate's exact high-pair cancellation with a finite physical error bound.
3. **Residue test is algebraic, not an experiment.** s41's `check_residue_recovery.py` computes H3 from known K, α and adds noise only after producing the complex H3 samples. For n=4,8,16 it reports 4n³ samples (256, 2048, 16384), max tested frequency ≈3, and small K errors, but uses γ≈1.25e−4, 2.68e−5, 6.25e−6, respectively; the reported Cauchy matrices are scaled by γ before conditioning. This checks interpolation arithmetic under the model, not the extraction of H3 from force/response records. In particular, the high-Q promise narrows each resonance and steady-state settling can require time proportional to at least 1/γ; if γ is an inverse-polynomial promise this is formally polynomial, but the constant, actual time, thermal/drift tradeoffs, and input amplitude/noise budget are not supplied.
4. **Count is not precision.** Per-pair rational interpolation has 4n poles, close pole pairs split on the damping scale; its noise gain depends on the pole/sample Cauchy matrix and target residue weights. The report contains sampled condition diagnostics but no theorem bounding the condition number uniformly in n under the stated promises. Subsequent congruence inversion/eigendecomposition additionally amplifies error by modal basis conditioning and 1/min(β-gap). Phase-cycling/Volterra estimation cost per cubic sample is unspecified, so O(n³) is a query count only.
5. **Strongest local falsifier from the operational gate.** s45's physically simulated first-order acquisition test ran the actual scalar-input/output ODE with three-tone phase cycling, eight sign patterns, two amplitudes and Richardson cancellation. Even with the H1 port term supplied as an oracle, at Ω=9 the hidden H3 was only 4.84×10^-7 versus a 6.44×10^-4 subtracted port term; at Ω=17 it needed a protocol-specific forecast of 3.88×10^13 scalar samples for 10% stochastic precision. That is first-order, not a direct refutation of oscillator resonance-residue interpolation. The oscillator case in s45 is spectral-only: at γ=.08 and Ω=9 it predicts |H3_hidden|=1.32×10^-8 and mixed output 2.67×10^-13 for three tones of amplitude .03; the document explicitly reports no oscillator advantage or acquired trajectory. This specifically falsifies any inference that the exact asymptotic identity or the residue-oracle test alone establishes a usable physical H3 acquisition protocol.

## Net assessment

The mechanism is mathematically substantive: it converts a rank-one physical input/output port into a hidden-coordinate bilinear form by taking a high-opposite-pair coefficient of the cubic response, then uses a matrix eigendecomposition to resolve the exact linear orthogonal ambiguity. Unlike ordinary model fitting, it does not assume K or the desired transfer function is provided, and the model parameters can be reconstructed from the resulting data under explicit generic conditions.

The strongest prior art is PWH identification, which contains the same H3 branch sum and already uses Volterra kernels plus low-rank tensor methods to identify branch dynamics. The exact model-specific result appears differentiated in its use of the H1 modal basis, shared physical resolvent, and coordinate matrix recovery, but priority is **UNKNOWN** from this focused search. The 2008 NOFRF and 2021 RCT work further show that mechanical FRF identification and cubic modal analysis are mature; their topology/sensing/isolated-mode assumptions do not match the dense one-port inverse problem.

Foundational target **OPEN**: no externally validated physical test, no independent proof review, and no end-to-end acquisition/precision/time theorem. The next decisive gate is a theorem that (i) uses only physically acquired H1/H3 samples, (ii) bounds rational residue recovery and modal inversion in the declared parameters, (iii) charges settling, cubic-kernel estimation, resets, calibration, and drift, and (iv) compares its resource cost with a matched PWH/Volterra fit on the same one-port oscillator family. Until that exists, record this as an exact generic structural-reconstruction candidate with a close prior-art overlap, not a demonstrated broad inverse-design capability.

## Addendum — conditional physical node census from a nonlinear quotient

The s43 canonical reduction supports a narrower consequence that is different from identifying the hidden stiffness matrix: under a connected symmetric positive-definite M-matrix stiffness `K` and a homogeneous positive cubic coefficient `α`, exact zero-initial one-port input-output data determine the *physical node count* `N`, even when they do not determine the hidden topology or individual synchronized coordinates.

Let `W` be the smallest `K`-invariant, cubic-closed subspace containing the port vector `e_1`. Since `K` is nonsingular, Cayley–Hamilton gives `K^{-1}=p(K)` for a polynomial `p`, so `K^{-1}e_1 ∈ W`. For an irreducible (connected) SPD M-matrix, `K^{-1}` is entrywise strictly positive. Therefore `W` has nonzero evaluation at every physical coordinate. The canonical ternary-subspace classification in `s43_proof/canonical_reduction.txt` says the active coordinates form signed-equality blocks; strict positivity rules out the negative-sign option, so every block `B` is an ordinary synchrony block on which vectors in `W` are constant. There are no inactive physical nodes, although directions transverse to `W` remain invisible to the port.

In the orthonormal block basis, whose vector `v_B` has entries `1/√|B|` on block `B`, the exact reduced model has cubic coefficient

\[
\bar\alpha_B=\frac{\alpha}{|B|}.
\]

The port is a singleton block, so its cubic response identifies the common α. The full-closure one-port reconstruction identifies the reduced coefficients \(\bar\alpha_B\) up to quotient-node permutation. Hence each multiplicity is the exact integer \(|B|=\alpha/\bar\alpha_B\), and

\[
N=\sum_B\frac{\alpha}{\bar\alpha_B}.
\]

This is a physical-size lift of the minimal realization: it does not identify `K`'s hidden block internals or restore transverse modes. The formula itself is an immediate consequence once the exact canonical quotient is known. The potentially substantive step is the interface-specific chain that recovers that quotient from one-port zero-initial controlled I/O, then proves positivity excludes inactive coordinates and sign-synchronized blocks. The hypotheses matter: heterogeneous `α`, a reducible/non-M-matrix `K`, or a port setup that does not identify the reduced cubic coefficients invalidates this census argument. The result is exact finite-jet identifiability, not a practical noisy census; s43's adaptive word-depth and rank/flatness steps retain their severe conditioning and acquisition-cost limitations.

### Closest size-identification and synchrony precedents

Tang et al., [“Dynamical network size estimation from local observations,” *New Journal of Physics* 22 (2020), 093031](https://doi.org/10.1088/1367-2630/abaf2f) construct a Hankel matrix from one node's unforced time series and estimate its rank. Their theory assumes full observability of the linearized network (and a trajectory that excites the relevant modes); more decisively, their nonlinear four-node example returns size three because two nodes have identical dynamics. They explicitly describe the result as a *minimal realization* and treat dynamically symmetric nodes as one. That is the same obstruction addressed here, not a counterexample to the conditional lift: rank alone returns quotient order, while the known homogeneous cubic coefficient supplies block multiplicities.

Brovia, Zino, Succar, and Porfiri, [“Inference of the size of nonlinear network systems from perceptible dynamics,” *Chaos* 36 (2026), 023147](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0318179), is a closer modern predecessor and must be cited in any novelty discussion. It is model-agnostic and demonstrates estimation from a single accessible node, but uses `M` independent measurements across different initial conditions or segments of a longer trajectory, clusters nearby measured trajectories, and forms a detection matrix for each cluster. The paper's rank-to-size step is for observable linearized time-varying dynamics. In the present zero-initial scalar-port interface, all admissible trajectories remain in `W`, so transverse synchronized modes need not be excited or observed; a detection-matrix rank then certifies only the accessible/minimal order. Their broader acquisition can potentially expose such modes when initial conditions vary across the physical system, whereas this census uses a different physical prior—homogeneous nodewise cubic response—to recover multiplicity without those inaccessible initial-state controls. Thus it neither subsumes the stated one-port result nor makes it broad or assumption-free.

The quotient structure itself is established territory. Schaub et al., [“Graph partitions and cluster synchronization in networks of oscillators,” *Chaos* 26 (2016), 094821](https://doi.org/10.1063/1.4961065), develop external equitable partitions and quotient graphs as invariant cluster-synchrony reductions. In those normalized quotient coordinates, the α/|B| coefficient is the ordinary componentwise-cubic scaling of a block-constant vector; it should not be presented as a new synchrony law. The distinction is the inverse statement: with only the port's controlled I/O, recover the minimal cubic quotient and use its coefficient normalization plus irreducible-M-matrix positivity to infer the number of underlying physical nodes.

**Gate decision:** this is a plausible conditional *node-count identifiability corollary* of s43, and the cited network-size literature does not directly subsume the zero-initial one-port, synchronized-hidden-mode case. The arithmetic lift is not independently novel; priority remains **UNKNOWN** pending a targeted search for quotient-parameter-based multiplicity recovery. It does not strengthen the oscillator H3 finite-band acquisition claim or certify practical inverse design.
