Abstract.
    

In this article we develop new methods for exhibiting convex semialgebraic sets that are not spectrahedral shadows. We characterize when the set of nonnegative polynomials with a given support is a spectrahedral shadow in terms of sums of squares. As an application of this result we prove that the cone of copositive matrices of size n≥5n\geq 5 is not a spectrahedral shadow, answering a question of Scheiderer. Our arguments are based on the model theoretic observation that any formula defining a spectrahedral shadow must be preserved by every unital ℝ{\mathbb{R}}-linear completely positive map R→RR\to R on a real closed field extension RR of ℝ{\mathbb{R}}.

1. Introduction




A set S⊆ℝnS\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n} is called a
spectrahedron
if




S={(x1,…,xn)∣A0+A1​x1+⋯+An​xn⪰0}S=\{(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})\mid A_{0}+A_{1}x_{1}+\dots+A_{n}x_{n}\succeq 0\}



for some k×kk\times k real symmetric matrices A0,A1,…,AnA_{0},A_{1},\dots,A_{n}. Here A⪰0A\succeq 0 says that the real symmetric matrix AA is positive semidefinite, and the expression
A0+A1​x1+⋯+An​xn⪰0A_{0}+A_{1}x_{1}+\cdots+A_{n}x_{n}\succeq 0 is called a linear matrix inequality (LMI) of size kk.
Note that spectrahedra are convex and semialgebraic, i.e.,
definable over ℝ{\mathbb{R}} by a first-order formula in the language of real-closed fields (i.e., the language of ordered rings).




There has been ample interest in representing convex semialgebraic sets K⊆ℝnK\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n} as a spectrahedral shadow. This amounts to finding real symmetric matrices A0,…,An,B1,…,BmA_{0},\ldots,A_{n},B_{1},\ldots,B_{m} of the same size such that




K={x∈ℝn∣∃y∈ℝm:A0+x1A1+⋯+xnAn+y1B1+⋯+ymBm⪰0}.K=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\mid\exists y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\colon A_{0}+x_{1}A_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}A_{n}+y_{1}B_{1}+\cdots{+}y_{m}B_{m}\succeq 0\}.



The interest stems primarily from the fact that there are efficient methods for approximately optimizing a linear function over such a set.
It was shown by Helton and Nie [11] that every convex semialgebraic set satisfying certain smoothness and curvature conditions is a spectrahedral shadow. Moreover, Scheiderer proved that every convex semialgebraic set in the plane is a spectrahedral shadow [30]. On the other hand, not every convex semialgebraic set is a spectrahedral shadow. The first counterexamples were constructed by Scheiderer [31]. Further examples were found in [8, 4], both using similar techniques as Scheiderer in his seminal work.




Recent efforts also focus on finding a representation as spectrahedral shadow with several LMIs of a bounded size.
For instance Averkov [2] showed that the cone of k×kk\times k real symmetric positive semidefinite matrices has no semidefinite extended representation
with LMIs of size less than kk (for a formal definition, see Section 2).
A generalization of this result in terms of convex geometry was given by Saunderson [29]. On the positive side, Scheiderer [32] proved that for every closed convex semialgebraic set K⊆ℝ2K\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{2} LMIs of size two are sufficient.




Our approach to these questions is based on an easy consequence of the Tarski principle for real closed fields. The question whether a given semialgebraic set K⊆ℝnK\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n} is a spectrahedral shadow is equivalent to the question whether KK has a primitive positive definition over the structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} with domain ℝ\mathbb{R} and with a relation S⊆ℝnS\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n} for every spectrahedron SS.
By the Tarski principle for real closed fields, we may
equivalently study this question over real-closed field extensions RR of ℝ\mathbb{R} instead of ℝ\mathbb{R}.
More precisely, if ϕ\phi is a formula in the language of real closed fields that defines KK,
let K∗K^{*} be the set defined by ϕ\phi over RR. Likewise,
let 𝔄∗{\mathfrak{A}}^{*} denote
the structure with domain RR which contains for every formula ψ\psi that defines a spectrahedron over ℝ{\mathbb{R}}
the relation defined by ψ\psi over RR.
Then KK has a primitive positive definition in 𝔄{\mathfrak{A}} if and only if K∗K^{*} has a primitive positive definition in 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*}.
A map h:R→Rh\colon R\to R preserves a relation K⊆RnK\subseteq R^{n}
if (h⁡(a1),…,h⁡(an))∈K(h(a_{1}),\dots,h(a_{n}))\in K whenever (a1,…,an)∈K(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in K.
We use the easy fact that K∗K^{*} does not have an existential positive definition in 𝔄∗{\mathfrak{A}}^{*} (and hence in particular no primitive positive definition) if
there exists a map h:R→Rh\colon R\to R that preserves all the relations of 𝔄∗{\mathfrak{A}}^{*} but not K∗K^{*}. In Appendix B we discuss this approach in more detail and prove a converse of the last statement.




We show that a map L:R→RL\colon R\to R preserves 𝔄∗{\mathfrak{A}}^{*} if and only if it is unital, ℝ{\mathbb{R}}-linear, and completely positive in the sense of Operator theory. Thus, in order to prove that KK is not a spectrahedral shadow, it suffices to exhibit a unital, ℝ{\mathbb{R}}-linear, and completely positive map L:R→RL\colon R\to R on a real closed field extension ℝ⊆R{\mathbb{R}}\subseteq R that does not preserve the set defined by ϕ\phi.
In this way we prove that the set of copositive matrices of size nn is not a spectrahedral shadow whenever n≥5n\geq 5, a family of convex semialgebraic sets that resisted Scheiderer’s efforts, see [31, Question 5.2].
A real symmetric matrix AA is copositive if x⊤​A​x≥0x^{\top}Ax\geq 0 for every non-negative real vector xx.
Note that for n≤4n\leq 4 it follows from the main result in [7] that this set is a spectrahedral shadow.




Note that convexity is not only preserved by conjunction (i.e., intersection) and existential quantification (i.e., projections), but also by universal quantification.
In the light of the results mentioned above one may ask whether every convex semi-algebraic set has a definition using a formula from positive first-order logic over the structure formed by all spectrahedra. Positive first-order logic is the extension of existential positive logic by universal quantification (so only negation from first-order logic is prohibited).
Our proof shows that the considered convex semialgebraic sets are not definable from spectrahedra even in this more expressive logic. The reason is that the map L:R→RL\colon R\to R that we construct is surjective.
Hence, we may combine the Tarski principle with the easy direction of Lyndon’s preservation theorem (see, e.g., Corollary 10.3.5 in [13])
to conclude that for example the set of copositive matrices does not have a definition from spectrahedra in positive first-order logic.




Apart from the Tarski principle the only ingredient to our proof is a separation theorem (Theorem 3.7) that follows from the results in [21]. We will give a self-contained proof in Appendix A.
In particular, we do not make use of the theory developed in [31]. Our result on copositive matrices is a consequence of the following more general result:



Theorem (see Theorem 3.15 below).



Let S⊂ℤ≥0nS\subset{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}^{n} a finite set and let P+⁡(S)\operatorname{P}_{+}(S) the set of all nonnegative polynomials
with coefficients from ℝ{\mathbb{R}}
whose support is contained in SS. Then the following are equivalent:


(1) 



P+⁡(S)\operatorname{P}_{+}(S) is a spectrahedral shadow.


(2) 



There exists an integer d>0d>0 such that p⁡(x1d,…,xnd)p(x_{1}^{d},\ldots,x_{n}^{d}) is a sum of squares for all p∈P+⁡(S)p\in\operatorname{P}_{+}(S).







Finally, in Section 4 we reprove Averkov’s above mentioned result in a similar fashion. Here we use results about positive definite maps [15].




We now give a brief outline of our proof strategy. We first observe in Section 2 that a map L:R→RL\colon R\to R for some real closed field extension RR of ℝ{\mathbb{R}} preserves 𝔄∗{\mathfrak{A}}^{*} if and only if LL is unital, ℝ{\mathbb{R}}-linear, and completely positive in the sense that applying LL entry-wise to a positive semidefinite symmetric matrix over RR gives a positive semidefinite matrix. When R=R′​[[ϵΛ]]R=R^{\prime}[[\epsilon^{\Lambda}]] is the field of Hahn series with value group Λ\Lambda and coefficients in another real closed field R′R^{\prime}, we consider maps Lf:R→RL_{f}\colon R\to R of the form Lf​(∑a∈Λca​ϵa)=∑a∈Λf⁡(a)​ca​ϵaL_{f}\left(\sum_{a\in\Lambda}c_{a}\epsilon^{a}\right)=\sum_{a\in\Lambda}f(a)c_{a}\epsilon^{a} for functions f:Λ→R′f\colon\Lambda\to R^{\prime}. On the other hand, extending ff linearly, we obtain a linear functional Tf:R′​[Λ]→R′T_{f}\colon R^{\prime}[\Lambda]\to R^{\prime} on the group ring of Λ\Lambda. The crucial insight of Section 2 is that LfL_{f} is completely positive whenever TfT_{f} is positive on every nonzero square in R′​[Λ]R^{\prime}[\Lambda]. In Section 3 we focus on the case Λ=ℚn\Lambda={\mathbb{Q}}^{n}. Elements of the group ring R′​[ℚn]R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}] can be regarded as functions on (R>0′)n(R^{\prime}_{>0})^{n}. If such a function takes only nonnegative values but is not a sum of squares in R′​[ℚn]R^{\prime}[{\mathbb{Q}}^{n}], then it can be separated by a linear functional TfT_{f} from the cone of sums of squares after a base change to a larger real closed field by Theorem 3.7. The corresponding map LfL_{f} is then completely positive but does not preserve a certain convex cone of nonnegative polynomials. Applying this construction to the so-called Horn polynomial proves that the cone of copositive matrices of size n≥5n\geq 5 is not a spectrahedral shadow.

2. kk-positive maps




In this section let RR always be a real closed field extension of ℝ{\mathbb{R}}.
Let Γk\Gamma_{k} be the set of all first-order formulas in the language of real closed fields with parameters from ℝ{\mathbb{R}} that express
a linear matrix inequality of size kk with coefficients from ℝ{\mathbb{R}},
and let Γ=∪k=1∞Γk\Gamma=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\Gamma_{k}. Let 𝔄k{\mathfrak{A}}_{k} be the relational structure with domain ℝ{\mathbb{R}} whose relations consist of the sets defined by the formulas in Γk\Gamma_{k}. We follow standard terminology in model theory, see, e.g., [13, 33]. For the convenience of the reader, we have collected the relevant concepts in Appendix B.1. The signature of 𝔄k\mathfrak{A}_{k} should not be confused with the signature of real closed fields. If a set KK has a primitive positive definition over 𝔄k{\mathfrak{A}}_{k} then we say that KK has a semidefinite extended formulation with LMIs of size kk, following the terminology of Averkov [2].




We say that a map h:R→Rh\colon R\to R preserves a formula in the language of real closed fields if it preserves the relation defined by the formula over RR.
The map hh is called unital if it preserves the formula x=1x=1, and for R′⊆RR^{\prime}\subseteq R it is called R′R^{\prime}-linear if it preserves the formula z=λ⋅x+μ⋅yz=\lambda\cdot x+\mu\cdot y for all λ,μ∈R′\lambda,\mu\in R^{\prime}.



Definition 2.1.



Let k∈ℤ>0k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{>0}. A map L:R→RL\colon R\to R is called kk-positive if for every k×kk\times k symmetric positive semidefinite matrix A=(ai​j)i,jA=(a_{ij})_{i,j} with ai​j∈Ra_{ij}\in R the matrix L⁡(A):=(L⁡(ai​j))i,jL(A):=(L(a_{ij}))_{i,j} is positive semidefinite as well. For technical reasons, we declare every map L:R→RL\colon R\to R to be 00-positive. If LL is kk-positive for every kk, then, following the nomenclature of operator theory (see, e.g., [6, §1.5]), we say that LL is completely positive.

Note that if L:R→RL\colon R\to R is kk-positive, then it is also k′k^{\prime}-positive for all k′∈{0,…,k}k^{\prime}\in\{0,\ldots,k\} since principal submatrices of positive semidefinite matrices are again positive semidefinite.

Appendix B Preservation Theorems




The approach to study definable sets via structure-preserving maps can be used to obtain exact characterisations of existential positive, positive, and first-order definability.
There are various formulations of such preservation theorems. Our formulation in Theorem B.1 (in Section B.2)
is closest to the setting of this article and we have been unable to find it in the literature, but it should be considered to be known.




B.1. Basic Terminology




A signature τ\tau is a set of function symbols and relation symbols. Each symbol is equipped with an arity k∈ℕk\in{\mathbb{N}}. A τ\tau-structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} consists of a set AA (the domain) and


•

• 



for each relation symbol R∈τR\in\tau of arity kk a relation R⊆AkR\subseteq A^{k}.



Note that the arity might in particular be 00; function symbols of arity 00 are also called constants.
We denote structures by 𝔄\mathfrak{A}, 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, ℭ\mathfrak{C}, and so on, and the corresponding underlying sets by the corresponding
roman letters AA, BB, CC, and so on.
If 𝔄\mathfrak{A} and 𝔅\mathfrak{B} are structures with the same domain and 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is obtained from 𝔅\mathfrak{B} by dropping some of the relations and operations, then 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is called a reduct of 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, and 𝔅\mathfrak{B} is called an expansion of 𝔄\mathfrak{A}.




In the usual way we define τ\tau-terms inductively; constant symbols from τ\tau are τ\tau-terms, variables are τ\tau-terms, and a function symbol of arity kk applied to a sequence of τ\tau-terms of length kk is a τ\tau-term. If tt is a τ\tau-term, we write t⁡(x1,…,xn)t(x_{1},\dots,x_{n}) if all the variables that appear in tt are from x1,…,xnx_{1},\dots,x_{n}.

•

K∗K^{*} is preserved by all endomorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*};


• 



KK has a positive definition in 𝔅\mathfrak{B} if and only if
K∗K^{*} is preserved by all surjective endomorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*};


• 



KK has a first-order definition in 𝔅\mathfrak{B} if and only if K∗K^{*} is preserved by all automorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}.



Here, 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} is the first-order reduct of 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} defined by the same formulas as the first-order reduct 𝔅\mathfrak{B} of 𝔄\mathfrak{A},
and K∗K^{*} is the relation defined over 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} by the same first-order formula that defines KK over 𝔄\mathfrak{A}.





We prove Theorem B.1 in Section B.4.



Remark B.2.



In our setting, the real-closed field ℝ{\mathbb{R}} plays the role of 𝔄\mathfrak{A}.
For the results in Section 3 we then
work with the first-order reduct
𝔅\mathfrak{B} of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} that contains all spectrahedra as relations, to show that specific relations KK with a first-order definition in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} do not have a positive definition in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}.
In Section 4 we work with the first-order reduct 𝔅\mathfrak{B} of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} that contains all relations that are defined by LMIs of size kk to show that some specific relations KK with a first-order definition in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} do not have a positive definition in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}. In both settings, Lemma 2.3 is a statement about the endomorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}.





The elementary extension 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} in Theorem B.1 will be a so-called special model (see, e.g., [13]). In the context of preservation theorems often saturated structures are used.
Special structures are more technical to define than saturated structures. However, the existence of saturated structures requires the generalised continuum hypothesis which we would like to avoid.





B.3. Saturated and Special Structures




For n≥0n\geq 0, an
nn-type of a theory TT is a set pp
of formulas with free variables x1,…,xnx_{1},\dots,x_{n} such that p∪Tp\cup T
is satisfiable.
An nn-type over a structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is an nn-type of the first-order theory of 𝔄{\mathfrak{A}};
note that we do not admit parameters in these formulas (i.e., we allow constants to appear in the formulas of the type only if they belong to the signature).
An nn-type pp of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is realised in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} if there exist a1,…,an∈Aa_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in A such that 𝔄⊧ϕ⁡(a1,…,an)\mathfrak{A}\models\phi(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}) for each ϕ∈p\phi\in p.
For an infinite cardinal κ\kappa, a structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is κ\kappa-saturated if,
for all β<κ\beta<\kappa and expansions 𝔄′\mathfrak{A}^{\prime} of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} by at most β\beta constants, every 11-type of 𝔄′\mathfrak{A}^{\prime} is realised
in 𝔄′\mathfrak{A}^{\prime}. We say that an infinite structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is saturated if it is |A||A|-saturated.
If λ\lambda is a cardinal, then λ+\lambda^{+} denotes the successor cardinal of λ\lambda.



Theorem B.3 (Theorem 4.3.12 in [16]).



Let τ\tau be a signature and λ≥|τ|\lambda\geq|\tau|.
Then every τ\tau-structure 𝔅\mathfrak{B} has a λ+\lambda^{+}-saturated
elementary extension of cardinality ≤|B|λ\leq|B|^{\lambda}.





So if we assume the Generalised
Continuum Hypothesis, then there
are saturated models of size κ+\kappa^{+} for all cardinals κ\kappa (Corollary 10.2.4 in [13]).



Definition B.4.



A structure 𝔄\mathfrak{A} of cardinality κ≥ω\kappa\geq\omega is
special if 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is the union of an elementary chain (𝔅λ)λ<κ(\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\kappa} and each 𝔅λ\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda} is λ+\lambda^{+}-saturated.





Saturated structures are special, because we can write a saturated structure
𝔄\mathfrak{A} of cardinality κ\kappa
as (𝔅λ)λ<κ(\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda})_{\lambda<\kappa} where
𝔅λ=𝔄\mathfrak{B}_{\lambda}=\mathfrak{A}; since 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is λ+\lambda^{+}-saturated for all λ<κ\lambda<\kappa, this proves that 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is special. The converse is not true; see [13, Exercises 6 to 8 on page 514]. The advantage of special structures is that they always exist, independently from set-theoretic assumptions.



Theorem B.5 (Theorem 10.4.2. in [13]).



Every infinite structure has a special elementary extension.






B.4. Proof of the Preservation Theorems




The following is at the heart of the proof of Theorem B.1.



Theorem B.6.



Let 𝔄\mathfrak{A} and 𝔅\mathfrak{B} be special structures of the same signature and cardinality.


• 



If every first-order sentence that is true in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is true in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, then there exists an isomorphism between 𝔄\mathfrak{A} and 𝔅\mathfrak{B}.


• 



If every positive sentence that is true in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is true in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, then there exists a surjective homomorphism from 𝔄\mathfrak{A} to 𝔅\mathfrak{B}.


• 



If every existential positive sentence that is true in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is true in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, then there exists a homomorphism from 𝔄\mathfrak{A} to 𝔅\mathfrak{B}.






Proof.



The first statement can be shown by a back and forth argument, and is Theorem 10.4.4. in [13].
The other two statements can be shown analogously.
∎




Lemma B.7 (see Theorem 10.4.5. in [13]).



First-order reducts of special structures are special.




Proof of Theorem B.1.



In each of the three statements, the forward direction is immediate, since
homomorphisms preserve existential positive formulas, surjective homomorphisms preserve positive formulas, and automorphisms preserve first-order formulas. Moreover, if KK has a certain definition in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}, then K∗K^{*} has the corresponding definition in 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} since all the involved structures and sets are first-order definable in 𝔄\mathfrak{A} and 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*}, respectively, and 𝔄\mathfrak{A} and 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} have the same first-order theory.




The three statements are well-known if 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is finite (see, e.g., [25]) so let us assume that 𝔄\mathfrak{A} is infinite.
Let 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} be a special elementary extension of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} which exists by Theorem B.5,
and let SS be the first-order theory of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} (and 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*}).
Let 𝔅\mathfrak{B} be a first-order reduct of 𝔄\mathfrak{A} with signature τ\tau and let K⊆AkK\subseteq A^{k} be first-order definable over 𝔄\mathfrak{A}.
Let 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} be the reduct of 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} and K∗⊆(A∗)kK^{*}\subseteq(A^{*})^{k} be as in the statement of the theorem. By
Lemma B.7 we have that 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} is special as well.




To show the converse of the first statement, suppose that K∗K^{*} is preserved by all endomorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}.
Let Ψ\Psi be the set of all existential positive
τ\tau-formulas ψ⁡(x1,…,xk)\psi(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
that hold on all tuples from K∗K^{*} in 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}.
Let a¯\underline{a} be a tuple satisfying
Ψ\Psi in 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}. Note that if there is no such tuple,
then ϕ\phi must be unsatisfiable in 𝔅\mathfrak{B}. In this case, ϕ\phi is equivalent to the existential positive formula ⊥\bot and we are done.




Let UU be the set of all primitive positive τ\tau-formulas θ⁡(x¯)\theta(\underline{x}) such that 𝔅∗⊧¬θ⁡(a¯)\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\neg\theta(\bar{a}). For any first-order τ\tau-formula η\eta
let T⁡(η)T(\eta) be the first-order formula
obtained from η\eta by replacing each atomic subformula of η\eta by its first-order definition over 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*}.
We claim that




S∪{T⁡(¬θ)∣θ∈U}∪{ϕ}S\cup\{T(\neg\theta)\mid\theta\in U\}\cup\{\phi\}



is satisfiable. Otherwise, by the compactness theorem of first-order logic there would be a finite subset VV of UU such that S∪{T⁡(¬θ)∣θ∈V}∪{ϕ}S\cup\{T(\neg\theta)\mid\theta\in V\}\cup\{\phi\} is unsatisfiable.
Since ¬T⁡(θ)=T⁡(¬θ)\neg T(\theta)=T(\neg\theta)
we get that S∪{ϕ}S\cup\{\phi\} implies ⋁θ∈VT⁡(θ)\bigvee_{\theta\in V}T(\theta).
Thus, ψ:=⋁θ∈Vθ\psi:=\bigvee_{\theta\in V}\theta is an existential positive formula such that S∪{ϕ}⊧T⁡(ψ)S\cup\{\phi\}\models T(\psi).
But then ψ∈Ψ\psi\in\Psi and 𝔅∗⊧ψ⁡(a¯)\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\psi(\underline{a}).
This is in contradiction to the assumption that 𝔅∗⊧¬θ⁡(a¯)\mathfrak{B}^{*}\models\neg\theta(\underline{a}) for all θ∈U\theta\in U.




Since 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} is a special model of SS, the nn-type
{T⁡(¬θ⁡(x¯))∣θ∈U}∪{ϕ⁡(x¯)}\{T(\neg\theta(\bar{x}))\mid\theta\in U\}\cup\{\phi(\bar{x})\} is realised by some b∈(A∗)kb\in(A^{*})^{k} in 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*}.
Let c1,…,ckc_{1},\dots,c_{k} be new constant symbols
and let ℭ\mathfrak{C} be the τ∪{c1,…,ck}\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}-expansion of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} where c¯ℭ=b¯\underline{c}^{\mathfrak{C}}=\underline{b}.
Let 𝔇\mathfrak{D} be the τ∪{c1,…,ck}\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\}-expansion of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*} where c¯ℭ=a¯\underline{c}^{\mathfrak{C}}=\underline{a}.
Clearly, ℭ\mathfrak{C} and 𝔇\mathfrak{D} are special as well.
Every primitive positive (τ∪{c1,…,ck})(\tau\cup\{c_{1},\dots,c_{k}\})-sentence θ\theta
that is true in 𝔇\mathfrak{D} is also
true in ℭ\mathfrak{C}: otherwise, if θ\theta were false in ℭ\mathfrak{C}, then write θ\theta as θ⁡(c¯)\theta(\underline{c}) for θ∈U\theta\in U,
in contradiction to the assumption that 𝔇⊧{¬θ⁡(c¯)|θ∈U}\mathfrak{D}\models\{\neg\theta(\underline{c})\;|\;\theta\in U\}.
Hence, since ℭ\mathfrak{C} is special and by Theorem B.6,
there exists a homomorphism from 𝔇\mathfrak{D} to
ℭ\mathfrak{C}. This homomorphism maps b¯\underline{b} to a¯\underline{a}.
Since b¯\underline{b} satisfies ϕ\phi in 𝔄∗\mathfrak{A}^{*} we have that
b¯∈K∗\underline{b}\in K^{*}, and since K∗K^{*} is preserved by endomorphisms of 𝔅∗\mathfrak{B}^{*}, we have a¯∈K∗\underline{a}\in K^{*}.
We conclude that S∪{T⁡(θ)∣θ∈Ψ}∪{¬ϕ}S\cup\{T(\theta)\mid\theta\in\Psi\}\cup\{\neg\phi\} is unsatisfiable, and again by compactness there exists a finite subset Ψ′\Psi^{\prime} of
Ψ\Psi such that S∪{T⁡(θ)∣θ∈Ψ′}∪{¬ϕ}S\cup\{T(\theta)\mid\theta\in\Psi^{\prime}\}\cup\{\neg\phi\} is unsatisfiable. Note that ψ:=⋀Ψ′\psi:=\bigwedge\Psi^{\prime} is
an existential positive formula
and S⊧∀x¯​(T​(ψ)⇔ϕ)S\models\forall\underline{x}(T(\psi)\Leftrightarrow\phi)
which is what we wanted to show.




The converse directions for statement two and three of the theorem can be shown analogously.
∎
