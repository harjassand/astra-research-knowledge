# N18 | Strange-metal transport without a quasiparticle clock

Date: 2026-10-09. Status: internally derived exact identities within the stated slow-mode hypothesis; established formulae, not a novelty or priority claim. Microscopic origin of the observed linear resistivity remains unresolved.

## Exact target

Determine what translation conservation, locality, spectral positivity, and optical sum rules fix about transport relaxation when quasiparticles are absent; then seek a mechanism that makes a linear-in-T resistivity and optical/thermal signatures without assuming \(\hbar/\tau\simeq k_B T\).

The useful result is a transport-matrix test for whether one slow mode carries both charge and heat. It is exact in the one-slow-mode projection and does not require quasiparticles or a Drude shape. A weak-lattice critical-operator calculation gives a known route to a linear rate, but does not fix its coefficient. The combination does not yet amount to a new microscopic explanation of cuprate strange metals.

## 1. What the exact constraints say

Use \(\hbar=k_B=1\) in derivations. In a passive equilibrium state, \(\sigma_1(\omega)\geq0\) for \(\omega>0\). The optical f-sum fixes the integrated weight, not the dc height or a line width. For a lattice Hamiltonian,

\[
W_x(T):=\frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^\infty d\omega\,\sigma_{1,xx}(\omega,T)
 = \mathcal D_{\rm dia}(T),
\]

where \(\mathcal D_{\rm dia}\) is the expectation of the diamagnetic kinetic-stress operator in the chosen convention. In a continuum Galilean-invariant system it is \(ne^2/m\). The lattice right-hand side can vary with temperature and is not generally the weight of a narrow coherent peak.

For example, the causal response \(\sigma(\omega)=D/(\Gamma-i\omega)\) has \(\int_0^\infty\sigma_1d\omega=\pi D/2\) for every \(\Gamma>0\), while \(\sigma_{dc}=D/\Gamma\). Thus the f-sum alone does not set \(\sigma_{dc}\) or \(\Gamma\). Locality constrains high-frequency moments through nested commutators with the local Hamiltonian; a finite number of such moments still does not identify a unique low-frequency line shape. Positivity and Kramers–Kronig causality do not imply a one-pole spectrum.

Translation conservation has one sharper consequence. If total momentum \(P_x\) is exactly conserved and \(\chi_{J_xP_x}\ne0\), the conductivity has a zero-frequency delta contribution and the clean dc resistivity vanishes. If \(\chi_{J_xP_x}=0\), as can happen at charge neutrality, the current can instead have finite incoherent conductivity despite conserved momentum. A single-particle decay rate, even if it is proportional to \(T\), is not automatically the transport relaxation rate.

## 2. Exact one-slow-mode electrical and thermal identity

Let the slow sector contain only momentum \(P_x\). Define the symmetric Onsager matrix using currents \(j_1=J_x\), \(j_2=Q_x/T\), with conjugate forces \(E_x\), \(-\nabla_xT\):

\[
L(\omega)=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma(\omega)&\alpha(\omega)\\
\alpha(\omega)&\bar\kappa(\omega)/T\end{pmatrix}.
\]

At zero field, the projected memory-matrix formula has the form

\[
L_{ab}(\omega)=R_{ab}(\omega)+
\frac{c_a c_b}{K(\omega)},\qquad
K(\omega)=M_{PP}(\omega)-i\omega\chi_{PP},
\]

where \(R\) is the response after projecting out \(P_x\), \(c_1=\chi_{J_xP_x}\), and \(c_2=\chi_{Q_xP_x}/T\). This is the exact one-mode block of the memory-matrix representation; treating \(R\) and \(M\) as smooth/constant at low frequency is an additional hydrodynamic approximation. In a relativistic fluid, or the corresponding normal-fluid hydrodynamic limit, \(c_1=n\) and \(c_2=s\) (charge and entropy densities); on a lattice the susceptibilities must be measured/calculated and need not equal those simple densities.

For any symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix \(R\), direct expansion gives

\[
\det L=\det R+\frac{c_1^2R_{22}+c_2^2R_{11}-2c_1c_2R_{12}}{K}.
\tag{1}
\]

The apparent double pole cancels exactly. If the slow-mode term dominates the electrical dc conductivity, \(K(0)\to0\) and \(c_1\ne0\), then

\[
\frac{\alpha}{\sigma}\longrightarrow r:=\frac{c_2}{c_1},\qquad
\frac{\kappa}{T}=\frac{\bar\kappa}{T}-\frac{\alpha^2}{\sigma}
\longrightarrow R_{22}-2rR_{12}+r^2R_{11}.
\tag{2}
\]

Equation (2) is the Schur complement: imposing zero electrical current projects out the shared convective/momentum channel. Thus a single common slow mode can dominate \(\sigma\), \(\alpha\), and \(\bar\kappa\) while leaving open-circuit heat transport controlled by the projected, incoherent channel. In the relativistic special case, \(\alpha/\sigma\to s/n\); this ratio is not asserted for a general lattice metal.

The optical version is stronger than comparing dc slopes. If \(R(\omega)\) is regular and one pole dominates, the singular residue matrix is \(c_ac_b/K(\omega)\), which has rank one at every frequency. Its charge/heat residue ratio is fixed by \(c_2/c_1\), independent of the pole width. A second independent slow mode with a non-collinear current-overlap vector can produce a rank-two singular matrix. Optical thermoelectric spectroscopy can therefore test whether electrical and thermal transport share one bottleneck without assigning a quasiparticle lifetime.

## 3. What a linear resistivity determines in this limit

At zero frequency, if \(R_{11}\) is subleading and \(c_1\ne0\),

\[
\rho_{dc}\simeq\frac{M_{PP}(0)}{\chi_{J_xP_x}^2}.
\tag{3}
\]

If \(M_{PP}=\chi_{PP}\Gamma\), define the coherent optical weight \(D_{\rm coh}=\chi_{J_xP_x}^2/\chi_{PP}\). Then

\[
\rho_{dc}\simeq\frac{\Gamma}{D_{\rm coh}},\qquad
\Gamma\simeq D_{\rm coh}\rho_{dc}.
\tag{4}
\]

Restoring units, a Planckian test is \(\hbar D_{\rm coh}\rho_{dc}/(k_BT)\) constant and of order one. Equation (4) is meaningful only when a single momentum pole dominates dc transport and \(D_{\rm coh}\) is the pole's optical area in the same units. Substituting the *total* f-sum for \(D_{\rm coh}\) is unjustified if spectral weight remains in an incoherent continuum or mid-infrared band. The f-sum-calibrated scale \(W_x/\sigma_{dc}\) is always an operational frequency, but is a physical decay rate only for a single-pole line shape exhausting the relevant weight.

The multi-rate counterexample is explicit. For

\[
\sigma(\omega)=\sum_i\frac{D_i}{\Gamma_i-i\omega},\quad D_i>0,
\]

the f-sum weight is \(\sum_iD_i\), while \(\sigma_{dc}=\sum_iD_i/\Gamma_i\). Hence \(W/\sigma_{dc}\) is the \(D_i\)-weighted harmonic mean of the rates, not a unique microscopic rate. With two fixed rates \(\Gamma_s=1\), \(\Gamma_f=4\), total weight \(D_s+D_f=1\), and \(D_s(T)=(1/(1.5T)-0.25)/0.75\), for \(1\leq T\leq2\) both weights remain nonnegative and \(\sigma_{dc}=1/(1.5T)\), i.e. \(\rho=1.5T\), although neither width changes with \(T\). This is a positive causal spectral counterexample with fixed total weight. It is not a claimed equilibrium local-Hamiltonian realization; it establishes that positivity and the f-sum do not by themselves identify the rate. A local microscopic explanation would have to explain the temperature-dependent redistribution of the weights.

## 4. A conditional microscopic route: weak-lattice critical umklapp

Take a translation-invariant interacting metal \(H_*\) and add a weak periodic perturbation

\[
H=H_*+g\int d^dx\,\cos(k_Lx)\,O(x).
\]

The force on momentum is \(\dot P_x\propto gk_L\int d^dx\,\sin(k_Lx)O(x)\). To leading order \(g^2\), the memory matrix gives, up to volume/Fourier conventions,

\[
M_{PP}(0)\propto g^2 k_L^2\lim_{\omega\to0}
\frac{\operatorname{Im}G^R_{OO}(k_L,\omega;T)}{\omega}.
\tag{5}
\]

This gives a microscopic, quasiparticle-free route: linear resistivity follows if the finite-momentum critical spectral slope in (5), multiplied by the static overlap factor in (3), scales as \(T\). It does not follow from locality or positivity alone. For the locally critical umklapp case analyzed by Hartnoll and Hofman, the memory-matrix result is \(\Gamma\sim T^{2\Delta(k_L)}\); choosing \(\Delta(k_L)=1/2\) yields a linear rate. This is a known scaling mechanism, not a new derivation of cuprate transport, and the dimensionless coefficient still depends on \(g\), spectral normalization, and \(\chi_{PP}\).

For random translation breaking \(h(x)O(x)\), the leading expression instead integrates \(q_x^2\overline{|h(q)|^2}\operatorname{Im}G^R_{OO}(q,\omega)/\omega\) over momentum. The momentum distribution of disorder and the critical dimension/spectral weight at each transferred momentum can change the temperature exponent and coefficient. Setting the disorder to zero restores the conserved-momentum delta peak when \(c_1\ne0\). A universal Planckian coefficient cannot be inferred without controlling these perturbations and thermodynamic overlaps.

## 5. Sum-rule consequence of optical scaling

Suppose the regular low-frequency spectrum has a one-variable scaling window

\[
\sigma_1(\omega,T)=T^{-a}\Phi(\omega/T),\quad 0\leq\omega\leq cT,
\]

with \(\Phi(x)\geq0\), \(\Phi(0)>0\), and \(C_c=\int_0^c\Phi(x)dx>0\). Then the window's partial optical weight is

\[
W_c(T)=C_cT^{1-a}\leq W_x(T).
\]

If \(W_x(T)\) stays bounded as \(T\to0\) and the scaling window persists to zero temperature, necessarily \(a\leq1\). In the linear-resistivity case \(a=1\), the scaled low-energy window carries constant integrated weight. This is a sum-rule constraint on a scaling ansatz, not proof that a microscopic relaxation event lasts \(1/T\). A curvature-defined optical width,

\[
\Gamma_{\rm curv}(T)=\left[-\frac{1}{2}\partial_\omega^2\ln\sigma_1(\omega,T)\big|_{\omega=0}\right]^{-1/2},
\]

is \(T[-\tfrac12\partial_x^2\ln\Phi(0)]^{-1/2}\) when that derivative is finite and negative. This remains meaningful for non-Lorentzian scaling curves, but is a shape width, not a microscopic lifetime. If scaling is only valid over a finite experimental window, the exponent restriction applies only as long as that window follows \(cT\) and the total sum is bounded.

## 7. Counterexamples, limits, and next decisive tests

* **No momentum relaxation:** if \(\chi_{JP}\ne0\), exact translation conservation gives a delta function and zero dc resistivity. A finite quasiparticle or single-particle width alone does not evade this conservation law.
* **No current overlap:** if \(\chi_{JP}=0\), the current is not protected by the momentum pole, so finite \(\sigma_{inc}\) does not measure momentum relaxation.
* **Changing overlap:** equation (3) contains \(\chi_{JP}^{-2}\). A doping dependence of \(\rho/T\) can come from current/momentum overlap even at fixed \(M_{PP}/T\). In a one-mode interpretation, the coherent optical area is the relevant overlap measure; the total f-sum need not equal it.
* **Several modes:** parallel Drude weights produce a harmonic combination of widths. A linear dc law can coexist with fixed widths if weights transfer with temperature, as the explicit spectral example shows. Conversely, a continuum of widths \(yT\) gives \(\sigma(\omega,T)=T^{-1}\int d\mu(y)D(y)y/[y^2+(\omega/T)^2]\), fixed total area, and linear \(\rho\) without a unique \(\tau\).
* **Disorder:** disorder changes the force spectrum and can add parallel/inhomogeneous channels. Matthiessen addition of a residual term is not automatic in a strongly interacting metal; controlled disorder dependence is needed to separate intrinsic and extrinsic slopes.
* **Strong momentum relaxation:** if no parametrically slow momentum or other small memory-matrix eigenvalue exists, the one-mode truncation is not controlled. The exact Kubo/f-sum statements remain, but the rate \(\Gamma=M/\chi\) in (4) loses its status as the observed optical width.

Decisive data would measure the complex electrical, thermoelectric, and thermal response in the same temperature/doping window; separate the central low-frequency weight from mid-infrared weight using full complex spectra and sum-rule accounting; and test whether the singular residue matrix is rank one with the susceptibility-predicted heat/charge ratio. Varying controlled disorder or lattice strength tests equation (5). If the rate is claimed Planckian, report \(\hbar D_{\rm coh}\rho/(k_BT)\) with the coherent area and its uncertainty, and also report the total optical sum so weight transfer is visible. A thermal response that retains the same leading pole after the open-circuit projection, or a non-rank-one residue, refutes the single-momentum bottleneck premise.

Still unresolved: derive the relevant finite-momentum critical spectrum and its coefficient from a realistic local cuprate Hamiltonian; show why a particular operator has the needed scaling dimension over the observed window; establish whether the 2026 fitted central optical weight equals a single slow-mode residue in the non-quasiparticle regime; quantify disorder and incoherent spectral corrections; and obtain a first-principles thermal/thermoelectric prediction. No simulation was used as empirical confirmation. No script was needed; the two-rate example is algebraic.

* **Source-reported:** the optical sum rule, momentum memory formula, and locally critical umklapp exponent are established source results listed above.
* **Candidate deduction:** bounded f-sum plus a persistent positive \(T^{-a}\Phi(\omega/T)\) window implies \(a\leq1\); linear-resistivity scaling implies constant low-energy partial area and a \(T\)-scaled curvature width.
