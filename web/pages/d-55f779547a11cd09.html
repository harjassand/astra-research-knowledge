Abstract
We give an explicit hitting set of size poly⁡(n,d,r)\operatorname{poly}(n,d,r) for the class of nn-variate degree-dd polynomials whose partial derivative space is bounded by rr, over any field 𝔽\mathbb{F} of characteristic zero. In particular, this yields a polynomial sized hitting set for the class of depth-33 powering circuits.
The main technical insight is the construction of a “formal derivation” and properties of the associated Wronskian with respect to this derivation, which was previously studied by Moura [24] in a very different context. The proofs in this paper are elementary and completely self-contained.
AI disclosure: The proof of this result was obtained during conversations [5] with OpenAI GPT-6 Astra. The proof presented in this writeup is a rewriting (in the authors’ words) of the proof obtained by the AI model in a form that we believe is understandable to researchers.
1 Introduction
Algebraic circuits are the most natural computational model for studying the complexity of multivariate polynomials via the number of basic operations (additions, multiplications) required to compute it. The field of algebraic complexity broadly deals with the classification of polynomials based on their computational complexity, and also on algorithmic questions regarding polynomials provided via such computations.
Two of the most important aspects of this study are the question of lower bounds (i.e., finding explicit polynomials that cannot be computed by small algebraic circuits), and the algorithmic task of polynomial identity testing (i.e., given a circuit CC, algorithmically check if CC is computing the zero polynomial). In the context of polynomial identity tests, there are two types of algorithms — whitebox PITs (where the algorithm is given the entire circuit description), or blackbox PITs (where we are only provided evaluation access to the circuit). Although the questions of proving lower bounds and constructing deterministic PITs appear to be of very different flavours, they are intimately connected to each other. Several classical results [18, 3, 19, 8, 16] show that a strong enough lower bound for circuits can be used to obtain efficient deterministic polynomial identity tests, and vice-versa. Although similar statements when specialised to restricted circuit classes are known in some contexts [9, 26, 8, 6], this has broadly been a guiding principle for the study of lower bound and PITs for subclasses of circuits. Historically, a polynomial identity test for a class 𝒞\mathscr{C} has been a successor to a lower bound for the class 𝒞\mathscr{C}, and is often heavily inspired by the techniques used in the lower bound proof. Paradoxically, even ‘natural’ “barriers” for proving algebraic circuit lower bounds stem from the conjectured existence of succinct polynomial identity tests [14, 11].
Over the recent years, there has been quite a lot of progress made towards proving lower bounds for natural subclasses of algebraic circuits, and also constructing whitebox and blackbox PITs for them [1, 17, 15, 22, 4, 13]. One subclass of circuits that has been just shy of a full resolution has been the class of depth-33 powering circuits.
Depth-33 powering circuits:
A depth-33 powering circuit computes a polynomial of the form f=c1​ℓ1d+⋯+cs​ℓsdf=c_{1}\ell_{1}^{d}+\cdots+c_{s}\ell_{s}^{d} where each ℓi\ell_{i} is a linear polynomial over the underlying variables. This is the circuit analogue of the classical notion of Waring rank, where the Waring rank of a polynomial ff is the smallest ss such that ff can be written as sum of ss powers of linear forms. Saxena [28] gave the first lower bound for this class of circuits, and also a polynomial time white-box PIT. A different white-box PIT was also given by Kayal [20]. Agrawal, Saha and Saxena [2], and Forbes and Shpilka [12] gave the first nO⁡(log⁡n)n^{O(\log n)}-size hitting set for this class (among constructions of quasi-polynomial sized hitting sets for other more general classes), and Forbes, Saptharishi and Shpilka [10] improved this to nO⁡(log⁡log⁡n)n^{O(\log\log n)}.
The key weakness of the above model that enabled all the lower bounds and PITs was the observation that any kk-th order partial derivative of f=c1​ℓ1d+⋯+cs​ℓsdf=c_{1}\ell_{1}^{d}+\cdots+c_{s}\ell_{s}^{d} is a linear combination of ℓ1d−k,…,ℓsd−k\ell_{1}^{d-k},\ldots,\ell_{s}^{d-k} and thus the space of all possible partial derivatives of ff (which we shall denote by ∂=⁣∗(f)\partial^{=*}(f)) is only O⁡(s​d)O(sd)-dimensional. The hitting sets constructed by [2, 10] also extend to the (potentially) more general class of polynomials ff whose space of all possible partial derivatives is low-dimensional. It continues to remain open if such polynomials with low-dimensional space of partial derivatives admit a not-too-large representation as a depth-33 powering circuit.
The main result of this paper is an explicit polynomial-sized hitting set for this class of polynomials over a characteristic zero field.
1.1.
Let 𝔽\mathbb{F} be a field of characteristic zero. For any n,r≥0n,r\geq 0, there is an explicit polynomial map (G1​(t),…,Gn​(t))∈𝔽​[t]n(G_{1}(t),\ldots,G_{n}(t))\in\mathbb{F}[t]^{n} with deg⁡Gi≤O⁡(n​r2)\deg G_{i}\leq O(nr^{2}) such that if f⁡(x1,…,xn)f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) is a polynomial such that dim∂∗f≤r\dim\partial^{*}f\leq r, then
f⁡(x1,…,xn)=0⇔f⁡(G1​(t),…,Gn​(t))=0.f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0\Leftrightarrow f(G_{1}(t),\ldots,G_{n}(t))=0.
In particular, the above yields a poly⁡(n,d,s)\operatorname{poly}(n,d,s)-sized hitting set for the class of size ss, degree dd depth-33 powering circuits. In fact, Gi​(t)G_{i}(t) can just be instantiated to be the truncated Taylor series of −ln⁡(1−i​t)-\ln(1-it):
Gi​(t)=i​t+(i​t)22+(i​t)33+⋯+(i​t)BBfor i=1,…,nG_{i}(t)=it+\frac{(it)^{2}}{2}+\frac{(it)^{3}}{3}+\cdots+\frac{(it)^{B}}{B}\quad\text{for $i=1,\ldots,n$}
where B=n​r2+rB=nr^{2}+r.
Using power series for constructing hitting-set generators:
The general structure of constructing a hitting-set generator via power series is reminiscent of the randomised PIT algorithms of Chen and Kow [7], or Lewin and Vadhan [21]. We describe the setting of [7] (the setting of [21] is very similar with irreducible polynomial playing the role of primes). They show that if f⁡(x1,…,xn)f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) is a nonzero polynomial, then
f⁡(±p1,1±⋯±p1,ℓ,⋯,±pn,1±⋯±pn,ℓ)≠0f(\pm\sqrt{p_{1,1}}\pm\cdots\pm\sqrt{p_{1,\ell}},\cdots,\pm\sqrt{p_{n,1}}\pm\cdots\pm\sqrt{p_{n,\ell}})\neq 0
where pi,jp_{i,j}’s are distinct primes with ℓ=O⁡(log⁡deg⁡(f))\ell=O(\log\deg(f)). However, an efficient cannot evaluate ff at these irrationals of infinite precision. [7] show that if the signs are randomly chosen, we can achieve an error of ε\varepsilon as long as we truncate the above irrationals to precision B⁡(ε)B(\varepsilon). This results in randomised algorithm for PIT where reducing the error is achieved by increasing the precision (and thereby the running time) but keeping the number of random bits fixed.
The PIT in this paper is similar in the sense that the generator is a set of algebraically independent power series (Pi(t):=−log(1−it):i∈[n])\left(P_{i}(t):=-\log(1-it)\;:\;i\in[n]\right). For the class of polynomials with low-dimensional partial derivative space, we show that
0≠f⟹f⁡(P1​(t),…,Pn​(t))≠0modtB0\neq f\implies f(P_{1}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t))\neq 0\bmod t^{B}
for a not-too-large BB.
Mrinal Kumar pointed out to us that f⁡(ez​t,ez2​t,…,ezn​t)≠0modtsf(e^{zt},e^{z^{2}t},\ldots,e^{z^{n}t})\neq 0\bmod t^{s} for any nonzero ss-sparse polynomial (thus giving a bivariate, low degree, hitting set generator for sparse polynomials). It would be interesting to see if this applies to a more general class of polynomials.
1.1 Proof overview
Suppose f⁡(x1,…,xn)f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) is a nonzero polynomial with low dimensional partial derivative space, and say g1,…,grg_{1},\ldots,g_{r} is a basis of ∂=⁣∗(f)\partial^{=*}(f) with gr=fg_{r}=f. Let us consider f⁡(P1​(t),…,Pn​(t))f(P_{1}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t)) for some univariates P1,…,PnP_{1},\ldots,P_{n}, and we wish to know if f⁡(P1​(t),…,Pn​(t))f(P_{1}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t)) can be divisible by tBt^{B} for a large BB. Then,
dd​t​(f∘P)=∑i=1nd​Pid​t⋅(∂xif)​(P1,…,Pn).\frac{d}{dt}(f\circ P)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{dP_{i}}{dt}\cdot(\partial_{x_{i}}f)(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}).
Inspired by the RHS above, let us define a “derivative operator” DPD_{P} given by
DP\displaystyle D_{P}
:𝔽⁡[t]​[x1,…,xn]→𝔽⁡[t]​[x1,…,xn]\displaystyle:\;\mathbb{F}[t][x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\rightarrow\mathbb{F}[t][x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
DP​(g⁡(t,x1,…,xn))\displaystyle D_{P}(g(t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))
=∂tg+(d​P1d​t)⋅∂x1g+⋯+(d​Pnd​t)⋅∂xng.\displaystyle=\partial_{t}g+\left(\frac{dP_{1}}{dt}\right)\cdot\partial_{x_{1}}g+\cdots+\left(\frac{dP_{n}}{dt}\right)\cdot\partial_{x_{n}}g.
Thus, if D:=DPD:=D_{P}, we have
did​ti​(f∘P)=Di​(f)∘P.\frac{d^{i}}{dt^{i}}(f\circ P)=D^{i}(f)\circ P.
Thus, if f∘Pf\circ P was divisible by tBt^{B}, then Di​f∘PD^{i}f\circ P is divisible by tB−it^{B-i}.
For any derivation DD, there is a well-studied notion of a DD-Wronskian of a set of polynomials g1,…,grg_{1},\ldots,g_{r} given by
WD​(g1,…,gr)=det[g1g2⋯grD⁡(g1)D⁡(g2)⋯D⁡(gr)⋱Dr−1​(g1)Dr−1​(g2)⋯Dr−1​(gr)].W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=\det\begin{bmatrix}g_{1}&g_{2}&\cdots&g_{r}\\
D(g_{1})&D(g_{2})&\cdots&D(g_{r})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
D^{r-1}(g_{1})&D^{r-1}(g_{2})&\cdots&D^{r-1}(g_{r})\end{bmatrix}.
The above is a polynomial in t,x1,…,xnt,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n} of not-too-large degree. The first observation is that, since the set {g1,…,gr}\left\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}\right\} is ∂x\partial_{x}-closed (i.e., any ∂xigj\partial_{x_{i}}g_{j} can be written as a linear combination of other elements in this set), one can argue that the above DD-Wronskian satisfies the property that ∂xiWD​(g1,…,gr)=0\partial_{x_{i}}W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=0 for all i∈[n]i\in[n]. In other words, the above is just a pure polynomial in tt and does not depend on x1,…,xnx_{1},\ldots,x_{n} at all! Thus, we can substitute xi↦Pix_{i}\mapsto P_{i} in the above matrix and that does not affect WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}).
Focusing on the last column of the matrix WD​(g1,…,gr)∘PW_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\circ P, since we assumed that f∘P=0modtBf\circ P=0\bmod t^{B}, we observe that every entry in the last column is divisible by tB−r+1t^{B-r+1}. Thus, in particular WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) must be divisible by tB−r+1t^{B-r+1}. However, we know that WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) is a polynomial in tt of suitably bounded degree (and composing by PP does not increase the degree at all since the polynomial did not depend on xx at all). If BB is set to be sufficiently large, we have our contradiction.
Unless of course, WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) happens to be the zero polynomial, and we get nothing! Thus, we would like DD to have the property that WD​(g1,…,gr)≠0W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\neq 0. Standard Wronskians capture linear independence, and it seems reasonable to hope that g1,…,grg_{1},\ldots,g_{r} being linearly independent should allow us to argue that WD​(g1,…,gr)≠0W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\neq 0. For this, however, we need some properties satisfied by the derivative operator DD, namely that its kernel is just the base field 𝔽\mathbb{F}. More formally, if
{h∈𝔽⁡(t)​(x1,…,xn):D⁡(h)=0}=𝔽,\left\{h\in\mathbb{F}(t)(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\ :\ D(h)=0\right\}=\mathbb{F},
then WD​(g1,…,gr)=0W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=0 if and only if g1,…,grg_{1},\ldots,g_{r} are 𝔽\mathbb{F}-linearly dependent.
Flipping this around, suppose we had a convenient “derivative operator” D=∂t+∑i=1nbi(t)∂xiD=\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}} that has the property that ker⁡(D)=𝔽\ker(D)=\mathbb{F}, then we can reverse-engineer P1​(t),…,Pn​(t)P_{1}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t) such that d​Pid​t=bi​(t)\frac{dP_{i}}{dt}=b_{i}(t) and the above argument essentially goes through. In this write-up, we work with a specific derivative operator
D~:=∂t+∑i=1ni1−i​t⋅∂xi\tilde{D}:=\partial_{t}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{i}{1-it}\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}
which indeed has the property that ker⁡(D~)=𝔽\ker(\tilde{D})=\mathbb{F} (Lemma 2.6). A derivative operator of a similar shape, and its associated Wronskian, appears to have been studied by Moura [24] in a very different context. For this choice, we have Pi​(t)=−ln⁡(1−i​t)P_{i}(t)=-\ln(1-it). Of course, these are infinite power series, and the bi​(t)b_{i}(t)’s are rational functions and not polynomials. But both of these are minor technicalities that are easily fixed.
1.2.
To be more precise, the properties we need from D~\tilde{D} for our proof are that ker⁡D~=𝔽\ker\tilde{D}=\mathbb{F} and that D~\tilde{D} commutes with ∂xi\partial_{x_{i}} for all ii. The latter property essentially forces D~\tilde{D} to be of the form a(t)∂t+∑ibi(t)∂xia(t)\partial_{t}+\sum_{i}b_{i}(t)\partial_{x_{i}}, and the above derivation is the simplest of this form that satisfies ker⁡D~=𝔽\ker\tilde{D}=\mathbb{F}.

2 Derivations and DD-Wronskians
2.1Derivations.
Let R=𝔽⁡(t)​[x1,…,xn]R=\mathbb{F}(t)[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] and let 𝕂=Frac⁡(R)=𝔽⁡(t)​(x1,…,xn)\mathbb{K}=\operatorname{Frac}(R)=\mathbb{F}(t)(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}). A map D:R→RD:R\rightarrow R is said to be a derivation if
•

•

\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
D^{m-1}(g_{1})&D^{m-1}(g_{2})&\cdots&D^{m-1}(g_{m})\end{bmatrix}

Thus, we may assume without loss of generality that cm=1c_{m}=1 (by scaling 𝒄\bm{c} if necessary).

Remark 1.
There appears to be a rich literature on the study of derivation fields with numerous examples of derivations that have a trivial field of constants. The derivation defined above appears to be a special case of a broader class of derivations called Shamsuddin derivations. Nowicki’s book [25] provides a fairly comprehensive study of this field.

2.3 DD-Wronskian of a derivative-closed set
The following is a simple yet important observation that makes the above machinary applicable to the setting of polynomials of low dimensional partial derivative space.
2.9Wronskian of derivative-closed sets are xx-independent.
Let D:R→RD:R\rightarrow R be the derivation defined in Definition 2.3, and let g1,…,gm∈𝔽⁡[x1,…,xn]g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] be a set of polynomials such that for all i∈[m]i\in[m] and j∈[n]j\in[n] we have that ∂xjgi∈𝔽−span⁡(g1,…,gm)\partial_{x_{j}}g_{i}\in\operatorname{\mathbb{F}-span}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}). Then, the DD-Wronskian WD​(g1,…,gm)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}) is a rational function in tt and is independent of the xix_{i}’s.
Furthermore, if g1,…,gmg_{1},\ldots,g_{m} are linearly independent and DD is a derivation with a trivial field of constants, then WD​(g1,…,gm)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{m}) is a nonzero element of 𝔽⁡(t)\mathbb{F}(t).
Proof.
As taking partial derivatives with respect to any xix_{i} reduces the degree in xix_{i}, we may assume that g1,…,gmg_{1},\ldots,g_{m} are arranged in increasing order of degree. Thus,
∂xigk=∑j=1k−1ci​j​k​gj\partial_{x_{i}}g_{k}=\sum_{j=1}^{k-1}c_{ijk}g_{j}
for some constants ci​j​k∈𝔽c_{ijk}\in\mathbb{F}. Also, since gkg_{k} does not depend on tt we have ∂tgk=0\partial_{t}g_{k}=0 and hence
∂xi(D⁡(gk))\displaystyle\partial_{x_{i}}(D(g_{k}))
=∂xi(Q⁡(t)⋅∂tgk+Q⁡(t)⋅∑j=1nj1−j​t⋅∂xjgk)\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{i}}\left(Q(t)\cdot\partial_{t}g_{k}+Q(t)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j}{1-jt}\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}g_{k}\right)
=∂xi(Q⁡(t)⋅∑j=1nj1−j​t⋅∂xjgk)\displaystyle=\partial_{x_{i}}\left(Q(t)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j}{1-jt}\cdot\partial_{x_{j}}g_{k}\right)
=Q⁡(t)⋅∑j=1nj1−j​t⋅∂xi∂xjgk\displaystyle=Q(t)\cdot\sum_{j=1}^{n}\frac{j}{1-jt}\cdot\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}}g_{k}

3 Constructing the hitting set
Now we have all the ingredients to prove Theorem 1.1. We state it here in a slightly different form from which Theorem 1.1 would readily follow.
3.1.
Suppose 0≠f⁡(x1,…,xn)∈𝔽⁡[x1,…,xn]0\neq f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{F}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}] such that dim∂=⁣∗(f)≤r\dim\partial^{=*}(f)\leq r. Let P1​(t),…,Pn​(t)∈𝔽⁡⟦t⟧P_{1}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t)\in\mathbb{F}\llbracket t\rrbracket be defined as
Pi​(t)=∑j≥1(i​t)jjP_{i}(t)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{(it)^{j}}{j}
Then, f⁡(P1​(t),⋯,Pn​(t))≠0modtBf(P_{1}(t),\cdots,P_{n}(t))\neq 0\bmod t^{B} for B=n​r2+rB=nr^{2}+r.
Theorem 1.1 follows from the above since we may truncate each PiP_{i} to the first BB terms and retain the nonzeroness of ff.
Proof of Theorem 3.1.
For simplicity, let us assume that dim∂=⁣∗f=r\dim\partial^{=*}f=r (else we will replace rr with the actual dimension in the proof below). Let g1,g2,…,grg_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{r} be a basis of the space VfV_{f} of all partial derivatives of ff in increasing order of degree with gr=fg_{r}=f. Let P⁡(t)=(P1​(t),P2​(t),…,Pn​(t))P(t)=(P_{1}(t),P_{2}(t),\ldots,P_{n}(t)) where Pi​(t)=−ln⁡(1−i​t)P_{i}(t)=-\ln(1-it) for i=1,…,ni=1,\ldots,n.
Assume on the contrary that f∘Pf\circ P is divisible by tBt^{B} for B=n​r2+rB=nr^{2}+r. Consider the DD-Wronskian WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}). By Lemma 2.6, we know DD has a trivial field of constants, and Lemma 2.2 asserts that WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) is nonzero since g1,…,grg_{1},\ldots,g_{r} are linearly independent. Since each entry Di​(gj)D^{i}(g_{j}) of the Wronskian matrix is in fact a polynomial in t,x1,…,xnt,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}, we have that WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) is a nonzero element of 𝔽⁡[t,x1,…,xn]\mathbb{F}[t,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]. Furthermore, by Lemma 2.9, it is independent of xx and hence WD​(g1,…,gr)=w⁡(t)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})=w(t) is nonzero polynomial in tt. By 2.5, row ii of the DD-Wronskian matrix of WD​(g1,…,gr)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r}) has polynomials of degree at most n⋅(i−1)n\cdot(i-1) in tt and therefore w⁡(t)w(t) is a nonzero polynomial of degree at most n​r2nr^{2}.
On the other hand, WD​(g1,…,gr)∘P=w⁡(t)W_{D}(g_{1},\ldots,g_{r})\circ P=w(t) (since ww is independent of xx-variables), and the last column of the matrix is the vector [f∘P,D​f∘P,D2​f∘P,⋯,Dr−1​f∘P][f\circ P,Df\circ P,D^{2}f\circ P,\cdots,D^{r-1}f\circ P]. By Corollary 2.8, this entire column is therefore divisible by tB−(r−1)t^{B-(r-1)}. Therefore, w⁡(t)w(t) is divisible by tB−(r−1)t^{B-(r-1)}, but this is impossible if B≥n​r2+rB\geq nr^{2}+r since w⁡(t)w(t) is a polynomial of degree at most n​r2nr^{2}.
Thus we have that f∘P≠0modtn​r2+rf\circ P\neq 0\bmod t^{nr^{2}+r}.
∎
