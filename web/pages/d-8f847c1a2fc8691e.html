Passive HMM minimax: rare switching makes long-range prediction cost quadratic
8 October 2026

VERDICT

There is an explicit, internally derived two-point minimax bound for passive
long-range prediction. Even when the learner is told the model is one of two
stationary, irreducible, two-state binary HMMs with fixed emission matrix and
well-conditioned rank-two moments, predicting one binary observation L steps
ahead from a three-symbol context with constant expected-TV accuracy requires
Omega(L^2) independent length-three training episodes. A simple adjacent-pair
test gives O(L^2) episodes for identifying which of these two models generated
the data, so the two-point identification problem has the matching L^2 rate.

The obstruction is slow switching. The two models have transition probabilities
that differ by Theta(1/L); their observed length-three laws are only
O(1/L)-separated, while their L-step endpoint forecast kernels differ by a
fixed amount. Thus no horizon-free finite-sample guarantee holds for this
prediction loss when a lower bound on the minimum transition probability (or an
equivalent temporal-stability parameter) is not supplied. This does not refute
finite-horizon learning with stated rank/margin assumptions, or a guarantee
whose sample bound is allowed to depend on the forecast horizon and transition
conditioning.

INTERFACE AND LOSS

The hidden state and observation alphabets are both {−1,+1}. A passive training
call returns three consecutive observations from a fresh independent episode
whose hidden chain starts in its stationary distribution. The learner receives
n such length-three strings. At evaluation it receives a separate fresh
three-symbol context H=(Y_1,Y_2,Y_3) and must return a Bernoulli law q(·|S,H)
for the endpoint Y_{3+L}; the intermediate observations Y_4,...,Y_{L+2} are
not revealed. Here S is all training data. There are no hidden-state labels,
interventions, conditional-sample queries, or matrix-response queries.

For model M_i, let K_i(·|H)=Law_i(Y_{3+L}|Y_{1:3}=H). The risk is

  R_i(q) = E_{S,H under M_i}[ TV(q(·|S,H), K_i(·|H)) ].

All computation is unrestricted in the lower bound. The predictor returns only
two probabilities, so the forecast output itself has constant size. The
statistical data bill is 3n training symbols plus the three context symbols;
resets are counted as n+1 episode starts. A model description has O(log L)
bits. This is an information-theoretic result for the declared reset/truncation
interface; it is not a lower bound for a single uninterrupted stream or for an
oracle that supplies longer trajectories at the same cost.

TWO MODELS

For a flip rate r in (0,1/2), let Z_t be a stationary symmetric two-state
Markov chain with P(Z_{t+1}≠Z_t)=r and P(Z_1=+1)=P(Z_1=−1)=1/2. Given Z,
observations are conditionally independent and P(Y_t=Z_t)=3/4. Define, for
integer L≥100,

  M_0: r_0 = 1/(8L),       M_1: r_1 = 1/(2L),
  lambda_i = 1 - 2r_i.

Both models are stationary and ergodic with the same initial distribution and
the same emission matrix. Their entries are rational with O(log L)-bit
descriptions. Both transition matrices are full rank; their smallest singular
values are lambda_i≥1/2. The observation matrix has singular values 1 and 1/2.
The two-state pair-probability matrix also has its smallest singular value
bounded below by a positive constant (for this convention,
sigma_2(P_{2,1})≥1/16). So the rank and usual second-order spectral separation
do not collapse as L grows. The minimum entry of T_i diag(O_y), however, is
r_i/4=Theta(1/L), the positivity/stochasticity parameter used in conditional
prediction analyses.

MINIMAX CLAIM

Put c=(3/4−e^(−1))/8, a fixed positive constant. For every L≥100 and every
predictor using n passive training episodes, if

  n+1 ≤ c^2 L^2 / 18,

then max_i R_i(q) ≥ c/4. Numerically c/4 is about 0.0119. Consequently, a
uniform expected-TV risk smaller than c/4 on this two-model family needs
n=Omega(L^2) training episodes.

Proof details are in
`work/programmes/acquisition_learning/leaves/passive_hmm_minimax/proof.md`.
The key calculations are:

1. With Y_t∈{−1,+1}, E[Y_t|Z_t]=Z_t/2, hence
   E_i[Y_sY_t]=(1/4)lambda_i^(t−s).
   Therefore
   E_0[Y_3Y_(3+L)]−E_1[Y_3Y_(3+L)]
      =(1/4)(lambda_0^L−lambda_1^L)
      ≥(1/4)(3/4−e^(−1)).
   Since a bounded [−1,1]-valued statistic's expectation gap is at most twice
   total variation, the joint laws of (H,Y_(3+L)) are at least c apart.

2. For any y∈{−1,+1}^3, the length-three probability is
   p_r(y)=1/8[1+(lambda/4)(y_1y_2+y_2y_3)
                 +(lambda^2/4)y_1y_3].
   It is at least 1/32. Also |d p_r(y)/dr|≤1/4. Since
   r_1−r_0=3/(8L), each word probability changes by at most 3/(32L), giving
   chi^2(P_0^{(3)}||P_1^{(3)})≤9/(4L^2) and
   KL(P_0^{(3)}||P_1^{(3)})≤9/(4L^2).
   For the evaluation context alone, Pinsker gives
   TV(P_0^H,P_1^H)≤3/(2 sqrt(2) L)≤c/4 for L≥100.

3. Disintegrating the joint law shows
   E_(H~P_0^H) TV(K_0(·|H),K_1(·|H)) ≥ c−TV(P_0^H,P_1^H) ≥3c/4.
   The training data and test context together have KL at most
   9(n+1)/(4L^2), hence TV at most sqrt(9(n+1)/(8L^2)). Under the displayed
   sample condition this is at most c/4. The triangle inequality for the two
   prediction losses then gives R_0+R_1≥c/2, so max_i R_i≥c/4.

MATCHING RATE FOR THIS TWO-POINT FAMILY

For identification, W=Y_1Y_2 has mean lambda_i/4. The means differ by
3/(16L). A threshold test on the sample average of n independent W values,
with Hoeffding's inequality, has error at most
2 exp(−9n/(2048L^2)); n=O(L^2) suffices for success probability at least 2/3.
Conversely, if a test succeeds with both errors at most 1/3, the two data laws
must have TV at least 1/3 and KL at least 2/9. Since the KL of n length-three
episodes is at most 9n/(4L^2), necessarily n≥8L^2/81. Choosing the identified
model gives its exact endpoint predictor, so the same O(L^2) test upper bound
solves prediction on this two-point family. This is a matching rate in L,
though the constants in the prediction-risk lower bound are deliberately
conservative.

UNKNOWN HIDDEN STATE COUNT

The number of states in a supplied representation is not identifiable even
from infinite data: a one-state fair-emission HMM and a two-state HMM whose two
states have identical fair emissions generate the same iid fair process, for
any stationary hidden transition matrix. If “state count” means minimal
observable/Hankel rank, population identifiability is possible under
nondegeneracy, but uniform finite-sample exact rank selection still needs a
quantitative margin. For example, compare the one-state iid fair model with a
two-state stationary model having flip rate 1/4 and emissions
P(Y=Z)=1/2+a. For a>0, the latter has nonzero lag-one covariance and minimal
rank two; as a→0, every fixed-length observed law converges to iid fair.
Thus any finite sample budget can be defeated by a small enough emission
separation. This is consistent with HMM identifiability theorems that assume
known state count and full-rank transition/emission families: population
identifiability does not supply a uniform modulus of estimation.

LITERATURE AND LOCAL CARDS

* Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang, “A Spectral Algorithm for Learning Hidden Markov
  Models,” JCSS 78 (2012), arXiv:0811.4413. Their learner takes m (state count)
  as an input and uses independent observation triples. Their finite joint-law
  bound depends polynomially on inverse singular values and scales quadratically
  in the forecasted sequence length t; their uniform-in-time expected
  conditional-KL theorem adds a positive stochasticity parameter alpha and
  other filter-stability quantities. This is a positive finite-rate result
  under declared margins, not a rank-agnostic uniform guarantee.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.4413
* Gassiat, Cleynen, and Robin, “Finite state space non parametric Hidden Markov
  Models are in general identifiable,” arXiv:1306.4657. For known k, full-rank
  transition matrix and linearly independent emissions identify the parameters
  up to label permutation from the population law of three observations.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.4657
* Kakade, Krishnamurthy, Mahajan, and Zhang, “Learning Hidden Markov Models
  Using Conditional Samples,” COLT 2023, arXiv:2302.14753. It distinguishes
  passive iid observation-sequence samples from interactive conditional-probability
  and conditional-sample oracles; the reported separation is computational and
  cannot be substituted for our passive minimax statement.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.14753

SUPPLIED REALIZATION CONTRAST AND COSTS

If an exact observable-operator or positive HMM realization of rank r is
supplied, the endpoint forecast after observed context h is an algebraic
calculation. In the observable-operator convention
f(w)=beta A_w alpha, with A_Sigma=sum_x A_x, it is

  P(Y_(m+L)=y | h)
    = beta A_y A_Sigma^(L−1) A_h alpha / (beta A_h alpha).

For dense matrices, a fixed endpoint query can compute A_Sigma^(L−1) by
O(log L) matrix multiplications (O(r^3 log L) arithmetic), then apply the
context and endpoint matrices; iterating instead costs O(L r^2). This charges
the supplied model's storage/bit length and arithmetic but no acquisition
episodes. A full conditional table for all possible histories is a different
output requirement. Passive acquisition must pay for enough observations to
separate the models; a supplied realization has already paid that information
cost elsewhere. Finite-precision rounding and model-validation costs remain
separate in either interface.

BOUNDARIES

This result is for an L-step endpoint prediction under stationary reset
sampling, with a three-symbol evaluation context and length-three training
episodes. It does not show a fixed lower bound for one-step next-symbol
prediction, nor a lower bound for one long uninterrupted training trajectory.
It establishes no unconditional computational hardness and no general
impossibility of teacher-free acquisition. It proves the specific quantitative
claim above and the exact representational-count nonidentifiability.
