Abstract
Causal representation learning (CRL) is the process of recovering causally-related latent variables
from high-dimensional observations. As a label-free inference method, CRL is particularly attractive for
applications where data labels are unavailable or impractical to obtain. While there has been significant
progress in understanding the identifiability guarantees of CRL, such guarantees often hold under highly
stylized assumptions, which temper the direct application to real-world problems. This paper has a
two-fold objective forinterventionalCRL. First, it establishes identifiability guarantees for substantially
weaker interventional assumptions, resulting inblock disentanglementof the causal variables, where
the block structure depends on the realistically available intervention mechanisms. Secondly, the block
disentanglement framework is used for embodied visual state estimation, in which the objective is to
recover the latent physical variables of a robotic system directly from visual data (images and videos)
without labeled data. These two components are critically complementary. The block disentanglement
theory delineates identifiability guarantees under weakened assumptions, and the application demonstrates
that the resulting objective remains effective in a controlled embodied setting despite further assumption
violations, providing a theory-to-practice bridge needed to translate the promise oflabel-freeCRL into
practical problems.
1 Introduction
Physical systems are generally described by a set of state variables needed to characterize the systems’
condition, in conjunction with the physical laws that delineate how these variables causally influence one
another. These variables and causal mechanisms determine how the system evolves and responds to different
forms of external stimulation or interventions. In many applications, however, these variables and mechanisms
are not observed directly. Instead, the system is monitored or perceived through high-dimensional sensory
systems (e.g., images and videos). This creates a fundamental need to recover hidden variables and causal
mechanisms from high-dimensional observable data. Such recovery is critical for meaningful reasoning
about the system (e.g., how the system responds to hypothetical stimuli) and for transferring models across
environments with distributional shifts.
The recovered latent models and mechanisms must be provably correct, generating system representations
that specify the true underlying system state variables. Supervised learning offers a direct solution to

Causal representation learning (CRL) has emerged as a form of identifiable representation learning apt for
physical systems. Specifically, it is formed at the confluence of three primary research directions: (i) disen-
tangling generative factors embedded in high-dimensional data, (ii) causal inference, and (iii) representation
learning. Because a system’s variables are not necessarily statistically independent (they are coupled through
complex interactions), CRL aims to jointly disentangle them and learn the causal mechanisms that connect
them (Schölkopf et al., 2021). Such causal mechanisms represent stable conditional laws relating causes to
effects. Extensive studies in recent years have established the identifiability limits of CRL, establishing when
latent causal factors are recoverable; for representative studies see (Varıcı et al., 2025; Ahuja et al., 2023; Yao
et al., 2025; Ng et al., 2025). In parallel, there has been an active interest in translating these CRL guarantees
into applications in genomics (Tejada-Lapuerta et al., 2025), biomedical (Sun et al., 2025), and dynamical
systems (Yao et al., 2024).
A particularly important application with growing interest in which CRL offers a natural solution is the
analysis of video observations of physical systems (e.g., robots), where the objective is recovering the latent
state variables from image sequences without requiring frame-by-frame labels (Lippe et al., 2023a;b; Chen
et al., 2025). A video provides a high-dimensional record of how the physical system evolves and responds to
external inputs, while the true variables (e.g., a robot’s positions, orientations, and velocity) are hidden and
embedded in the video record. In such applications, while videos are readily and inexpensively available,
annotating each frame is prohibitively laborious and costly. Therefore, this is a setting in which CRL can
offer significant potential by identifying and recovering latent variables and their causal dynamics directly
from unlabeled video.
Theory-to-practice gap.Achieving these objectives in videos, however, necessitates operationalizing
CRL in regimes beyond what the current CRL theory covers. Specifically, while identifiability results in
CRL often provide strong guarantees, they also rely oncollectivelystrong assumptions such as access to
counterfactual observations; atomic (single-node) intervention targets; stochastic hard ordo interventions;
independent samples; exhaustive interventional diversity; and an exactly known latent dimensionality. While
each of these assumptions can be justifiably valid in certain settings, in real-world settings, such as systems
evolving continuously over time, they might not all be satisfiedsimultaneously. Challenges that violate
this set of assumptions include sequential access to information and partial or imperfect interventions with
unknown targets. As a result, the gap between identifiable CRL theory and label-free state recovery from
video is fundamentally a gap in the joint validity of the assumptions required by existing guarantees. For
more discussion on the existing CRL guarantees and their attendant assumptions, we refer to Appendix A.
To bridge the gap between current CRL theory and practical visual state estimation, we take two comple-
mentary steps, one theoretical and one empirical. Both are critically needed, as they address different but
complementary obstacles.
• Theoretical bridge:The theoretical step weakens the intervention assumptions required for identifiability.
Specifically, current CRL theory focuses on disentanglingindividualvariables, which in turn often
necessitates intervention structures that isolate one variable at a time. To address this, we consider
block disentanglement, and establish conditions under which an intervenedblockof variables remains
2
identifiable even when individual variables cannot be disentangled separately.
• Empirical bridge:On the empirical side, we further weaken the assumptions and investigate whether
the resulting block disentanglement principle remains effective when applied to embodied visual data,
where temporal dependence, imperfect intervention effects, and uncertainty in the latent representation
arise naturally. For this purpose, robotics is a natural target and has long motivated CRL (Schölkopf et al.,
2021): an effective robotic world model must capture the causal factors governing the robot’s state and
its interaction with the environment. In our experiments, we show that even under extensive assumption
violations, the proposed method retains significant information about the “active block” of variables in a
simulated robot pose estimation task.
Collectively, our results demonstrate that interventional CRL remains highly effective beyond stringent
regimes defined by stylized assumptions. We achieve this by relaxing the identifiability target from individual
causal variables to an intervention-defined block under weaker conditions; the resulting block disentanglement
principle remains effective even when experimental data further depart from the theoretical requirements.
Accordingly, our results point to a step-by-step transition in which progressively weaker theoreticaland
empirical conditions still yield useful representation learning objectives.

CRL block disentanglement.The main objective in CRL is to use observational data X to recover the
latent variablesZ and the latent causal graphG. Identifiability in CRL pertains to establishing conditions
under which a unique equivalence class of models explains the observed data. Existing CRL identifiability
results mainly focus onelementwisedisentanglement of Z, that is, recovering the variables{Zi :i∈[n]}

4 Empirical Bridge: Block Disentanglement to Embodied Video
The theoretical bridge in Section 3 already relaxes assumptions (C1)–(C4), while retaining (C5)–(C6), and
establishes block-disentanglement guarantees under this weaker setting. The next question is whether the
resulting CRL principles remain effective when we move further toward practical visual state estimation and
additionally relax (C5)–(C6). This constitutes ourempirical bridge.
To evaluate this progression, we consider two complementary empirical settings. The first, which we call
theassumption-matched benchmark, satisfies the full set of assumptions (C1)–(C6). It provides a reference
point for the performance attainable when the canonical CRL conditions hold, and allows us to assess the
overall effectiveness of the methodology in its most favorable identifiable setting. The second, which we
call theembodied setting, additionally dispenses with (C5)–(C6), on top of the relaxation of (C1)–(C4)
already established by the block-disentanglement theory. Comparing these two settings therefore serves
two complementary purposes: benchmarking performance under the canonical assumptions, and assessing
how far the combined theoretical and empirical bridges can carry CRL toward visual state estimation under
substantially weaker conditions.
4.1 Setup and Data Generation
We consider estimating a robot’s pose and joint configuration from visual observations as an important
instance of visual state estimation. This capability is particularly relevant when a robot’s internal state is
unavailable, unreliable, or cannot be directly communicated to other agents. Accordingly, visual recovery of
articulated robot state has received growing attention in computer vision and robotics. We refer to Appendix A
for a thorough review of the related literature.
For our study, the latent physical state of interest is the robot arm’s joint configuration. The rendered images
depend on these joint angles, as well as other intrinsic and extrinsic factors such as link geometry, camera
pose, lighting, and background. To isolate the state estimation problem, we vary only the joint angles while
holding the remaining generative factors fixed. We generate the visual observations using the Panda-Gym
platform (Gallouédec et al., 2021), which provides controlled actuation of the robotic arm and corresponding
rendered images. To preserve observability of the full joint configuration and support the injective rendering
assumption onf, we record each pose from two distinct camera viewpoints.
As briefly mentioned earlier, in our analysis we consider the following empirical regimes.
• Assumption-matched benchmark.This setting satisfies (C1)–(C6). Interventions are atomic (C1),
replacing one joint’s sampling distribution with ahardintervention while leaving the others untouched
(C2); environments come in matched pairs (qi,¯qi) per joint as (C3) requires, and are designed to satisfy
(C4); joint angles are sampled i.i.d. and rendered independently, so there is no temporal structure (C5);
and the true number of varying joints is known (C6). This regime validates whether disentanglement
and variable estimation are numerically stable on rendered 128×128 images, and whether the canonical
objective recovers the intended coordinates at this visual scale.

• Embodied setting.This setting moves beyond the conditions of Theorem 1 and additionally violates

Empirical objectives.The experiments are designed to determine how far the CRL principles developed in
the preceding sections carry from the assumption-matched setting toward embodied visual data. In particular,
we address the following questions:
• Can CRL recover physical state from visual observations without state labels?In the assumption-
matched benchmark, we test whether the canonical score-based CRL objective can recover individual joint
variables from high-dimensional images when its identifiability assumptions are satisfied, and compare its
performance with supervised pose-estimation methods.
• Does the block-disentanglement principle remain effective beyond the theory?When we additionally
violate the assumptions underlying the block identifiability theorem, we test whether our approach still
concentrates information about the relevant physical variables in the target block?
• Where in the pipeline is state information retained or lost?We compare how much information about
the true state is retained after visual compression, in the complete learned latent representation, and in
the recovered active block. This lets us distinguish limitations from feature extraction from those from
representation learning or block selection.
• How sensitive is the resulting representation to departures from the idealized setting?We study the
effect of factors such as the bottleneck dimension and other experimental variations to assess whether the
observed disentanglement behavior persists as the representation and data-generating process become less
tightly matched to the theoretical assumptions.

6 Concluding Remarks
We have proposed a two-step path from CRL identifiability theory toward label-free visual state estimation. In
this first step, we weaken canonical intervention assumptions and establish block-disentanglement guarantees,
showing that an active group of causal variables can remain identifiable even when individual variables cannot
be recovered separately. In the second step, we show that the resulting principle remains effective for retaining
relevant information in embodied visual settings that further violate the theoretical assumptions. Empirically,
score-based CRL recovers robot joint states from images without state labels in the assumption-matched
setting, and the block-based objective continues to be informative about the relevant physical variables under
temporal dependence, imperfect interventions, and overcomplete representations. These two components are
complementary. The theory characterizes what remains identifiable under weaker assumptions, while the
experiments assess whether those principles remain effective as CRL moves toward practical visual data. To
further narrow the theory-practice gap and realize CRL’s important potential, a key next step is to extend the
identifiability theory to settings with temporally dependent observations, approximate intervention effects,
and unknown or overcomplete latent dimensionality.

A Related Work and Limitations
Identifiable causal representation learning.Without additional structure or distributional diversity, recov-
ering latent variables from observations under an unknown nonlinear mixing function is generally impossible
(Hyvärinen & Pajunen, 1999; Locatello et al., 2019). Causal representation learning (CRL) addresses this
ambiguity by exploiting causal structure and changes across environments. A substantial literature establishes
identifiability from interventions under different assumptions on the observation map, latent causal model, and
intervention mechanisms (Squires et al., 2023; Buchholz et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2023; von Kügelgen et al.,
2023; Varıcı et al., 2024; 2025; Acartürk et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2026). Other approaches
include counterfactual CRL (von Kügelgen et al., 2021), unified frameworks to analyze interventional CRL
along with nonlinear ICA and perturbation models (Yi et al., 2026), and temporal (Lippe et al., 2023a)
and multi-view CRL literature (Yao et al., 2025). These results range from linear latent representations or
observation models to general nonlinear transformations, and consider settings including hard, soft, known,
and unknown interventions.
17
Interventional diversity is particularly relevant to our work. Existing nonlinear CRL guarantees commonly
obtain elementwise disentanglement by exploiting sufficiently localized and diverse changes across interven-
tional environments (von Kügelgen et al., 2023; Varıcı et al., 2025) or the nonlinear ICA connection under
Gaussian additive noise models (Ng et al., 2025). Our result instead asks what remains identifiable when
such intervention structure is unavailable, replacing intervention-by-intervention requirements with collective
conditions on the support and span of score differences. The precise comparison between these assumptions
and our conditions is given in Section 3.
Block disentanglement.When individual latent coordinates cannot be identified, a weaker objective is
to recover groups of variables up to an invertible transformation within each group. von Kügelgen et al.
(2021) identify the invariant content block from paired observations (also called counterfactual setting) whose
augmentations preserve content while sufficiently varying the remaining style variables, allowing causal
dependence between content and style.
Under polynomial transformation, Ahuja et al. (2023) exploit intervention-induced support independence to
obtain block-affine guarantees. For general nonlinear transformation, Liang et al. (2023) identify intervention-
target blocks when the causal graph and target sets are known and sufficiently diverse perfect interventions
satisfy a block score-rank condition. Ahuja et al. (2024) use ancestrally closed soft intervention target sets
(with proper distributional assumptions) to disentangle the non-intervened variables from the intervened
ones. Yao et al. (2025) establish a broaderinvariant-blockidentification result by combining information
preservation with an invariance condition. Our Theorem 1 establishes recovery of the intervention-affected
block through collective score diversity, even when its individual coordinates cannot be separated. The
discussion following the theorem compares the required intervention structure and diversity conditions with
these earlier guarantees.
Visual robot pose estimation.Visual robot pose estimation is most commonly formulated as a supervised
prediction problem. DREAM (Lee et al., 2020) estimates robot pose through image-space keypoints, while
RoboPose (Labbé et al., 2021) iteratively refines pose and joint-angle estimates. More recent methods
include HPE (Ban et al., 2024) and RoboPEPP (Goswami et al., 2025), the latter combining self-supervised
visual pretraining with supervised joint-angle regression. These methods demonstrate strong pose-estimation
performance, but ultimately rely on pose or joint-state supervision for the prediction task. In contrast, our
objective is to recover the underlying physical state representation without per-frame state labels, using
variation across intervention environments as the learning signal.
Classical alternatives include geometry- and marker-based systems, such as fiducial-marker approaches
(Garrido-Jurado et al., 2014; García-Ruiz et al., 2023). These methods provide a different tradeoff, relying
on known geometric structure, calibration, or instrumentation rather than learned latent representations.
Our experiments are not intended to replace these specialized pose-estimation pipelines; rather, robot pose
provides a controlled embodied setting in which to study whether identifiable-CRL principles can be carried
from high-dimensional observations toward state estimation under progressively weaker assumptions.
Limitations.We list the main limitations of our paper.
• Our empirical analysis is limited to simulation studies in a controlled environment. This is a deliberate
choice to maximize controllability of the experimental setting. However, in order to demonstrate practical-
ity, CRL field needs to start using real-world data as well. This paper shows that such a transition does not
18
have to be done abruptly.
• Proving robust estimation for both score estimation and disentanglement stages against using non-i.i.d.
data would address violation V1 (temporal dependence) directly, and are likely a prerequisite for any
theoretical treatment of video modality from the interventional CRL perspective.
• An approximate version of Theorem 1 that bounds the degradation of block recovery in terms of the
magnitude of off-block score deviations would be required to treat violation V2 (off-target effects) from a
theoretically grounded perspective.
• There exists CRL approaches that can not only toleratedb>n but can also identifyn. While this would
help with addressing violation V3 (overcomplete bottleneck), our empirical analysis implies that our
method is currently robust against using an overcomplete learned representation.
• As shown in Appendix D, violations of the injectivity of the transformation f are very impactful: a
consistent occlusion of a joint from an unfavorable camera angle impedes recovery of that joint, and
requires a multi-camera setup to resolve.

C.1 Data Generation
Data collection replaces i.i.d. rendering with continuous trajectories. We construct six environments, one for
each robot joint. Each environment is generated from 4000 closed-loop trajectories and differs through the
goal-sampling distribution used for its nominally targeted joint.
Each episode proceeds as follows.
1. Sample a start configuration and a goal configuration. In environmentk, the goal distribution prioritizes
displacement of jointk (sampled uniformly at random with minimum displacement of 0.5 radians), while
the remaining joint targets are changed in a small amount (shifted by a uniformly random value less than
0.05radians).
2. Drive the arm from its start configuration toward the sampled goal using a proportional controller. Every
simulator step is rendered and retained, so consecutive observations differ by one controller step.
3. Terminate the trajectory once the goal is reached within a prescribed tolerance or after a fixed step budget
(max. 40 frames), and reset the environment before generating the next trajectory.
We use the same two-camera rendering configuration: one at 45 and other 135 degrees with respect to the
arm’s natural position, with observations from the two views concatenated after AE1 feature extraction.
Start states are sampled uniformly at random from the valid angles. The simulator itself rejects malformed
configurations (e.g., where the robot arm intersects the table), so no additional processing during generation
were needed.
The target distribution at the default configuration (the default “embodied regime” setup) is chosen such that
in environmentk, thek-th joint angle is set to be significantly different (difference≥0.5 radians) from the
initial state. The off-target joints are instead targeted with relatively smaller perturbations (<0.05radians).
The proportional controller gain is set to 1; however, the trajectories were stable and smooth across the
configuration space. Maximum trajectory length was set at40frames.
The resulting data exhibit strong temporal dependence within each trajectory. Resets limit dependence across
episodes but do not remove the correlation between consecutive frames within one episode. Moreover,
targeting one joint does not yield an atomic intervention in the resulting trajectory: closed-loop motion
generally induces simultaneous motion of other joints. Thus, the environment label indicates the joint whose
displacement is emphasized rather than an intervention that changes only a single latent coordinate.
In the finalized experiments we use all six environments jointly, soI= [n] . Consequently, off-target motion
makes the individual environments non-atomic and couples their intervention effects.
C.2 Score Difference Estimation
Unlike the assumption-matched setting, the embodied regime does not contain a separately generated
observational dataset. Instead, we exploit the directional structure of each controlled trajectory to construct
the two distributions required by the log-density-ratio estimator.
For every trajectory in environmentk, we divide the sequence into two non-overlapping temporal halves.
Samples from the first half form one classifier class and samples from the second half form the other. We

then train one binary LDR classifierf (k)
LDR for each environment. As described in the main pipeline, its logit
gradient provides the corresponding feature-space score difference estimate.
In the embodied setting, this temporal-half contrast is anempirical proxymotivated by the theoretical score-
difference construction, rather than a direct estimate of the interventional score difference in Theorem 1.
We deliberately use non-overlapping temporal halves rather than frame-by-frame contrasts between nearby
portions of the same trajectory. At finite sample size, heavily overlapping temporal supports would cause the
classifier to observe nearly indistinguishable samples from the two classes over much of the trajectory, thus
substantially weakening the density-ratio signal. The half-trajectory construction instead provides a coarser
but more separable contrast while retaining the continuous-video nature of the data.

performance.

their final asymptotic performances were nearly identical, irrespective of the batch size.
