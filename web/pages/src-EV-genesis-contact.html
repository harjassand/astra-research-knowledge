The obstruction selected for attack was:
How can one create many mutually adjacent connected branch sets while preserving their disjointness, without replacing ordinary contact by a stronger—and potentially false—requirement?
A \(K_t\)-minor can be represented by \(t\) disjoint connected vertex sets, with an edge between every pair of sets. Call these sets branch sets.
The smallest nontrivial branch sets are edges. A matching whose edges are pairwise touching therefore gives a clique minor.
Let \(H\) be a graph on an even number \(N\ge4\) of vertices, and set \(G=\overline H\). Define
\[ D=\Delta(H), \qquad C=\max_v c_H(v), \]
where \(c_H(v)\) counts the induced four-cycles of \(H\) containing \(v\).
Choose \(x,y\in(0,1)\), and put
\[ L=(1-x)^D(1-y)^C. \]
If
\[ \boxed{ \frac1{N-1}\le xL^2, \qquad \frac1{(N-1)(N-3)}\le yL^4, } \tag{1} \]
then \(G\) has a pairwise-touching perfect matching. Consequently,
\[ h(G)\ge N/2. \]
Choose a perfect matching \(M\) uniformly from all perfect matchings of \(K_N\). There are two kinds of failure.
A matching edge might belong to \(H\), rather than \(G\). Each specified forbidden edge occurs with probability
\[ p_1=\frac1{N-1}. \]
Alternatively, two legal matching edges might fail to touch. If their endpoint pairs are \(\{a,b\}\) and \(\{c,d\}\), this happens precisely when
\[ ac,ad,bc,bd\in E(H), \qquad ab,cd\notin E(H). \]
Thus their four vertices induce a four-cycle in \(H\), and the matching contains its two diagonals. Each specified event of this kind has probability
\[ p_2=\frac1{(N-1)(N-3)}. \]
This is an exact characterization of contact failure, not a surrogate based on density or average connectivity.
These events specify partial matchings inside a uniform perfect matching. The Lu–Székely negative-dependency theorem permits the lopsided Lovász local lemma in this probability space. We may use a dependency graph joining all events whose vertex supports intersect. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/0905.3983v4)
Each vertex participates in at most \(D\) first-kind events and \(C\) second-kind events. Assign activities \(x\) and \(y\), respectively. An event supported on \(r\) vertices has a local-lemma neighbour-product at least \(L^r\). Conditions (1) therefore imply simultaneous avoidance of every failure event.
The resulting matching consists of legal edges, and every pair touches. Contracting its edges gives \(K_{N/2}\). \(\square\)
For any sequence of even-order graphs satisfying
\[ \Delta(H_N)=o(N) \]
and
\[ \boxed{ \limsup_{N\to\infty} \frac{\max_v c_{H_N}(v)}{N^2} < \frac1{4e}, } \tag{2} \]
the complement has a pairwise-touching perfect matching for all sufficiently large \(N\).
To see this, choose \(c<1/(4e)\) above the limsup and set
\[ x=A/N,\qquad y=B/N^2. \]
Then \(\log L\ge-cB-o(1)\). The limiting requirements become
\[ Ae^{-2cB}>1, \qquad Be^{-4cB}>1. \]
Taking \(B=1/(4c)\) makes the second expression \(1/(4ce)>1\); one then chooses \(A\) sufficiently large.
The constant \(1/(4e)\) is a threshold of this activity calculation. It is not claimed to be the optimal graph-theoretic threshold.
The same certificate has a useful completion form.
Suppose \(B_1,\ldots,B_r\) already form a clique-minor model, and \(U\) is an even set of unused vertices. For each old branch set define
\[ S_i=\{u\in U:N_G(u)\cap B_i=\varnothing\}. \]
A new matching edge fails to touch \(B_i\) exactly when both endpoints lie in \(S_i\). Therefore form the forbidden-edge graph
\[ F=\overline{G[U]}\ \cup\ \bigcup_{i=1}^r K_{S_i}. \]
Use \(D=\Delta(F)\) in (1), while retaining the induced-four-cycle count of \(\overline{G[U]}\). When the certificate holds, the new matching extends the old model to
\[ K_{r+|U|/2}, \]
with all old branch sets unchanged and all new branch sets disjoint.
This is the substantive operation retained from the first approach. Its limitation is explicit: high chromatic number has not been shown to produce the required local budgets.
The matching-space argument also admits an exact elementary operation.
Suppose a perfect matching is uniformly distributed conditional on containing an edge \(ab\). Choose \(x\) uniformly among vertices other than \(a\). If \(x=b\), leave the matching unchanged. Otherwise, let \(xy\) be the matching edge at \(x\), and replace
\[ \{ab,xy\} \quad\longmapsto\quad \{ax,by\}. \tag{3} \]
The output is an unconditioned uniform perfect matching.
The reason is a bijection: from an output matching, recover the unique choice \(x\) as the partner of \(a\); the partner of \(b\) then determines the inverse switch.
For several prescribed edges, release them successively, temporarily excluding endpoints of edges still prescribed. This removes the conditioning exactly. Moreover, every newly created edge meets the support of the original prescribed edges. A canonical failure event supported entirely outside that support cannot be newly created.
This belongs to the established resampling-oracle framework; it is not a newly invented general local lemma. Nor does the bijection alone prove convergence of an arbitrary repeated-repair algorithm. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.02044)
The supplied code checks the bijection and support property exhaustively through order ten. At order ten, each of the 945 perfect matchings has exactly one preimage in each tested release experiment.
The distinction between ordinary contact and domination matters.
In an ordinary model, some vertex of one branch set must meet some vertex of the other. In a dominating model, earlier branch sets must dominate every vertex of later branch sets. Illingworth and Steiner’s September 2026 construction disproves the corresponding exact dominating conjecture using complements of triangle-free subgraphs of Suzuki–Tits graphs. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35361)
I tested the contact certificate against a modification of that construction.
For every sufficiently large \(q=2^{2h+1}\), there is a graph \(G_q\), with \(N=q^4\) vertices, such that
\[ \alpha(G_q)=2, \qquad \chi(G_q)=N/2, \]
and \(G_q\) has a \(K_{N/2}\)-model with every branch set of size two, but has no dominating model of order
\[ \left\lceil(1/2-\delta)N\right\rceil \]
for a fixed absolute \(\delta>0\).
Thus
\[ h(G_q)\ge\chi(G_q), \]
despite the failure of a smaller dominating model. Equality \(h(G_q)=\chi(G_q)\) is not asserted.
The published geometric input is a graph on \(\mathbb F_q^4\) whose edges lie on special affine lines. Each vertex is on \(q^2+1\) such lines; all triangles lie on one line. Adjacent pairs have \(q-2\) common neighbours, and nonadjacent pairs have \(q(q-1)\). These are properties established in the source, not discoveries of this investigation. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35361)
Choose one special direction. Its \(q^3\) parallel lines partition the vertices.
On these lines, fix balanced bipartitions into two sets of size \(q/2\). On every other special line, independently assign fair binary labels to its vertices. Retain only edges between different labels, producing \(H_q\), and let
\[ G_q=\overline{H_q}. \]
The graph \(H_q\) is triangle-free. Its fixed parallel class contains a perfect matching, so the complement can be coloured with \(N/2\) independent pairs. Triangle-freeness gives the reverse inequality, proving
\[ \chi(G_q)=N/2. \]
The decisive calculation concerns four-cycle incidence.
For noncollinear vertices \(u,w\), their \(q(q-1)\) potential common neighbours use distinct pairs of special lines. At most two witnesses involve fixed parallel-class lines. Every other witness survives independently with probability \(1/4\). Chernoff bounds and a union bound therefore give, uniformly,
\[ d_{H_q}(u,w)=\bigl(1/4+o(1)\bigr)q^2. \]
For collinear pairs, codegree is at most \(q\).
In a triangle-free graph,
\[ c_H(v) = \sum_{w\ne v}\binom{d_H(v,w)}2. \]
Each four-cycle has a unique vertex opposite \(v\), so there is no additional factor. There are only \(O(q^3)\) collinear choices of \(w\). Hence
\[ \begin{aligned} c_{H_q}(v) &\le O(q^3)\binom q2 + N\binom{(1/4+o(1))q^2}{2}\\ &\le (1/32+o(1))N^2. \end{aligned} \]
Also \(\Delta(H_q)=O(q^3)=o(N)\). Since
\[ \frac1{32}<\frac1{4e}, \]
Theorem 1 supplies the ordinary \(K_{N/2}\)-model.
Finally, the modification preserves the three hypotheses used by the published deterministic dominating-model obstruction: balanced-enough line sides, a codegree lower bound for noncollinear pairs, and independence number below \(2q^3\). For the last condition, a fixed \(2q^3\)-vertex set is independent in the nonfixed lines with probability at most \(2^{-q^5}\); the union bound is \(2^{q^4-q^5}=o(1)\). The source’s deterministic argument therefore remains applicable. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.35361)
What this establishes: the geometric obstruction to domination does not obstruct ordinary chromatic-size contact models in this modified family.
What it does not establish: that arbitrary high-chromatic graphs admit such models.
A small counterexample prevents an unjustified extrapolation.
Take two copies of \(K_4\) and join them by one bridge. Then
\[ \alpha(G)=2,\qquad \chi(G)=h(G)=4. \]
A perfect matching cannot use the bridge: doing so leaves three vertices on each side with no available cross edge. Thus every perfect matching consists of two edges on each side.
On either side, one matching edge avoids the bridge endpoint. It cannot touch any matching edge on the opposite side. Therefore no pairwise-touching perfect matching exists.
Yet four singleton vertices in either \(K_4\) already give the required chromatic-size minor.
The exhaustive check finds nine legal perfect matchings and zero pairwise-touching ones. The mathematical consequence is more important than the enumeration:
Requiring all vertices to be covered by two-vertex branch sets creates an obstruction that the original minor problem does not have.
Improving the local-lemma constant cannot repair that mismatch.
The second approach allows branch sets of arbitrary size, provided each induces a connected bipartite graph.
Suppose a graph \(J\) has such a partition \(\mathcal P\), with a perfect quotient \(Q=J/\mathcal P\). Then
\[ \boxed{ \chi(J)\le2\chi(Q) =2\omega(Q) \le2h(J). } \tag{4} \]
The first inequality uses two colours for each quotient colour. The last holds because connected disjoint bags make the quotient a minor.
Unfortunately, requiring this partition for every entire graph is false. Scott, Seymour and Wood constructed graphs in which every perfect connected partition has an arbitrarily large clique inside a part. [Princeton Math](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~pds/papers/chordal/paper.pdf)
The important observation is that their explicit recursive obstruction does not remain an obstruction after preserving only the graph’s chromatic content.
Let \(A(k,r)\) denote the explicit recursive graphs in Section 3 of the cited preprint. Direct analysis of that construction gives
\[ \boxed{ \chi(A(k,r))=\omega(A(k,r)) = 1+\frac{2k(k^2-1)}3+k(r-1). } \tag{5} \]
This formula is derived here from the construction; it is not being quoted as a theorem of the paper.
The proof follows its two recursions. The \(r\)-step attaches a previous graph completely to a \(k\)-clique, increasing both the chromatic number and clique number by \(k\). In the \(k\)-step, each new gadget consists of two \(K_{k+1}\)’s sharing a vertex. Each gadget vertex forbids at most \(k-1\) colours from the old core. The gadget is \(k\)-degenerate and retains at least \(k+1\) available colours per vertex, so its colouring extends greedily. Writing \(a_k=\chi(A(k,1))=\omega(A(k,1))\), this gives
\[ a_1=1,\qquad a_k=a_{k-1}+2k(k-1), \]
which yields (5). The full argument is in the research note. The underlying recursion is specified in the source. [Princeton Math](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~pds/papers/chordal/paper.pdf)
Consequently, every graph in that family contains an induced clique with its full chromatic number. Delete everything outside that clique, and the partition obstruction disappears completely.
This does not invalidate the published theorem. It identifies exactly what that theorem obstructs: partitioning the entire graph, including chromatically redundant structure.
The resulting question is precise:
Does every finite vertex-chromatic-critical graph admit a partition into connected induced bipartite subgraphs whose quotient is perfect?
Here vertex-chromatic-critical means that deleting any vertex reduces the chromatic number.
An affirmative proof would imply the factor-two target: repeatedly delete chromatically redundant vertices, apply the partition theorem to the remaining full-chromatic core, then use (4).
This statement remains unproved. It is a falsifiable structural proposal, not an achieved removal of the central difficulty.
There is some structural support beyond merely restating a desired inequality. A critical graph has no nontrivial clique cutset: colourings on pieces meeting in a clique can be aligned by permuting colours, making the whole chromatic number the maximum of the piece chromatic numbers. A proper piece would then preserve the full chromatic number, contradicting criticality. This removes a basic attachment mechanism used by the published counterexamples.
The partition property is also preserved by the Mycielski construction. Retain the original bags and put all new shadow vertices together with their new root into one bag. That bag is an induced star and touches every old bag; the quotient becomes the cone over the old perfect quotient and remains perfect. Thus this route is not restricted to bounded chromatic number.
The finite falsification tests found partitions for all 18 nonempty critical graphs in the graph atlas through order seven. A stronger connected-palette condition also passed on 99 reproducibly generated critical examples through order fourteen. These are small tests, with repeated isomorphism types in the random sample—not evidence sufficient for the universal theorem.
I also examined the statement
\[ \chi(G)\le\max_{J\preceq G}\chi_f(J), \tag{6} \]
which might appear to combine with the known fractional bound \(\chi_f\le2h\). [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.10831v1)
But (6) is equivalent to exact Hadwiger, not an independently easier bridge.
One direction follows by taking a clique minor. Conversely, assume (6), and take a minor-minimal \(k\)-chromatic graph \(J\). Every proper minor is at most \((k-1)\)-colourable.
Unless \(J=K_k\), its order \(n\) exceeds \(k\). Colour \(J-v\) with \(k-1\) colours for each vertex \(v\), and give every resulting colour class weight \(1/(n-1)\). This produces a fractional colouring with
\[ \chi_f(J) \le \frac{n(k-1)}{n-1} <k. \]
Every proper minor also has fractional chromatic number below \(k\), contradicting (6). Thus all minor-minimal \(k\)-chromatic graphs would have to be \(K_k\), which implies exact Hadwiger.
That route is discarded because its proposed hypothesis repackages the original difficulty.
