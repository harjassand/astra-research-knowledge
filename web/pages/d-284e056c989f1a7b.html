1 Introduction and statement
An equilibrium point of a ﬁxed electrostatic ﬁeld is a position at which the force on a test charge
vanishes. For a Coulomb potential this is precisely a critical point of the potential: the conventional
electric ﬁeld is−∇V , and the sign does not aﬀect its zero set. The question considered here is how
many isolated equilibria can be produced by n distinct positive point charges in three-dimensional
space. The answer is unknown even in its order of growth.

1.2 History of known bounds
The problem goes back to Maxwell’s discussion of equipotential surfaces in his 1873 Treatise [13]. In
the standard modern reconstruction, the quadratic estimate conventionally attributed to Maxwell
is the conjectural inequality
M(n)≤ (n− 1)2. (2)
The topology forcesn−1 equilibria, and Maxwell asserted that there could be at most (n−1)(n−2)
additional ones. Maxwell did not supply a complete proof; Gabrielov, Novikov, and Shapiro [ 5]
reconstruct the argument, explain its unproved geometric step, and formulate (2) as the Maxwell
conjecture. Early critical-point-theoretic treatments include Kiang’s study of nondegenerate
Newtonian potentials [10] and the work of Morse and Cairns, who apparently independently posed
the problem of ﬁnding an upper bound [14, p. 293].
The universal topological baseline. For a positive Morse conﬁguration, harmonicity permits
only Morse indices 1 and 2, and the global degree relation is
m2−m1 =n− 1, # Crit(V ) =n− 1 + 2m1. (3)
Proposition 1.2 gives a self-contained proof. Consequently every admissible conﬁguration has at
least n− 1 equilibria and
# Crit(V )≡n− 1 (mod 2) . (4)
The lower bound is sharp as a statement about every conﬁguration: collinear positive charges have
exactly one equilibrium in each interval between consecutive sources, all of index 2. In particular
M(n)≥n− 1, and the elementary two-charge case has M(2) = 1.
