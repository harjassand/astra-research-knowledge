2019</meta-value></custom-meta></custom-meta-group></article-meta></front><body><sec id="Sec1" sec-type="introduction"><title>Introduction</title><p id="Par2">Life is energetically expensive and mostly paid in adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In all aerobic organisms, reducing equivalents from the oxidative breakdown of nutrients are converted to ATP in a process termed oxidative phosphorylation. Located in the cytoplasmic membrane of bacteria or the inner membrane of mitochondria, the members of the respiratory chain create and maintain a proton motive force (<italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>) by proton coupled electron transfer reactions, e.g. from NADH to oxygen. The <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, composed of a proton gradient (ΔpH) and a membrane potential (Δψ), energizes the rotary mechanism of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase that catalyzes the synthesis of ATP from its substrates ADP and P<sub>i</sub> (for a review, see<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR1">1</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR4">4</xref></sup>). The F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase, which in most organisms can also work as an ATP driven proton pump, is composed of the membrane embedded F<sub>0</sub> part and the soluble F<sub>1</sub> part and can be divided into a stator and a rotor part. During ATP synthesis, when the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> is high, protons are transported through the interface of subunit a and the c-ring converting the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> into mechanical rotational energy. The c-ring is connected with subunits ε and γ forming the rotor, which transmits the rotary energy to the catalytic sites in the α<sub>3</sub>β<sub>3</sub> headgroup. In the absence of a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, the enzyme can hydrolyze cellular ATP to establish an electrochemical gradient (the enzyme rotates in the opposite direction). This hydrolytic function of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase is vital for some bacteria under anaerobic conditions to maintain the membrane potential, when substrate level phosphorylation is the only source of ATP.</p><p id="Par3">In order to minimize wasteful ATP hydrolysis, organisms have evolved different regulatory mechanisms, such as the inhibitory proteins IF1 in mitochondria<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR5">5</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref></sup>. A similar function has been attributed to the ζ subunit of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase of <italic toggle="yes">Paracoccus denitrificans</italic>, but is currently debated<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref></sup>. In other bacteria such as B<italic toggle="yes">acillus</italic> PS3 and <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, the ε subunit has been proposed to ratchet the rotor in the synthesis direction<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref></sup> by extending its C-terminal domain along the γ subunit<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref></sup>, a conformational change that might be controlled by ATP binding in some bacteria<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR13">13</xref></sup>. An alternative mechanism was suggested recently, in which the inhibitory part of ε works in a similar fashion as IF1 in mitochondria: by blocking both ATP synthesis and hydrolysis in absence of a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> and being released upon establishment of the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> <sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR12">12</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref></sup>. A further role has been attributed to the ε subunit in the coupling of F<sub>1</sub> reactions and F<sub>0</sub> proton pumping<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR8">8</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR9">9</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR15">15</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR17">17</xref></sup>. In addition to these mechanisms, all F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthases show a decreased ATP hydrolysis rate in the presence of high ADP concentrations, a phenomenon known as the MgADP-inhibition of ATP hydrolysis<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR18">18</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref></sup>. During MgADP inhibition, the enzyme is in a locked state, from which it can be reverted by e.g. the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>. In some bacteria like <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, MgADP inhibition is less pronounced and the threshold <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> for re-activation lowered<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR20">20</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR21">21</xref></sup>. Taken together, these mechanisms prohibit complete cellular depletion of ATP in the absence of a sufficient <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>.</p><p id="Par4">In contrast to ATP hydrolysis that can be investigated in detergent solution or even with the F<sub>1</sub> part only, ATP synthesis experiments require the presence of a proton tight membrane and a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>. Quantitative ATP synthesis experiments have been performed with a variety of biological material such as intact mitochondria, submitochondrial particles, chloroplasts, bacteria and inverted bacterial membranes, and purified F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase reconstituted into liposomes<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR27">27</xref></sup>. Depending on the used system, the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> was established by natural proton pumps<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR23">23</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR28">28</xref></sup> or an acid-base treatment in combination with a potassium/valinomycin diffusion potential to mimic the membrane potential<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR25">25</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR29">29</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR30">30</xref></sup>. Different methods to quantify ATP have been applied, including the following four. <italic toggle="yes">(I)</italic> The most popular and sensitive technique is based on the luciferase/luciferin couple, which in the presence of oxygen and ATP emits light that can be detected by a luminometer (for a review, see<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR31">31</xref></sup>). The technique is very sensitive and is applied to determine the total ATP content from all types of samples. However, it cannot be used to measure the synthesis of newly formed ATP in the presence of physiological concentrations of background ATP (mM) which is ~1000-fold too high. Consequently, very little to no ATP is present in kinetic ATP synthesis measurements that are followed by this method. <italic toggle="yes">(II)</italic> The problem is circumvented by the second method, where radioactively labeled <sup>32</sup>PO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup> or <sup>33</sup>PO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup> is added to the solution. Newly synthesized ATP will incorporate radiolabeled phosphate, while the already present ATP is not radioactive. After separation of excess labeled phosphate by extraction, the newly formed ATP can be quantified via autoradiography or scintillation counting. The drawbacks are the use of radioactive substances and the laborious workup that prohibits continuous measurements. <italic toggle="yes">(III)</italic> The third method is based on the conversion of produced ATP by coupled enzymatic reactions that can be detected by spectroscopy, e.g. addition of D-glucose in the presence of hexokinase and subsequent dephosphorylation by glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase in the presence of NAD(P) that is reduced to NAD(P)H and can be followed spectroscopically. Similar to luciferase, this method cannot discriminate between present or freshly synthesized ATP and is less sensitive than the other methods. (IV) A further and more indirect technique originally developed by Chance and colleagues<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR22">22</xref></sup> to quantify ATP synthesis is to measure the alkalization in the surrounding buffer solution during ATP synthesis. During ATP formation from ADP and P<sub>i</sub>, a water molecule is formed that requires uptake of a proton from solution that can be measured either by a pH electrode or a pH sensitive dye such as phenol red. This approach is insensitive towards high concentrations of nucleotides and has successfully been used. However, it comes with limitations that have to be carefully addressed, such as low buffering capacity, limited pH range, and contribution of other proton dependent processes<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par5">In the present work, we describe a modification of the luciferase/luciferin technique that allows rapid continous measurement of the ATP synthesis rate with a high time resolution at physiological (millimolar range) concentrations of ATP. The principle is based on the properties of a modified ATP, adenosine-5′-O-(1-thiotriphosphate), or ATPαS, which is a hydrolytic substrate for the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase, but is not detected by the luciferin/luciferase system. With the developed method, we were able to measure and compare the ATP synthesis in inverted <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> membrane vesicles as a function of ADP, ATPαS, and inorganic phosphate (P<sub>i</sub>) concentrations. We observe that physiological concentrations of ATP (5 mM), under the form of ATPαS, reduce the ATP synthesis rate to ~10%, and that this rate can be restored using elevated ADP concentrations, but not P<sub>i</sub>, suggesting that the ATP synthesis is controlled by the ATP/ADP ratio and not by the ATP/(ADP∙P<sub>i</sub>) ratio. The applicability of the technique to more isolated systems was confirmed in ATP synthesis experiments in liposomes containing purified <italic toggle="yes">bo</italic><sub>3</sub> oxidase and ATP synthase from <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>.</p></sec><sec id="Sec2" sec-type="results"><title>Results</title><sec id="Sec3"><title>A modified luciferin/luciferase assay</title><p id="Par6">The widely used reaction of D-luciferin with ATP, catalyzed by the luciferase from firefly, suffers from the drawback that it cannot discriminate between already present and freshly synthesized ATP. In addition, the high sensitivity of the assay impairs the use of ATP concentrations higher than a few micromolar, which is ~1000-fold less than what is found <italic toggle="yes">in vivo</italic>. The luciferase first catalyzes the binding of D-luciferin to the α-phosphate of ATP-Mg<sup>2+</sup>, forming PP<sub>i</sub>-Mg<sup>2+</sup> and luciferyl-AMP, which is subsequently oxidized by oxygen to form AMP, CO<sub>2</sub> and oxyluciferin in an excited state that emits a photon while returning to the ground state (Supplementary Fig. <xref rid="MOESM1" ref-type="media">S1</xref>, for a review, see<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR33">33</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR34">34</xref></sup>). ATPases like the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase, however, cleave ATP between the β- and the γ-phosphate, leaving ADP and P<sub>i</sub> behind. We therefore reasoned that the use of an α-phosphate modified ATP prohibits the reaction with the luciferase but allows the reaction with ATPases. Indeed, commercially available ATPαS, in which the double-bonded oxygen on the α-phosphate is substituted for a sulfur, has been shown not to be a substrate for the luciferase in the deoxy form<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR35">35</xref></sup>, but is hydrolyzed by ATPases including the chloroplast F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR36">36</xref></sup> and the F<sub>1</sub> ATPase from <italic toggle="yes">Bacillus</italic> PS3<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par7">We tested this hypothesis experimentally by comparing the reaction of ATP and ATPαS with the firefly luciferase/luciferin supplied as a mixture in a commercial kit (CLS II, Roche) that is used by many to detect ATP synthesis. A typical amount of the assay was mixed with buffer and varying amounts of normal ATP or ATPαS were added to reach a final nucleotide concentration ranging from 10<sup>−12</sup> M up to 5 × 10<sup>−3</sup> M. The observed signal in the reaction with ATPαS is due either to reaction with ATPαS (with a high K<sub>m</sub> or low V<sub>max</sub>) or to contaminating ATP in the ATPαS preparation. We also found that ATPαS concentrations above 100 µM decreased the overall luciferase signal (&lt;50% at 1 mM), probably due to competition of ATPαS with ATP for the luciferase binding site. Experimentally, this effect is addressed by adding a known concentration of ATP (2.5 µM in our experiments) to relate the arbitrary units of the luminescent signal to the concentration of ATP for the quantification of ATP production. We show that the sulfur-substituted alpha phosphate indeed barely reacts with luciferin. The observed signal increase upon rising ATPαS concentration (~10<sup>4</sup> times lower than ATP) is due either to a very slow reaction of ATPαS with luciferin or to slight ATP contamination in the ATPαS preparation. ATPαS binding to luciferase/luciferin as a competitive inhibitor is likely, as the overall signal for a constant amount of ATP is decreasing with increasing concentrations of ATPαS. Technically, this effect was compensated by the addition of a precisely known, suitable amount of ATP for every measurement to quantify the luminescence signal. In contrast to its reaction with luciferin/luciferase, ATPαS behaved similarly to ATP when used to drive ATP hydrolysis with the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase. As with the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> enzyme in chloroplasts and the isolated F<sub>1</sub> part of the ATP synthase of <italic toggle="yes">Bacillus</italic> PS3, ATP hydrolysis and proton pumping kinetics with the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> enzyme were comparable. In the reaction with ATPαS, V<sub>max</sub> was ~75% of the one found for ATP and both had a similar K<sub>m</sub>’ of ~200–300 µM in the absence of ADP. Earlier, Löbau <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. reported an apparent K<sub>m</sub>(MgATP) = 140 µM for <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR45">45</xref></sup>. Data for ATPαS with intact F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase is missing, but in elegant single-molecule experiments following the rotation of the gamma subunit, Noji and coworkers have found an apparent K<sub>m</sub> (91 µM) with the purified F<sub>1</sub> ATPase from <italic toggle="yes">Bacillus</italic> PS3 lacking the regulatory epsilon subunit<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR37">37</xref></sup>. In comparison to published values, the Km values reported here were ~2-fold higher, which is likely due to different assay conditions (e.g. buffer, Mg<sup>2+</sup> concentration) and different enzyme preparations from different organisms. However, given the similar behavior within our preparation, it was concluded that ATPαS can be used to replace background ATP in ATP synthesis experiments. In the past, ATP synthesis experiments in the presence of high ATP were performed following the incorporation of radioactive <sup>32</sup>PO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> or <sup>33</sup>PO<sub>4</sub><sup>2-</sup> into newly formed ATP.</p></sec><sec id="Sec8"><title>The ATP synthesis rate is strongly reduced by an increasing ATP/ADP ratio</title><p id="Par15">The physiological nucleotide concentration ratio (ATP/ADP ~20–1000) strongly favors ATP hydrolysis (ATP/ADP at equilibrium is ~10<sup>−5</sup>). However, the presence of respiratory proton pumps (e.g. <italic toggle="yes">bo</italic><sub>3</sub> oxidase in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>) generating and maintaining a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> are able to shift the net reaction towards ATP synthesis. Typical <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> ATP synthesis studies nevertheless employ conditions where no ATP is present due to the incompatibility with the luciferase assay, while ADP is used in excess to maximize the observed rates. In this report, we describe the use of ATPαS to overcome this limitation and have investigated the effect of cellular concentrations of ATPαS (as a substrate analogue of ATP) on the enzymatic reaction of the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase directly. Similar measurements have been performed in intact mitochondria, but the interpretation of results is complicated by the presence of <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> driven phosphate and nucleotide carriers from the SLC25 family that transport these substrates from the bulk to the site of ATP synthesis in the mitochondrial matrix. Consequently, the different substrates added to the outside affect the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase only indirectly. By contrast, no such transporters are present in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> and the complete inversion of protein orientation in IMVs allows direct access of the experimentally added substrates ATP, ADP and P<sub>i</sub> to the catalytic sites in the F<sub>1</sub> part of the ATP synthase.</p><p id="Par16">The influence of the cytoplasmic ATP/ADP ratio on the ATP synthesis rate had been investigated with intact mitochondria by e.g. Küster <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR24">24</xref></sup> and LaNoue <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>.<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref></sup>. Küster <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. report that the extra-mitochondrial ATP/ADP ratio controls the ATP synthesis rate, dropping steeply after a ratio of ~5 with essentially no ATP synthesis at a ratio of 100. LaNoue <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. report that ATP synthesis and ATP hydrolysis are only in equilibrium at low <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, while at high <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> values, the increased net ATP synthesis is due to nearly blocked ATP hydrolysis rather than an increased ATP synthesis rate. Both studies confirm a kinetic control of the ATP synthesis rate with the ATP/ADP translocase being the controlling module<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR46">46</xref></sup>. Omitting the added complexity of the ATP/ADP translocase, Ferguson and colleagues investigated cells and membranes of the gram negative bacterium <italic toggle="yes">Paracoccus denitrificans</italic><sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR47">47</xref></sup>. In their studies, they find that membrane vesicles of <italic toggle="yes">P. denitrificans</italic> are not capable of ATP hydrolysis, neither under high or low <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> conditions, and conclude that the kinetic control of ATP synthesis is an intrinsic property of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase. There is an ongoing discussion to determine if the backwards reaction in <italic toggle="yes">P. denitrificans</italic> is blocked by the ζ subunit or not<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR6">6</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR7">7</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR48">48</xref></sup>, or if the inhibitory effect is caused by Mg-ADP inhibition, as proposed earlier<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR49">49</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par17">With the present study, we can add information to the situation in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, which represents a mixed situation. Like <italic toggle="yes">P. denitrificans</italic>, no ATP/ADP translocase is present, allowing a more direct investigation of the properties of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase. However, in contrast to <italic toggle="yes">P. denitrificans</italic>, membranes of <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> show no respiratory coupling and the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase is fully active in ATP hydrolysis direction<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR32">32</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR50">50</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR51">51</xref></sup>. In our measurements with a constant amount of ADP and phosphate, an increasing ATPαS concentration (and thus ATP/ADP ratio) provokes a sharp decrease of ATP synthesis over an ATP/ADP ratio of 1, with 50% activity at ratio of ~20 (1 mM ATPαS) and &lt;10% activity at a ratio of 100 (5 mM ATPαS), which is in good agreement with the data from mitochondria. As the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> in our experiments is unchanged (no notable effect of increasing nucleotide concentrations on respiratory activity were found), the data indicates that the observed effect is either due to product inhibition by the presence of ATPαS or by an increased rate of ATP hydrolysis. Since our assay directly detects newly formed ATP with a very high time resolution and an immediate decrease in ATP synthesis rate is observed, we can confirm that a direct competition of ATPαS and ADP indeed takes place. We cannot rule out an increased ATP hydrolysis rate, but the effective ATPαS concentrations (&gt;1 mM) are well above the apparent K<sub>m</sub> of ATP for ATP hydrolysis, which however could be increased in the presence of a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>. In similar experiments, where ADP was raised in parallel to keep constant the ATPαS/ADP ratio and mass-action ratio (Γ’ = 100 M<sup>−1</sup>), the inhibitory effect of ATPαS was only mildly observed, suggesting that the competition of ADP and ATPαS indeed control the rate of ATP synthesis. A more detailed interpretation of these results is complicated by the presence of three catalytic sites that have different affinities for ATP and ADP depending on their conformation state (tight, loose, and empty)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref></sup>. Finally, by varying the ATP and P<sub>i</sub> concentrations in parallel, resulting in the same Γ’ = 100 M<sup>−1</sup>, but increasing the ATP/ADP ratio, we found that a variation of the phosphate concentration could not prevent the inhibitory effect, indicating that ATP synthesis is not controlled by the phosphorylation potential variable [ATP]/([ADP]∙[P<sub>i</sub>]), but rather by the ATP/ADP ratio. The inhibitory effect of ATPαS at higher phosphate concentrations was even more pronounced (&lt;5% residual activity at 5 mM ATPαS), which can be attributed to a slight inhibitory effect of P<sub>i</sub> concentrations &gt;100 mM.</p><p id="Par18">In respiring <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, the ATP and ADP concentrations have been found to be ~3.5 mM and 0.12 mM, respectively, yielding an ATP/ADP ratio of ~30<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref></sup>. From our measurements, we can thus assume that despite saturating <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> synthesizes ATP with a rate of only ~20% of v<sub>max</sub>. Etzold <italic toggle="yes">et al</italic>. determined a maximal turnover rate of 270 ATP s<sup>−1</sup> per ATP synthase in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> at 37 °C<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR54">54</xref></sup>. However, in their measurements, 95% of the ATP synthases were blocked to maximize the available driving force per F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase. Furthermore, their experiments were performed in the presence of 1 mM ADP and the total absence of ATP (ATP/ADP = 0). It can thus be estimated that the ATP synthesis rates is only 5 to 10 ATP s<sup>−1</sup> per F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase in growing <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>. The observed necessary concentrations of ADP &gt;1 mM to restore maximal ATP synthesis are not physiologically relevant. However, the nearly universal ATP hydrolysis inhibition mechanism by Mg-ADP<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR52">52</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR55">55</xref>–<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR59">59</xref></sup> could be responsible for the apparent absence of ATP hydrolysis in our experiments. We have thus measured the inhibitory effect of ADP on proton pumping on the same vesicles as used for ATP synthesis. The data from Fig. <xref rid="Fig4" ref-type="fig">4c</xref> show that the apparent K<sub>i</sub> of ADP increases from ~100 µM to ~400 µM in the presence of 50 µM ATP or 2.5 mM ATP, respectively. An even higher K<sub>i</sub> is expected during ATP synthesis under high <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, that has been shown to counteract ADP inhibition of ATP hydrolysis<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR19">19</xref>,<xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR57">57</xref></sup>, and hence being considerably higher compared to the ADP concentrations found in growing cells (~120 µM)<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR53">53</xref></sup>. ADP concentrations &gt;400 µM, however will be easily reached when the <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> is low and ATP is converted to ADP, and the mechanism thus serves as effective protection against cellular depletion of ATP. Finally, our results seem to be independent of the reported inhibition of ATP hydrolysis by the ε subunit, as for the <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> enzyme it has been shown to be absent in the presence of a <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> and independent of the ATP concentration<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR14">14</xref></sup>. In the future, it will be interesting to investigate, if ATP synthesis in other organisms than <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> is similarly dependent on the ATP concentration.</p></sec></sec><sec id="Sec9"><title>Concluding Remarks</title><p id="Par19">In this work, we have introduced a novel, alternative, non-radioactive method to measure ATP synthesis <italic toggle="yes">in vitro</italic> under physiological nucleotide concentrations and high <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic>, using ATPαS as a replacement for ATP. The method allows to selectively measure newly produced ATP and is unaffected by the presence of high concentrations of ATPαS. The luciferase setup allows rapid and continuous measurements, avoiding the necessity of taking samples at distinct time points, thus considerably increasing time resolution in measurements.</p><p id="Par20">We have applied this method to inverted membrane vesicles of <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> and proteoliposomes containing F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase and <italic toggle="yes">bo</italic><sub>3</sub> oxidase and find that the ATP/ADP ratio, but not ATP/(ADP∙P<sub>i</sub>), controls the rate of ATP synthesis. The effect is based on a direct competition between of ATPαS and ADP for the catalytic binding sites. From this, we can conclude that the physiological ATP concentration prevents a maximal ATP synthesis rate despite saturating <italic toggle="yes">pmf</italic> conditions. Finally, our results suggest that Mg-ADP inhibition of ATP hydrolysis is not relevant to maximize ATP synthesis rates in <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic>, but is an effective tool to prevent total loss of cellular ATP during energy starvation.</p></sec><sec id="Sec10" sec-type="materials|methods"><title>Material and Methods</title><p id="Par21">Chemicals and kits were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and Thermo Scientific if not otherwise stated.</p><sec id="Sec11"><title>Growth conditions of bacterial cells</title><p id="Par22"><italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strain DK8, lacking the <italic toggle="yes">unc</italic> operon coding for its ATP synthase, was transformed with a pBWU13 plasmid and grown at 37 °C in LB-Lennox medium containing 50 μg/ml ampicillin.</p></sec><sec id="Sec12"><title>Preparation of Escherichia coli DK8:pBWU13 inverted membrane vesicles</title><p id="Par23">The following procedure was performed at 4 °C. Bacterial cells were suspended in 10 ml/g HMG buffer (20 mM Hepes pH 7.5, 2 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, and 10% glycerol), supplemented with a tiny spatula tip of Pefabloc serine protease inhibitor, phenylmethanesulfonyl fluoride and DNAse I, and broken with a French pressure cell press or a Microfluidizer. Unbroken cells were collected by centrifugation at 8000 <italic toggle="yes">g</italic> for 10 min. The supernatant containing the membrane vesicles was centrifuged at high speed (200′000 <italic toggle="yes">g</italic>) for 1 h, resuspended in 5 ml HMG supplemented with 0.5% cholate per gram of initial cells, to remove proteins loosely associated with the membrane, and centrifuged again at high speed. The membrane vesicles were resuspended in HMG and centrifuged a third time at high speed to remove traces of cholate. They were then resuspended in HMG to a concentration of 10 mg/ml total proteins (quantified with a bicinchoninic acid assay), aliquoted and flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen for storage at −80 °C.</p></sec><sec id="Sec13"><title>Purification of F1F0 ATP synthase from <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic></title><p id="Par24">The F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase of <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> strain DK8:pBWU13 was purified as previously described<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR26">26</xref></sup>.</p></sec><sec id="Sec14"><title>ATP hydrolysis assay by ACMA quenching</title><p id="Par25">With this method we indirectly measure the hydrolytic activity of the F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub>-ATPase by following the establishment of the resulting proton gradient, using the self-quenching property of the fluorescent dye 9-Amino-6-Chloro-2-Methoxyacridine (ACMA). The fluorescence of this membrane permeable dye quenches when a proton gradient forms across a lipid compartment<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR60">60</xref></sup>.</p><p id="Par26">The fluorescence of the dye 9-Amino-6-Chloro-2-Methoxyacridine (ACMA) (excitation wavelength: 410 nm; emission wavelength: 480 nm) was monitored with a Cary Eclipse Fluorescence Spectrophotometer from Agilent Technologies. A cuvette with stirring magnet was filled with HMK buffer (10 mM Hepes-KOH pH 7.5, 1 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 10 mM KNO<sub>3</sub>), 150 μg/ml inverted bacterial membrane vesicles and 2 μM ACMA in a final volume of 1 ml. The fluorescence baseline, taken as 100% fluorescence to determine the percentage of quenching, was measured and hydrolysis was started by the addition of 1–500 μM ATP or ATPαS. The pH gradient established by the ATP synthase quenched the ACMA dye proportionally to the hydrolytic activity and was eventually dissipated by the addition of 20 mM NH<sub>4</sub>Cl.</p><p id="Par27">To assess the effect of valinomycin and the coupling of IMVs, a buffer containing 10 mM MOPS-KOH pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, and 2.5 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub> was used instead of HMK. The measurement was carried as above, with or without 1 μM valinomycin, and started with 500 μM ATP.</p></sec><sec id="Sec15"><title>ATP hydrolysis assay by phenol red spectrophotometry</title><p id="Par28">Phenol red is a pH-sensitive dye which can be used to detect the dissociation of protons in the ATP hydrolysis reaction<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR38">38</xref></sup>, at pH &gt; 7.2: ATP<sup>4−</sup> + H<sub>2</sub>O ⇌ ADP<sup>3−</sup> + P<sub>i</sub><sup>2−</sup> + H<sup>+</sup> (see for example<sup><xref ref-type="bibr" rid="CR61">61</xref></sup>).</p><p id="Par29">Phenol red buffer (0.5 mM Hepes-KOH pH 8, 100 mM KCl, 4 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 0.1 mM EDTA, 10% glycerol, 0.02% triton X-100) was mixed with 25 μM phenol red and desired concentrations of ADP, ATP and ATPαS, to a final volume of 1 ml. The pH was adjusted to 8 with KOH and the baseline absorbance was recorded at 557 nm with a Cary 60 UV-Vis spectrophotometer. 20 nmol HCl were used for standardization and 5 μg <italic toggle="yes">E. coli</italic> purified F<sub>1</sub>F<sub>0</sub> ATP synthase were added to start the reaction.</p></sec><sec id="Sec16"><title>Luciferin/luciferase reaction with ATP versus ATPαS</title><p id="Par30">The firefly enzyme luciferase drives an ATP-dependent reaction in two steps to produce light that is proportional to the amount of ATP present and can be measured with a luminometer.</p><p id="Par31">To compare the reactivity of ATP <italic toggle="yes">versus</italic> ATPαS with the luciferin/luciferase system, 200 μg/ml luciferase reagent (containing luciferin, luciferase and magnesium) of an ATP Bioluminescence Assay Kit CLS II from Roche Applied Science was added to 50 mM Hepes-NaOH pH 7.5, 10 mM total phosphate and 3 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, and the baseline was measured. 1 μM to 5 mM ATP or ATPαS was then added and the luminescence measured.</p></sec><sec id="Sec17"><title>Luciferin/luciferase ATP synthesis assay</title><p id="Par32">The assay was performed in clear 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes in a final volume of 100 μl. 50 mM Hepes-NaOH pH 7.5 were used as buffer and the assay mixture contained 10 mM MgCl<sub>2</sub>, 200 μg/ml luciferase reagent, 40 μg/ml inverted bacterial membrane vesicles, 2 mM (saturating) DTT and variable concentrations of ATPαS, ADP (typically 50–100 μM) and total phosphate (typically 10 mM). The luminescence was monitored with a Promega GloMax luminometer in steps of 30 s, allowing additions of relevant reagents to the tube when required.</p><p id="Par33">The baseline was first measured (30 s), before 250 pmol ATP were added as a calibration standard (30 s). Ubiquinone Q<sub>1</sub>, an ethanol soluble analogue of ubiquinone that can be reduced by DTT, was used to energize the membrane by <italic toggle="yes">bo3</italic> oxidase proton pumping and start the ATP synthesis (3 × 30 s). ATP synthesis could be interrupted by the addition of 400 nM potassium cyanide or 10 μM CCCP (2 × 30 s).</p></sec></sec><sec sec-type="supplementary-material"><title>Supplementary information</title><sec id="Sec18"><p>
