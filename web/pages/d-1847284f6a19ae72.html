# Ecological community control: invasion criteria, chemostat reconstruction, and scope

## Verdict

The strongest actionable mathematical interface found is the invasion-graph permanence theorem of Hofbauer and Schreiber (2022), together with Patel and Schreiber's (2018) finite-time average-Lyapunov criterion. They connect an externally chosen periodic environment to persistence through measurable boundary growth rates. The falsifier is quantitative: on a terminal boundary community, if every missing species has negative long-run per-capita growth, then permanence fails; under the paper's regularity assumptions this is stated explicitly using a stable-manifold argument. Positive invasion rates at all required terminal communities imply robust permanence when the invasion graph is acyclic and the paper's sign-consistency assumptions hold.

This run did not prove a new permanence theorem. It independently reconstructed the published periodic-chemostat example and numerical thresholds, and found a discrepancy in the published enumeration of its terminal communities. That discrepancy is a finite numerical audit, not a refutation of the theorem: the additional candidate community is itself invadable. It means the article's complete boundary sign table and its source parameter transcription should be checked before relying on the worked example as a turnkey certificate.

A second, distinct Astra candidate (N33) covers arbitrary measurable rate schedules for finite endotactic power-law reaction diagrams, with a common compact absorber and finite rate-path-independent entry bound on each positive stoichiometric class. It is stronger with respect to schedule generality, but it is not the Monod chemostat model below, is source-derived and externally unreviewed, and has no useful effective entry-time or certificate-synthesis bound. The existing stochastic Astra cards stop at finite-horizon safety and do not prove stochastic permanence.

## The control theorem and its experimentally visible interface

Consider ecological equations

\[
\dot x_i=x_i f_i(x,y),\qquad \dot y=g(x,y),
\]

with locally Lipschitz dynamics and a compact absorbing set. Patel and Schreiber, Theorem 3.2, assume the extinction-set attractor has a finite Morse decomposition. For each Morse set \(M_k\), if there are positive weights \(p_{ki}\) such that for every \(z\in M_k\) there is a finite \(T_z>0\) satisfying

\[
\sum_i p_{ki}\int_0^{T_z} f_i(z\cdot t)\,dt>0,
\]

then the system is permanent. Their Corollary 3.3 gives a falsifier: if the corresponding weighted integral is negative from every point of one Morse set, the extinction set attracts and the system is not permanent. The theorem establishes existence of a persistence lower bound, but does not provide a generally usable numerical value for that bound. It is robust to sufficiently small perturbations of the dynamics under the paper's compactness hypotheses.

Hofbauer and Schreiber make the community-assembly step explicit. Their model is \(\dot x_i=x_i f_i(z),\dot y=g(z)\), with (A1) locally Lipschitz dynamics, (A2) dissipativity, and (A3) (a) nonzero invasion rates for every missing species on every boundary ergodic measure and (b) sign consistency for a missing species across ergodic measures with the same resident support. If the invasion graph is acyclic, their Theorem 1 says robust permanence follows when every boundary subcommunity is invadable; Corollary 1 reduces this sufficient check to the “−i communities,” where species \(i\) is absent and every other absent species has nonpositive (hence, under A3a, negative) invasion rate. Their Remark 3 says that a twice continuously differentiable model with a boundary community at which all missing species have negative invasion rates is not permanent.

For a periodic intervention, the environment phase can be included as a variable on a circle. For a resident periodic orbit of period \(T\), the missing-species rate is directly observable as

\[
r_i=\frac1T\int_0^T f_i(z(t))\,dt
   =\frac1T\log\frac{x_i(T)}{x_i(0)}
\]

in the rare-invader linearization. In a chemostat, this becomes the measured integral of strain growth minus dilution. One can test the sign by introducing a calibrated rare inoculum into a phase-locked resident cycle, measuring abundance over repeated cycles, and estimating the log-growth slope. A negative slope at a terminal community falsifies permanence; the full positive-sign conclusion requires all relevant boundary communities, sign consistency, and acyclicity. Sampling only the stable face attractors is insufficient if other ergodic boundary measures exist.

The sinusoidal forcing considered below is smooth and fits the phase-lift interface. Arbitrary measurable intervention histories do not automatically fit that autonomous locally Lipschitz environment model. Astra N33 has broader measurable-time quantifiers, but on a different reaction-system interface.

The acyclicity hypothesis is load-bearing. In the cyclic May–Leonard competition system

\[
\dot x_1=x_1(1-x_1-\beta x_2-\alpha x_3),\quad
\dot x_2=x_2(1-\alpha x_1-x_2-\beta x_3),\quad
\dot x_3=x_3(1-\beta x_1-\alpha x_2-x_3),
\]

take \(\alpha=0.8\), \(\beta=1.3\), so \(\alpha+\beta>2\). At each single-species equilibrium, one absent species has invasion rate \(1-\alpha=+0.2\) and the other has rate \(1-\beta=-0.3\). Yet the boundary dynamics approach a rock–paper–scissors heteroclinic cycle and the system is not permanent. This is an exact counterexample to promoting “every singleton resident is invadable” into a persistence guarantee without checking the whole invasion graph and its cycles. It is the kind of failure the invasion-graph theorem is designed to exclude.

## Reconstructed chemostat test case

Hofbauer and Schreiber's application, attributed to Wolkowicz and Zhao, uses

\[
\dot R=(R_0-R)D(t)-\sum_i \mu_i(R)X_i,\qquad
\dot X_i=X_i(\mu_i(R)-D(t)),
\]

where \(D(t)=D_0+a\cos(\omega t)\), \(\mu_i(R)=\alpha_iR/(\beta_i+R)\), and

\[
R_0=11,\quad D_0=0.4675,\quad \omega=0.2,\quad
\alpha=(1,0.7,0.64),\quad \beta=(1,0.3,0.2).
\]

The figure caption gives \(\beta_2=+0.3\), which is the value used here and is consistent with the stated type-II response. The article's body instead prints \(\beta_2=-0.3\), which makes the stated response singular at positive resource \(R=0.3\). This is a source transcription conflict that needs checking against the original 1998 paper before treating the example as a formal reproduction.

The compactness premise can be checked directly. With \(Z=R+X_1+X_2+X_3\),

\[
\dot Z=D(t)(R_0-Z).
\]

For the amplitudes considered, \(a<D_0\), so dilution stays positive, \(Z\) is ultimately bounded, and adjoining the periodic phase gives a compact absorbing phase-space region. The per-capita invasion rate on a resident ergodic orbit is \(r_i=\int(\mu_i(R)-D(\theta))\,d\mu\).

The fixed-step RK4 reproduction integrates residents and three accumulated log-invasion coordinates over a period \(T=2\pi/\omega=31.4159265\). It gives:

| Dilution amplitude | Resident boundary orbit | Missing-strain rates per unit time | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(a=0.300\) | \(\{1,2\}\) | \(r_3=+2.4657387\times10^{-5}\) | Barely positive invasion margin |
| \(a=0.300\) | \(\{1,3\}\) | \(r_2=+3.6260939\times10^{-5}\) | Barely positive invasion margin |
| \(a=0.300\) | \(\{2\}\) | \(r_1=+2.1580957\times10^{-2}\), \(r_3=-3.4700641\times10^{-3}\) | Additional candidate “−1 community” |
| \(a=0.325\) | \(\{1,2\}\) | \(r_3=-1.9908408\times10^{-4}\) | Species 3 cannot invade this boundary orbit |
| \(a=0.275\) | \(\{1,3\}\) | \(r_2=-1.2608377\times10^{-4}\) | Species 2 cannot invade this boundary orbit |
| \(a=0.200\) | \(\{3\}\) | \(r_1=-9.5736727\times10^{-3}\), \(r_2=-1.6257236\times10^{-3}\) | Both missing strains decline at this boundary orbit |

Across 960–1920 RK4 steps per period, the displayed invasion-rate values for the key pair orbits changed by less than about \(10^{-10}\) per time unit. These are finite numerical diagnostics, not interval-certified signs. At \(a=0.300\), the positive \(r_3\) rate on \(\{1,2\}\) is only about a \(0.0775\%\) increase per forcing cycle. At \(a=0.325\), the negative rate corresponds to about a \(0.624\%\) decline per cycle. Near the reported threshold, a lab would need repeated cycles and precise abundance measurements; the sign may be smaller than strain-to-strain and batch variation.

The direct full-community RK4 trajectory was not converged in this run: at \(a=0.300\), its period-map residual was \(1.02\times10^{-4}\) after the 500-cycle cap. This computation did not itself demonstrate an interior periodic coexistence orbit; it reconstructed boundary rates for the invasion criterion.

Bisection on the two reported boundary rates estimates zeros at \(a\approx0.3031558\) for \(r_3\) on \(\{1,2\}\), and \(a\approx0.29493995\) for \(r_2\) on \(\{1,3\}\). The interval between these two values is a candidate coexistence window only conditional on all other signs, uniqueness/sign consistency of resident ergodic measures, and an acyclic invasion graph. It is not a proved threshold or an experimental prediction with uncertainty bounds.

The paper reports only two “−i communities” at \(a=0.3\): \(\{1,2\}\) and \(\{1,3\}\). Under the caption's positive \(\beta_2\) parameters, the reduced species-2 resident problem has the invariant eventual plane \(R+X_2=11\). A 2,001-start sweep of its scalar Poincaré map found the positive periodic root \(R(0)=4.86941182, X_2(0)=6.13058818\), in addition to the washout root. The computed orbit has \(r_3<0\) and \(r_1>0\); a two-resident face run beginning with species 2 and 3 converged numerically to support \(\{2\}\). By the paper's stated definition, that support also qualifies as a candidate “−1 community.” Its invader, species 1, has positive rate, so this extra node does not reverse the reported permanence conclusion. It does conflict with “only two” and should be resolved by independently checking the article's numerical workflow and the \(\beta_2\) sign.

The mean-matched constant-dilution control distinguishes temporal control from average dilution. For \(a=0\), the single-strain break-even resource levels \(R_i^*=\beta_iD_0/(\alpha_i-D_0)\) are approximately \((0.877934,0.603226,0.542029)\), so strain 3 has the lowest threshold and is predicted to exclude the others. The periodic example instead has positive invasion rates on the reported two-species boundaries. This is consistent with timing and resource feedback changing interactions in a nonlinear Monod model. It does not contradict Patel–Schreiber Proposition 5.1, whose averaging result is for a fixed-interaction Lotka–Volterra form with environmental variation in the intrinsic-growth vector; the Monod chemostat is outside that form.

An experimental control protocol is therefore concrete but conditional: fit \(\alpha_i,\beta_i\) and measure the actual dilution trace; phase-lock the resident cycles; measure rare-invader log slopes on the relevant boundary communities; compare sinusoidal schedules to a constant schedule with the same mean dilution; and vary amplitude around the two candidate zeros. A decisive falsifier is a reliably negative terminal invasion slope where the model predicts positive, or a reliably positive slope where the claimed nonpermanence prediction requires negative. The existing 2022 glucose-pulsed \(E. coli\)–\(S. cerevisiae\) co-culture experiment shows that resource pulses can stabilize a continuous-bioreactor co-culture through successive diauxic shifts, but its cross-feeding/resource-use mechanism is not this one-resource Monod model and does not validate these numerical thresholds.

N33's clean failure tests are useful for intervention design: for \(0\rightleftarrows A\), dropping the positive lower rate bound by setting inflow \(1/(1+t)\) and outflow 1 gives \(x'=(1+t)^{-1}-x\) and extinction; dropping the upper bound with inflow \(e^t\) gives an unbounded solution. Its different-span switching counterexample shows why independently switching between reaction subsystems requires a common stoichiometric span for classwise absorption. The line-by-line proof also emphasizes that arbitrary stochastic jump control and unrestricted exogenous forcing do not follow from N33.

## Falsification and next gate

The cheapest decisive check is not a large community simulation. Resolve the parameter transcription against the 1998 primary paper, then independently compute all ergodic supports and invasion signs for the pinned 2022 parameter set, including species-2-only and all other boundary faces. Specifically:

1. Establish whether the figure-caption value \(\beta_2=+0.3\) is intended and whether the species-2-only orbit is an attracting resident cycle on the \(x_1=0\) face.
2. Use interval arithmetic or a validated Poincaré-map enclosure to certify signs of each reported invasion exponent and isolate the amplitude roots. Ordinary fixed-step RK4 is not sufficient to prove a small positive sign.
3. Verify A3b over all ergodic measures with the same support and prove the invasion graph has no directed cycle. A numerical attracting-orbit table alone does not establish these assumptions.
4. For a real microbiome or engineered consortium, acquire growth-response data and phase-resolved dilution/resource trajectories; then test the predicted rare-invader slopes against a mean-matched constant-control condition.

Until these gates pass, the correct status is: established external theorem; source-reported numerical example; internally reconstructed finite numerical diagnostics; `UNKNOWN` on a complete validated sign table and experimentally verified control window. The strongest negative result is the explicit theorem-level boundary: sign checks on a selected collection of observed attractors are not enough if unobserved ergodic boundary measures or invasion cycles remain possible.
