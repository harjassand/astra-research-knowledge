A solution to a strengthened conjecture of Bukh, van Hintum and Keevash on additive bases
Zixiang Xu
††thanks: Email: zxxu8023@qq.com.
Abstract
Motivated by the change-of-domain problem for additive bases, Bukh, van Hintum and Keevash conjectured that if A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{n} and
\{\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}:1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}\subseteq A+B,
then |A|+|B|\geq 2n. They further proposed the strengthened conjecture: if
|A|=n-t, then |B|\geq n+\binom{t+1}{2}.
Bukh also explicitly asked whether the same bounds hold for A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} and an arbitrary basis S of \mathbb{R}^{n}, under the assumption S+S\subseteq A+B.
We prove the full strengthened statement over \mathbb{R}^{n}: if S+S\subseteq A+B and |A|\leq n-t with 0\leq t\leq n-1, then
|B|\geq n+\binom{t+1}{2},
which is sharp for every basis S and every 0\leq t\leq n-1.
The proof is short, using edge contractions in a graph-theoretical framework and a new coloring lemma over \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.
1 Introduction
A basic topic in additive number theory and additive combinatorics is to understand how efficiently a set can be generated by sums. Classical instances include Waring’s problem [4, 8] and Goldbach-type problems [5, 7, 12]. In finite settings, initiated by Erdős and Newman [6], one asks how small a set B can be while still satisfying A\subseteq B+B, or more generally A\subseteq kB. This viewpoint has led to connections with extremal and geometric problems in additive combinatorics, including discrete Kakeya-type phenomena [1]; see also [9, 11].
Recently, Bukh, van Hintum and Keevash [3], motivated by questions of Ruzsa [10], studied a natural change-of-domain problem: how does the minimum size of an additive basis change when the basis is required to lie in a more restrictive domain? In the rational-versus-integer setting, they proved that every rational basis of size n can be replaced by an integer basis of size at most 2n, and constructed examples showing that this factor 2 is tight up to a lower-order error.
A key ingredient in their argument is a linear-algebraic vector model. Let \bm{e}_{1},\ldots,\bm{e}_{n} be the standard basis of \mathbb{R}^{n}. In the case of 2-bases, this model asks for the minimum possible value of |A|+|B|, where A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{n} and
\{\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}:1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}\subseteq A+B.
Bukh, van Hintum and Keevash [3] proved the lower bound 2n-O(\sqrt{n}) for |A|+|B| and conjectured that the sharp answer is exactly 2n [3, Conjecture 13]. They further proposed the refined extension that if |A|=n-t, then one should have
|B|\geq n+\binom{t+1}{2}.
On his open problem list, Bukh [2, Problem 12] asked explicitly whether this entire conjectural picture persists over \mathbb{R}^{n}: if S is a basis of \mathbb{R}^{n} and A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n} satisfy S+S\subseteq A+B, do the same bounds still hold?
Our main result answers this question affirmatively, including the refined extension.
Theorem 1.1.
Let S be a basis of \mathbb{R}^{n}, and let A,B\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}. Suppose that
S+S\subseteq A+B.
Let t be an integer with 0\leq t\leq n-1. If
|A|\leq n-t,
then
|B|\geq n+\binom{t+1}{2}.
The bound is sharp for every basis S=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n}\} and 0\leq t\leq n-1. Indeed, take
A=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n-t}\}
and
B=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n}\}\cup\{\bm{s}_{i}+\bm{s}_{j}-\bm{s}_{1}:n-t<i\leq j\leq n\}.
Then |A|=n-t and |B|=n+\binom{t+1}{2}, with the displayed elements of B being distinct by the linear independence of S. Moreover, S+S\subseteq A+B: sums involving one of \bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n-t} are immediate, while the remaining sums are represented as
\bm{s}_{i}+\bm{s}_{j}=\bm{s}_{1}+(\bm{s}_{i}+\bm{s}_{j}-\bm{s}_{1}) for
n-t<i\leq j\leq n.
Since the standard basis of \mathbb{Q}^{n} is also a basis of \mathbb{R}^{n}, Theorem 1.1 immediately yields the vector-model conjecture of Bukh, van Hintum and Keevash [3, Conjecture 13] and its extension.
Corollary 1.2.
Let A,B\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{n}, and suppose that
\{\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}:1\leq i\leq j\leq n\}\subseteq A+B.
If 0\leq t\leq n-1 and |A|\leq n-t, then
|B|\geq n+\binom{t+1}{2}.
Combined with their reduction from the change-of-domain problem to the vector model, Corollary 1.2 shows the factor 2 in [3, Theorem 1] is best possible already for 2-bases: for every n, there exists a set of integers with a rational 2-basis of size at most n whose smallest integer 2-basis has size exactly 2n.
2 Proof of Theorem 1.1
If B is infinite, then the conclusion is immediate. Thus we may assume that B is finite. It suffices to prove the lower bound in the case S=\{\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}\}, where \bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n} are the standard basis vectors. Indeed, if S=\{\bm{s}_{1},\dots,\bm{s}_{n}\} is an arbitrary basis, then there is an invertible linear map sending \bm{s}_{i} to \bm{e}_{i} for every i. Applying this map preserves cardinalities and sumset inclusions.
If n=1, then t=0, and the inclusion S+S\subseteq A+B implies that B is nonempty. Hence |B|\geq 1=n+\binom{t+1}{2}. We may therefore assume that n\geq 2.
For every pair 1\leq i\leq j\leq n, the containment S+S\subseteq A+B guarantees at least one representation of \bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j} as a sum of an element of A and an element of B. Choose and fix one such representation:
\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}=\bm{a}_{ij}+\bm{b}_{ij},\qquad\bm{a}_{ij}\in A,\quad\bm{b}_{ij}\in B.
Let A_{0} be the set of distinct vectors among the \bm{a}_{ij}, and let B_{0} be the set of distinct vectors among the \bm{b}_{ij}. Put
p=|A_{0}| and q=|B_{0}|.
Then
p\leq|A|\leq n-t and q\leq|B|.
We construct a bipartite graph G as follows. Its left vertex set is a copy of A_{0}, and its right vertex set is a copy of B_{0}; these two vertex sets are regarded as disjoint copies, even if the same vector occurs on both sides. For each 1\leq i\leq j\leq n, put an edge \varepsilon_{ij} from the left vertex \bm{a}_{ij} to the right vertex \bm{b}_{ij}. Later contractions may create parallel edges, so we allow multigraphs from this point on.
Define \varphi:V(G)\to\mathbb{R}^{n} by setting \varphi(\bm{a})=\bm{a} for left vertices \bm{a}, and \varphi(\bm{b})=-\bm{b} for right vertices \bm{b}. Then for every 1\leq i\leq j\leq n,
\varphi(\bm{a}_{ij})-\varphi(\bm{b}_{ij})=\bm{a}_{ij}+\bm{b}_{ij}=\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}.
(1)
Claim 2.1.
The subgraph of G whose edge set is
\{\varepsilon_{11},\dots,\varepsilon_{nn}\}
is a forest.
Proof of the claim.
Suppose not. Then this subgraph contains a cycle \bm{v}_{0},\bm{v}_{1},\dots,\bm{v}_{r}=\bm{v}_{0}
whose edges are distinct. For each 1\leq k\leq r, let the edge
\bm{v}_{k-1}\bm{v}_{k} be \varepsilon_{i_{k}i_{k}}.
Since the edges on the cycle are distinct and there is only one diagonal
edge \varepsilon_{ii} for each i, the indices
i_{1},\dots,i_{r} are distinct.
By (1), for each k we have
\varphi(\bm{v}_{k-1})-\varphi(\bm{v}_{k})=\sigma_{k}\cdot 2\bm{e}_{i_{k}}
for some \sigma_{k}\in\{\pm 1\}, depending on which direction we traverse
the edge \varepsilon_{i_{k}i_{k}}. Summing these identities over
k=1,\dots,r, the left-hand side becomes zero. Hence
\sum_{k=1}^{r}\sigma_{k}\cdot 2\bm{e}_{i_{k}}=0.
This is a nontrivial linear relation among the distinct basis vectors
\bm{e}_{i_{1}},\dots,\bm{e}_{i_{r}}, contradicting the
linear independence of
\bm{e}_{1},\dots,\bm{e}_{n}.
∎
Now contract all diagonal edges \varepsilon_{ii}, meaning that each connected component of the subgraph formed by these edges is identified to a single vertex. By Claim 2.1, these n edges form a forest. Hence contracting them reduces the number of vertices by exactly the number of contracted edges. Thus the resulting graph H satisfies
|V(H)|=p+q-n.
(2)
We color the vertices of H by elements of the quotient group \mathbb{R}^{n}/2\mathbb{Z}^{n}. Let
\pi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}/2\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be the quotient map. If X is a vertex of H, choose any vertex \bm{x} of G that was contracted to X, and assign to X the color \pi(\varphi(\bm{x})). This is well-defined. Indeed, if two vertices \bm{x},\bm{y}\in V(G) are contracted to the same vertex of H, then they are connected in G by a path consisting only of diagonal edges. Along each diagonal edge \varepsilon_{ii}, the two \varphi-values differ by \pm 2\bm{e}_{i}. Hence \varphi(\bm{x})-\varphi(\bm{y})\in 2\mathbb{Z}^{n}, and so
\pi(\varphi(\bm{x}))=\pi(\varphi(\bm{y})).
Let \mathcal{C} be the set of colors appearing on the vertices of H, and let \mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{C} be the set of colors of those vertices of H which contain at least one left vertex of G. Since every color in \mathcal{X} is witnessed by at least one left vertex of G, and distinct colors require distinct witnesses, we have
|\mathcal{X}|\leq p\leq n-t.
(3)
For every off-diagonal edge \varepsilon_{ij} with i<j, let X and Y be the vertices of H containing the left endpoint \bm{a}_{ij} and the right endpoint \bm{b}_{ij}, respectively. By (1), the color of X minus the color of Y is
\pi(\varphi(\bm{a}_{ij}))-\pi(\varphi(\bm{b}_{ij}))=\pi(\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}).
Consequently,
\{\pi(\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}):1\leq i<j\leq n\}\subseteq\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{C}.
(4)
We shall take advantage of the following lemma.
Lemma 2.2.
Let n\geq 2 and \Gamma be an abelian group, and let K\leq\Gamma be a subgroup identified with \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}. Write
D_{n}=\{\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}:1\leq i<j\leq n\}\subseteq K.
Let u be an integer with 0\leq u\leq n. Suppose that X\subseteq C\subseteq\Gamma, |X|\leq n-u, and
D_{n}\subseteq X-C.
Then
|C|\geq n+\binom{u}{2}.
Proof of Lemma 2.2.
First suppose that X,C\subseteq K=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}. Since n\geq 2, the set D_{n} is nonempty, and hence the containment D_{n}\subseteq X-C implies that X is nonempty. Choose \bm{x}_{0}\in X and replace X and C by X-\bm{x}_{0} and C-\bm{x}_{0}, respectively. This preserves both the containment X\subseteq C and the difference set X-C, so we may assume that \bm{0}\in X.
Put m=|X|, and let
W=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{F}_{2}}(X).
Since \bm{0}\in X, the space W is generated by the at most m-1 nonzero elements of X, and hence
\dim W\leq m-1.
Let
\rho:\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/W
be the quotient map, and put s=\dim(\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/W). Then
s=n-\dim W\geq n-m+1.
The point of passing to the quotient is that all elements of X collapse to the zero coset. We shall use the following consequence of the covering assumption D_{n}\subseteq X-C.
Claim 2.3.
\rho(D_{n})\subseteq\rho(C).
Proof of the claim.
Let \bm{d}\in D_{n}. By the assumption D_{n}\subseteq X-C, we may write
\bm{d}=\bm{x}-\bm{c}
for some \bm{x}\in X and \bm{c}\in C. Since X\subseteq W, we have \rho(\bm{x})=\bm{0}, and therefore
\rho(\bm{d})=\rho(\bm{x})-\rho(\bm{c})=-\rho(\bm{c})=\rho(\bm{c}),
where the last equality uses that \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/W has exponent 2. Thus \rho(\bm{d})\in\rho(C), as claimed.
∎
We now count the elements of C according to the W-cosets they occupy. Since X\subseteq C\cap W, the zero coset of W already contains at least the m elements of X. Moreover, by Claim 2.3, every nonzero element of \rho(D_{n}) corresponds to a nonzero W-coset that meets C. Hence
|C|\geq m+|\rho(D_{n})\setminus\{\bm{0}\}|.
Since the vectors
\rho(\bm{e}_{1}),\dots,\rho(\bm{e}_{n})
span \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/W, we may choose indices
1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{s}\leq n
such that
\rho(\bm{e}_{i_{1}}),\dots,\rho(\bm{e}_{i_{s}})
form a basis of \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}/W. The pairwise sums of these s basis vectors are nonzero and pairwise distinct. For every 1\leq a<b\leq s,
\rho(\bm{e}_{i_{a}})+\rho(\bm{e}_{i_{b}})=\rho(\bm{e}_{i_{a}}+\bm{e}_{i_{b}})\in\rho(D_{n}).
Hence
|\rho(D_{n})\setminus\{\bm{0}\}|\geq\binom{s}{2}.
Moreover, since s=n-\dim W\geq n-m+1 and m\leq n-u, we have
|C|\geq m+\binom{s}{2}\geq m+\binom{n-m+1}{2}=n+\binom{n-m}{2}\geq n+\binom{u}{2}.
It remains to reduce the general case X\subseteq C\subseteq\Gamma to the case already proved, in which both sets lie inside K=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}. The key observation is that, since every element of D_{n} lies in K, any representation of an element of D_{n} as a difference \bm{x}-\bm{c}, with \bm{x}\in X and \bm{c}\in C, must use two elements from the same coset of K. We therefore fold each K-coset meeting X back onto K.
For each coset \alpha+K meeting X, choose an element
\bm{x}_{\alpha}\in X\cap(\alpha+K),
and define
X_{\alpha}^{\prime}=(X\cap(\alpha+K))-\bm{x}_{\alpha}\subseteq K,\qquad C_{\alpha}^{\prime}=(C\cap(\alpha+K))-\bm{x}_{\alpha}\subseteq K.
Let
X^{\prime}=\bigcup_{\alpha}X_{\alpha}^{\prime} and
C^{\prime}=\bigcup_{\alpha}C_{\alpha}^{\prime},
where the unions range over all cosets of K meeting X. Since X\subseteq C, we have
X^{\prime}\subseteq C^{\prime}\subseteq K.
Moreover,
|X^{\prime}|\leq|X| and
|C^{\prime}|\leq|C|,
because translation preserves cardinalities within each coset, while sets coming from distinct cosets may overlap after being translated into K. We then claim that the covering property of D_{n} is preserved under this folding operation.
Claim 2.4.
D_{n}\subseteq X^{\prime}-C^{\prime}.
Proof of the claim.
Let \bm{d}\in D_{n}. Since D_{n}\subseteq X-C, we write
\bm{d}=\bm{x}-\bm{c}
for some \bm{x}\in X and \bm{c}\in C. As \bm{d}\in K, the elements \bm{x} and \bm{c} lie in the same coset \alpha+K. Therefore
\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\alpha}\in X_{\alpha}^{\prime} and
\bm{c}-\bm{x}_{\alpha}\in C_{\alpha}^{\prime},
which further implies that
\bm{d}=(\bm{x}-\bm{x}_{\alpha})-(\bm{c}-\bm{x}_{\alpha})\in X^{\prime}-C^{\prime}. This finishes the proof.
∎
By construction and Claim 2.4, the pair X^{\prime},C^{\prime} lies inside K and satisfies
X^{\prime}\subseteq C^{\prime}, |X^{\prime}|\leq|X|\leq n-u and
D_{n}\subseteq X^{\prime}-C^{\prime}.
Therefore the special case already proved for subsets of K=\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} applies to X^{\prime} and C^{\prime}, and yields
|C^{\prime}|\geq n+\binom{u}{2}.
Finally, the folding operation used to construct C^{\prime} can only decrease cardinality, so
|C|\geq|C^{\prime}|\geq n+\binom{u}{2}.
This completes the proof.
∎
We now apply Lemma 2.2 with
\Gamma=\mathbb{R}^{n}/2\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and K=\mathbb{Z}^{n}/2\mathbb{Z}^{n}\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}.
Moreover, set
X=\mathcal{X}, C=\mathcal{C}, and u=t.
Under the natural identification of K with \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}, the set D_{n} becomes
\{\pi(\bm{e}_{i}+\bm{e}_{j}):1\leq i<j\leq n\}.
By definition, \mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{C}. Moreover, (3) gives
|\mathcal{X}|\leq n-t,
while (4) gives
D_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}-\mathcal{C}.
Hence all hypotheses of Lemma 2.2 are satisfied, and therefore
|\mathcal{C}|\geq n+\binom{t}{2}.
Since \mathcal{C} is the set of colors appearing on the vertices of H, we have
|\mathcal{C}|\leq|V(H)|.
Combining this with (2), we obtain
p+q-n=|V(H)|\geq|\mathcal{C}|\geq n+\binom{t}{2}.
Thus
q\geq 2n+\binom{t}{2}-p.
Finally, using p\leq n-t and q\leq|B|, we get
|B|\geq q\geq 2n+\binom{t}{2}-(n-t)=n+t+\binom{t}{2}=n+\binom{t+1}{2}.
This completes the proof.
