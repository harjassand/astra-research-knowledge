I’m filtering out concepts that rely on unmeasured behavior or merely recombine known actuators, focusing instead on demonstrated controls that genuinely remove a bottleneck.
Two nanofluidic routes already have detailed precedents, while water–carbon interfaces remain promising: water and alcohols differently affect graphene electron cooling, suggesting selective coupling but not yet transport.
I developed a candidate physical operation that would let one sealed liquid flow drive another while suppressing the reverse influence of the receiving liquid’s back-pressure. The liquids would remain chemically isolated. The coupling would pass through stationary conductive walls, electrical wiring and a passive nonreciprocal circuit—not through a shared fluid connection, moving diaphragm or sensor-controlled pump.
The investigation produced a precise constitutive law, a general nonreciprocity-transfer identity, an experimentally testable implementation and a sharp efficiency bound. It also rejected the most attractive initial interpretation: this is not presently a credible route to superior bulk energy recovery. The surviving opportunity is a different kind of nanofluidic interconnection and physical control.
The complete operation has not been demonstrated. Its central unresolved requirement is measurable, reciprocal, near-equilibrium conversion between neutral-liquid motion and terminal electronic current. Related experiments provide a physical starting point, but do not establish the complete transducer required below.
The starting question was whether electrically accessible interactions at a liquid–solid interface could do more than generate a small voltage: could they transmit mechanical work and impose a new relationship between otherwise isolated liquid flows?
There is relevant experimental leverage. Lizée and colleagues measured electronic currents associated with moving liquid droplets on carbon surfaces, including nonionic liquids. Importantly, their supplementary controls include glycerol on graphene at zero longitudinal electrical bias, with a separate back gate. This is a stronger starting point than a current observed only while an external voltage drives the conductor. Nevertheless, the experiment does not supply the complete bidirectional, steady-flow energy-conversion characterization needed here. [APS Journals](https://journals.aps.org/prx/pdf/10.1103/PhysRevX.13.011020)
Two initially attractive directions proved to be prior work. Coquinot, Bocquet and Kavokine already proposed direct conversion of small-scale liquid-flow energy into electricity through hydroelectronic drag, including the reciprocal current-to-liquid-flow effect. Separately, momentum transmission between liquids through a solid has already been investigated theoretically and through molecular simulation. Neither is a new invention of this investigation. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.20209v2)
The more distinct opportunity is therefore:
Use the liquid–electron interface as a mechanical port, then use an external electrical network to determine how separate liquid ports exert pressure-dependent forces on one another.
In particular, a nonreciprocal electrical network could make that influence directional.
This differs from adding a Hall sensor to a pump. The proposed electrical connection would carry the energy and mechanical back-action itself. No measurement-and-feedback loop is required for the ideal operating principle.
The potential originality is this particular stationary-interface, neutral-liquid, remote electrical composition. Gyrators, Hall chirality, hydraulic pressure exchange and network elimination are established concepts; even acoustic gyrators have a long history. The search did not establish historical priority for the proposed combination. [AIP Publishing](https://pubs.aip.org/jasa/article/25/3/575/623274/An-Acoustic-Gyrator)
Consider two separate flow cells.
Each contains a liquid channel with an electrically contacted carbon wall. The electrical contact pads are kept outside the liquid. The two flow cells have separate reservoirs, separate supports where practical, and no shared hydraulic connection.
Their electrical terminals connect to a dry, passive Hall network. The magnetic field is confined to that network rather than applied to the liquids.
The intended sequence is
\[ \text{source pressure} \longrightarrow \text{liquid motion} \longrightarrow \text{electronic current} \longrightarrow \text{nonreciprocal electrical transport} \longrightarrow \text{force on a second liquid}. \]
The source pressure supplies the work. A static magnetic field supplies time-reversal asymmetry, not energy.
Separating the wet interfaces from the Hall element is important. It avoids requiring one material simultaneously to provide strong liquid–electron momentum exchange, favorable hydraulic properties and excellent Hall transport. Those properties cannot simply be assumed to coexist.
The initial experiment need not achieve perfect isolation. A room-temperature, lossy Hall element with a measured asymmetric terminal response would suffice to test the composition. Near-perfect chirality would be a later, more demanding case—not an assumed component.
The distinctive operation would be directional pressure influence between liquids that never contact one another.
For example, one fluid stream could supply work to a second fluidic unit while changes in that unit’s back-pressure have little linear effect on the first stream. That could be useful where chemical isolation and stationary interfaces matter more than conversion efficiency.
It would not transport molecules through the electrical connection. It would not produce flow without an energy source. It would not automatically remove thermal noise, substrate vibration or nonlinear back-action.
The analysis must begin with experimentally acquired coefficients, not favorable material parameters assigned to a schematic.
For wet transducer \(i\), define pressure drop \(p_i\), volumetric flow \(Q_i\), terminal voltage \(V_i\), and current \(I_i\) entering the transducer. Choose signs so that
\[ p_iQ_i+V_iI_i \]
is power entering the device.
At fixed temperature and gate setting, near equilibrium and without a local magnetic field, the required transport law is
\[ \begin{pmatrix} Q_i\\ I_i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} K_i&C_i\\ C_i&G_i \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} p_i\\V_i \end{pmatrix}. \tag{1} \]
This is the established reciprocal transport description used in hydroelectronic conversion theory. Here it is a measurement requirement: both cross-responses must be acquired for the actual device and operating regime. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.20209v2)
Specifically,
\[ C_i= \left.\frac{\partial I_i}{\partial p_i}\right|_{V_i=0} = \left.\frac{\partial Q_i}{\partial V_i}\right|_{p_i=0}. \]
A flow-dependent current under electrical bias does not establish this equality. Neither does a transient charging signal establish its steady-flow counterpart.
Passivity requires
\[ K_i>0,\qquad G_i>0,\qquad C_i^2\le K_iG_i. \tag{2} \]
The coefficients must include the real device’s hydraulic, electronic and contact losses under the stated boundary conditions.
For several independent wet devices, let \(K,C,G\) be diagonal matrices. Let the dry electrical network have terminal admittance \(Y\), including its contacts and wiring.
Electrical current conservation gives
\[ I=-YV. \]
Eliminating the electrical variables from equation (1) gives
\[ \boxed{ Q=Hp,\qquad H=K-C(G+Y)^{-1}C. } \tag{3} \]
This is the central design equation.
It says exactly which hydraulic interaction a measured electrical network would impose through measured liquid–electron interfaces. It does not require reconstructing the microscopic interaction to predict the assembled device’s small-signal response.
Set \(A=G+Y\). Direct subtraction gives
\[ \boxed{ H-H^{T} = C A^{-T}(Y-Y^{T})A^{-1}C. } \tag{4} \]
If every wet coupling \(C_i\) is nonzero, the matrices surrounding \(Y-Y^T\) are invertible. Therefore,
\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{rank}(H-H^T) = \operatorname{rank}(Y-Y^T). } \tag{5} \]
This is useful because it does not require identical wet devices. Unequal conductances do not automatically restore reciprocity.
But rank preservation is not useful-power preservation. The hydraulic cross-response can become arbitrarily small as the wet couplings weaken. A mathematically nonzero effect may be technologically irrelevant.
If reversal of the remote magnetic field transposes the electrical response while leaving the wet interfaces unchanged, then
\[ \boxed{H(-B)=H(B)^T.} \tag{6} \]
That provides a particularly clean experimental signature.
The hydraulic input obeys
\[ p^TQ = \sum_i\left(p_iQ_i+V_iI_i\right) + V^TYV. \tag{7} \]
The first term is dissipation in the wet transducers; the second is dissipation in the dry network. Both are nonnegative for passive components.
Thus the composite device remains passive. Its nonreciprocal response supplies no extra energy.
For a quasistatic hydraulic network with positive compliance matrix \(S\),
\[ S\dot p=-Hp, \qquad E=\frac12p^TSp, \]
and
\[ \dot E=-p^T\operatorname{sym}(H)p\le0. \]
This establishes stability in that specified model. It does not establish arbitrary bandwidth: fluid inertia, electrical capacitance, ionic relaxation and delays must be included when they matter.
My initial candidate used an almost lossless Hall gyrator. That would have made the energy-transfer argument substantially more attractive.
The physical literature rules out treating an ordinary ohmically contacted Hall plate that way. Contact boundary conditions create dissipation that persists even when the bulk longitudinal conductivity approaches zero. Increasing the magnetic field does not simply remove the terminal losses. Reactive coupling can change the finite-frequency problem, but does not establish efficient DC transfer. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/1312.5190v3)
Experimental microwave Hall circulators exist, but their demonstrated microwave operation must not be substituted for a steady-flow electrical component. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevX.7.011007?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
This forces a change in the apparatus model: include the actual electrical contacts from the beginning.
A useful benchmark is a three-contact chiral conductor, with its third contact at the electrical reference potential. A simple forward/backward transmission model has
\[ Y= \begin{pmatrix} g_++g_-&-g_-\\ -g_+&g_++g_- \end{pmatrix}, \qquad g_+,g_-\ge0. \tag{8} \]
The ideal chiral limit is \(g_-=0\). Such directional multiprobe transport has a basis in the established quantum Hall framework; it is not a new electronic mechanism proposed here. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevB.38.9375?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
For two possibly unequal wet transducers, equation (3) yields
\[ \boxed{ \frac{H_{21}}{H_{12}} = \frac{g_+}{g_-}, } \tag{9} \]
when the denominator is nonzero.
The directional contrast is inherited from the electrical circuit, independently of unequal wet conductances. Its absolute magnitude still depends on both wet couplings.
In the ideal chiral limit,
\[ H_{12}=0,\qquad H_{21}\ne0 \]
provided \(C_1C_2\ne0\). The receiver’s pressure then has no linear effect on donor flow, while donor pressure influences receiver flow.
This is genuine one-way cross-response. A pure gyrator, by contrast, has opposite-sign but equal-magnitude off-diagonal responses and is not automatically an isolator.
Perfect chirality is an ideal limit. A real experiment must use the measured \(Y\), rather than declaring unwanted transmission zero.
For the ideal chiral case, write \(g=g_+\) and \(g_-=0\). Define
\[ \kappa_i=K_i-\frac{C_i^2}{G_i+g}, \]
and
\[ b=\frac{|C_1C_2|g}{(G_1+g)(G_2+g)}. \]
After choosing the receiver’s hydraulic orientation appropriately,
\[ H= \begin{pmatrix} \kappa_1&0\\ b&\kappa_2 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{10} \]
Drive the source with \(p_1\). Connect the receiver to a passive hydraulic resistance \(R\), so that \(p_2=-RQ_2\). Then
\[ Q_1=\kappa_1p_1,\qquad Q_2=\frac{bp_1}{1+\kappa_2R}. \]
The work-transfer efficiency is
\[ \eta(R)= \frac{RQ_2^2}{p_1Q_1}. \]
Optimizing the receiving load gives
\[ R_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac1{\kappa_2}, \qquad \eta_{\mathrm{load,max}} = \frac{b^2}{4\kappa_1\kappa_2}. \tag{11} \]
Now define whole-device coupling measures
\[ \chi_i=\frac{C_i^2}{K_iG_i}, \qquad Z_i=\frac{\chi_i}{1-\chi_i}. \tag{12} \]
For a single reciprocal converter, write
\[ \eta_c(Z)= \frac{\sqrt{1+Z}-1}{\sqrt{1+Z}+1}. \]
Optimizing the electrical matching as well yields
\[ \boxed{ \eta_{\mathrm{isolator}} \le \frac14\,\eta_c(Z_1)\eta_c(Z_2) \le\frac14. } \tag{13} \]
With \(t_i=g/G_i\),
\[ \frac{b^2}{\kappa_1\kappa_2} = \prod_{i=1}^{2} \frac{\chi_it_i} {(1+t_i)(1+t_i-\chi_i)}. \]
Each factor is maximized at
\[ t_i=\sqrt{1-\chi_i}, \]
where its maximum equals \(\eta_c(Z_i)\). Combining this with equation (11) gives equation (13).
The equality conditions may not be simultaneously achievable with a fabricated pair. Consequently, the expression is an upper bound, not a performance prediction.
Scope matters: the 25% ceiling applies to this contact-inclusive, grounded three-contact, quasistatic topology. It is not a universal limit on every hydraulic isolator, active device or finite-frequency nonreciprocal circuit.
Published hydroelectronic theory estimated approximately \(Z=4\times10^{-4}\) for one graphene case and \(Z\approx11\) for a graphitic case. These are theoretical material/device estimates—not measured complete transducers—and the larger estimate relies on interpreting interfacial friction as useful liquid–electron coupling. The candidate’s possible value lies elsewhere: directional coupling with chemical isolation and stationary interfaces.
Optimizing only the flow-induced current would be a mistake.
A modification might increase the cross-coefficient \(C\) while also increasing hydraulic friction, electrical dissipation or contact losses. The relevant quantity is
\[ \frac{C^2}{KG-C^2}, \]
measured for the complete device—not current amplitude alone.
Likewise, a short slip length does not establish that the lost mechanical momentum reaches an electrically recoverable degree of freedom. It could predominantly reach the lattice or another dissipative channel.
Experimental nanofluidic work shows substantial sensitivity to the immediate liquid–solid interface and considerable variation in inferred slip. It also demonstrates that electrical conductivity alone does not determine water transport. This prevents importing a favorable friction coefficient into an unrelated fabricated channel. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23325-3)
For a sufficiently slender slit channel in a continuum regime, the usual conductance estimate is
\[ K_{\mathrm{geom}} = \frac{Nwh^3}{12\mu L} \left(1+\frac{6b_{\mathrm{slip}}}{h}\right), \tag{14} \]
where \(N\) is channel count, \(w\) width, \(h\) height, \(L\) length and \(\mu\) viscosity. Such slip-modified descriptions are used in experimental slit-channel analysis, with important qualifications at very small confinement. [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-23325-3)
Equation (14) is not automatically the \(K\) in equation (1): the electrical boundary condition must match.
The design conflict is physical. Confinement can increase the importance of the liquid–electron interface, but narrowing the channel can reduce throughput and increase sensitivity to entrances, deformation, molecular structure and contamination.
Parallel replication does not remove the efficiency limitation. For identical parallel transducers,
\[ K\mapsto NK,\qquad C\mapsto NC,\qquad G\mapsto NG, \]
so \(\chi\) and \(Z\) remain unchanged. Replication increases capacity only if fabrication yield, manifolds and electrical matching also scale.
The complete energy boundary must include the source pump when one is used, electrical contacts, hydraulic manifolds, gate charging and leakage, magnetic-field generation, control electronics, cleaning and component replacement.
A quantum Hall element connected to warmer liquid interfaces requires particular care. Refrigeration is not free, and temperature gradients introduce thermoelectric heat currents and possible additional work sources. The isothermal bound above cannot be applied unmodified when such a gradient supplies electrical power.
No endurance, fouling, drift, fabrication-yield or maintenance result has been established for the proposed assembly. “No moving interface” does not imply indefinite lifetime.
The most valuable next experiment is not to build a complicated Hall network. It is to establish whether the wet interface satisfies the required work-conversion contract.
At fixed temperature and gate setting, measure
\[ K=\frac{\partial Q}{\partial p},\qquad G=\frac{\partial I}{\partial V}, \]
together with
\[ C_{\mathrm{flow\to current}} = \left.\frac{\partial I}{\partial p}\right|_{V=0}, \qquad C_{\mathrm{voltage\to flow}} = \left.\frac{\partial Q}{\partial V}\right|_{p=0}. \]
The central test is whether the two cross-coefficients agree in the same near-equilibrium regime.
Use long pressure steps as well as low-frequency modulation. A response that disappears after charging transients cannot be treated as continuous DC conversion.
If a substantial forward response exists but no corresponding reverse response is found, the first conclusion should be that the operating conditions or mechanism do not satisfy the assumed reciprocal two-port description—not that Onsager reciprocity has been overcome.
Nominally pure water does not establish an ion-independent mechanism.
The measurements should quantify liquid conductivity, contact leakage, temperature gradients, gate leakage and possible electrode chemistry. Appropriate controls include protected metal contacts, electrically insulating interface controls, reciprocal electrical substitutes, and a suitably low-conductivity nonaqueous liquid where feasible.
Mechanical vibration, strain-dependent conductance and displacement currents must be independently bounded.
Even a successful composite experiment would not uniquely identify quantum friction. Another reciprocal liquid–electron mechanism could produce the same terminal law. The proposed operation is mechanism-agnostic at that level; its microscopic interpretation requires further discrimination.
Independently acquire the dry network’s full terminal matrix \(Y(B,\omega)\). Then calculate

before connecting the wet devices.
The consequential checks are donor–receiver interchange, magnetic-field reversal, opening the electrical connection, replacing the network with a reciprocal one, and changing its electrical loading.

A corresponding hydraulic asymmetry that follows the independently measured electrical asymmetry would be more persuasive than simply detecting a small receiver motion.
Load the receiver and measure

The experiment must distinguish a flow sensor from a work-transmitting interface. A measurable voltage, open-circuit pressure or displacement alone does not establish useful power transfer.

The strongest relevant alternatives set different tests.
For bulk energy recovery, the proposed DC architecture loses. Its contact-inclusive efficiency ceiling is already unfavorable before unmeasured material and auxiliary costs enter.
For fluid routing, established mechanical approaches remain serious competitors. Recent experiments on distributed soft leaflets demonstrate directional transport through nonlinear fluid–structure interactions and spatiotemporal excitation. The proposed electronic connection would need to justify itself specifically where chemical isolation, stationary wet interfaces or remote interconnection matter—not merely by showing that unidirectional flow is possible. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.27474)
For nonreciprocal signals, the principle is not new. Existing Hall and acoustic devices already provide that functionality. The possible addition is a neutral-liquid mechanical interface that sustains the relevant low-frequency or steady-flow response. [APS Journals](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevX.7.011007?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
If the wet transducer is established with sufficiently strong coupling, the broader capability would be electrically configurable mechanical interactions among chemically isolated fluidic units. Equation (4) shows that this extends beyond an identical two-device example: a network’s nonreciprocal structure can survive heterogeneous reciprocal interfaces.
That could support a new class of nanofluidic interconnections, back-pressure isolation and probes of interfacial momentum transfer. Whether it enables materially better manufacturing or instrumentation remains an experimental and engineering question.
The substantive result is a candidate physical composition with an exact operating law, a robust directional-transfer identity, a contact-inclusive efficiency limit and a no-free-parameter assembly test.
