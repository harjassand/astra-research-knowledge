1 Introduction

Simulation of quantum many-body systems is widely expected to be a major application of future quantum computers. Consequently, preparing quantum thermal and ground states—central tasks in this vision—has motivated a broad array of sophisticated quantum algorithm proposals [TOV+11, SM21, CKBG25, DLL25, JI24, DCL24, RWW23] as well as experimental demonstrations [MMS+24].

Nevertheless, many end-to-end applications in chemistry and materials science, especially when integrated with machine learning workflows, ultimately require estimating a large collection of observables. Because quantum measurements are inherently destructive, this task leads to a rich set of fundamental problems in quantum state tomography [HHJ+17, OW16, KOS07, HWM+22, BMBO20, ZRM21, WHLB23, KGKB25, PSTW25].
Despite important progress on some aspects of this problem [Aar18, HKP20], the best general-purpose efficient protocol [Sin25]
for estimating many observables only achieves a quadratic improvement over the straightforward measure, discard, and repeat procedure that uses an extensive number of samples.
This heavy multiplicative overhead from tomography threatens to narrow the regime of quantum advantage in quantum simulation, especially because the competing classical approaches (e.g., tensor networks [Sch11, Orú19, VNS+23]) typically provide an explicit description of the samples, which often enables extracting observables with an additive cost.

In this work, we introduce a general measurement protocol for estimating many thermal observables which is:

• 

Sample efficient. The number of thermal samples scales logarithmically with the number of observables and quadratically with the desired precision.

• 

Computationally efficient. The protocol uses single-copy measurements. The total (controlled) Hamiltonian evolution time scales linearly with the inverse temperature and the number of observables, and quadratically with the precision.

Generally, shadow tomography [Aar18] has shown that many observables can be estimated information-theoretically from a small number of copies of an unknown quantum state. However, it relies on entangled operations across many copies and appears computationally inefficient. Classical shadow methods with single-copy measurements can provide computationally efficient protocols, but only for restricted classes of observables, such as low-rank or few-body operators [HKP20]. Further developments in this direction have shown [KGKB25], that using two-copy measurements one can efficiently estimate a broader class of observables, however the corresponding protocols are more involved and often feature degraded precision dependence. By contrast, our single-copy measurement protocol matches the corresponding tight classical sample-complexities, and can be efficiently implemented for arbitrary bounded and efficiently measurable observables, including Green’s functions and high-weight operators that commonly arise in quantum simulation.

The key idea behind our protocol is a new use of recently constructed quantum Gibbs samplers as

measurement channels that preserve the Gibbs state when outcomes are ignored.

Operationally, the measurement protocol can be viewed as running a carefully designed Gibbs sampling algorithm, where the Kraus operators are tailored to the desired observables. Conceptually, the mechanism for estimating multiple observables resembles making statistical inferences from trajectories of classical Markov chains. Our results show that the destructive nature of quantum measurements can be effectively circumvented by exploiting quantum detailed balance, and the quantum trajectories can give surprisingly powerful statistics. Even though our work focuses entirely on tomography and keeps the thermal state preparation as a black box, we expect the interplay between the algorithmic and tomographic nature of quantum Gibbs samplers to be a fertile ground [JLL26].

More broadly, our protocol suggests that many black-box tomography problems admit fundamentally different solutions when additional structure of the initial state—such as knowledge of the Hamiltonian—is available [CK25]. In such cases, this structure can translate into orders-of-magnitude reductions in state-preparation costs in practical quantum simulation workflows, and substantially accelerate acquisition of quantum data at scale.

Subsequently, the widely celebrated framework of classical shadows [HKP20] demonstrated that the central promise of shadow tomography can be achieved efficiently for certain classes of observables, particularly low-rank or low-weight operators. An appealing feature of this protocol is that the observables need only be specified after the measurements are performed. However, the sample complexity depends on a quantity known as the shadow norm, which can grow prohibitively large for high-rank or high-weight observables.
More recently, a rapidly growing family of shadow-based tomography protocols established non-trivial tradeoffs between sample complexity and the class of observables that can be estimated [CLL24, KGKB25]. Nevertheless, it remains open whether a sample complexity logarithmic in the number of observables can be achieved for arbitrary observables.

A complementary line of work seeks polynomial improvements over the naive

While these approaches can provide non-trivial quadratic speedups, it seems unlikely that larger improvements are achievable using similar methodology.

Our protocol offers a different conceptual route for reducing tomography costs: implicit knowledge about the initial state. In many quantum simulation settings, the Hamiltonian is known, even though preparing the corresponding Gibbs state may be computationally hard. We show that this structure can be exploited to effectively reuse Gibbs samples by designing measurement protocols that satisfy (KMS) quantum detailed balance. Concretely, we construct a Gibbs sampler whose Kraus operators are associated with the desired observables, allowing measurements to be performed while preserving the Gibbs state when outcomes are ignored. The key distinction from the general shadow tomography problem is that the thermal setting is fundamentally computational: information-theoretically, the Gibbs state is already specified by the Hamiltonian. Remarkably, access to the Hamiltonian alone suffices to achieve shadow-tomography-like sample complexity while avoiding the destructive overhead coming from extensive repeated state preparation.

Proof ideas.

Recently, a new wave of quantum algorithms emerged for preparing thermal states using detailed-balanced Lindbladians or quantum channels [TOV+11, SM21, RWW23, CKBG25, DCL24, DLL25, GCDK24]. Operationally, these algorithms can be viewed as implementing carefully controlled system–ancilla interactions. When the ancillae are traced out, the resulting effective dynamics can be designed to satisfy an exact structural property—quantum detailed balance [CKG23]—that guarantees the Gibbs state to be a fixed point.

The key idea of our protocol is a conceptual extension of these quantum Gibbs-sampling algorithms. Rather than discarding the ancilla, we realize that the ancilla—recording which transition or “jump” has occurred—already functions as a metrological device. This perspective leads us to introduce the central primitive of our algorithm: detailed-balanced measurement channels (Figure 1).

2 Discussion

To overcome the apparent measurement overhead in quantum simulation, we designed a protocol that measures 
M
MM observables using only 
\log(M)
log⁡(M)\log(M) thermal states. The protocol only requires the observables to be spectrally bounded (which captures most physical observables, such as Green’s functions), can be efficiently implemented on a quantum computer with access to (controlled) time-evolution by the system Hamiltonian, and is sample-optimal in a certain black-box model. We believe our protocol could play an important role in practical applications by enhancing the rate of data acquisition in thermal simulation workflows. In turn, this could fundamentally accelerate quantum data-intensive chemistry and materials applications in broader settings.

Broadly, our results provide a novel way of reusing complex quantum states by designing measurements that statistically preserve the given mixed quantum state. We briefly compare our results to other related developments as follows. Recently, quantum Gibbs states (and approximate stationary states of detailed-balanced Lindbladians) have been proved to satisfy a local Markov property: perturbations coming from local noise can be locally removed by the natural Lindbladian dynamics [CR25, BCV25]. However, this Markov property turns out to be unnecessary to our shadow tomography statement; we do not need to run any additional Lindbladian between our detailed balanced measurements, provided access to multiple independent Gibbs states. To our knowledge, so far the Markov property has only been shown to be useful for measurements with an additional assumption of correlation decay, where a single copy of the state can produce effectively independent measurement results by interspersing measurements with Lindbladian dynamics [BCV25, Che26].

In the case of estimating properties of a gapped ground state — another natural target of quantum simulation — a single copy of the state is sufficient for measuring observables [FGH+10, CK25] regardless the precision and the number of observables. However, this is unique to the pure state setting, and the efficiency depends on the corresponding spectral gap. By contrast, our shadow tomography protocol for the Gibbs state only requires the efficient simulation of the system Hamiltonian on top of providing independent quantum Gibbs samples.

Extending efficient shadow tomography beyond thermal states brings additional caveats. Indeed, our protocol heavily relies on the exact interplay between the Gibbs state, detailed balance, and knowledge of the Hamiltonian.
For (approximate) stationary states [GHC+25] of generic Lindbladians that do not correspond to sensible thermal states, however, much less is currently known both from an algorithmic and a theoretical perspective.
Indeed, when the measurement channel no longer preserves the target state, the 
\log(M)
log⁡(M)\log(M) scaling may be lost, as each measurement can accumulate systematic deviation from the target state.

That said, we believe our protocol shall be extendable to approximate stationary states of Gibbs samplers [BCV25], although one likely needs to restrict to observables that preserve the approximate stationary state to a sufficiently high extent.
In the case where the Hamiltonian is only known approximately, or the algorithmic implementation is noisy — such as when interfacing with analog samples — the number of observables one can measure on the same state will depend on the errors. Even though our presentation focuses on fault-tolerant, digital simulation settings, we believe the idea of detailed-balanced measurements will be a valuable component in broader settings and may be instantiated in many different ways in practical quantum simulation workflows.
