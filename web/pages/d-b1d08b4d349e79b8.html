Identiﬁcation by Edge Contraction in Linear Structural Equation

Abstract
We extend graph-based identiﬁcation methods for
linear models by allowing background knowledge in
the form of externally evaluated parameters. Such
information could be obtained, for example, from a
previously conducted randomized experiment, from
substantive understanding of the domain, or even
from another identiﬁcation technique. To manage
such information systematically, we propose an op-
eration called edge-contraction (EC), which trans-
forms the original model into a new one where
the value of the known coeﬃcient would be equal
to zero. The transformed graph can then admit
conventional methods of identiﬁcation (e.g., single-
door criterion, instrumental variables, half-trek cri-
terion) and model testing (e.g., d-separation, over-
identiﬁcation). By iteratively alternating steps of
identiﬁcation and contraction, the EC-operation can
improve the power of existing identiﬁcation meth-
ods, even without additional knowledge. We opera-
tionalize this general approach for instrumental sets
(a generalization of instrumental variables) and show
that the resulting method subsumes the most gen-
eral identiﬁcation method for linear systems known
to date. We further discuss the application of the
EC-operation in the tasks of model testing and z-
identiﬁcation.
1 Introduction
Many researchers, particularly in economics, psy-
chology, epidemiology, and the social sciences, use
linear structural equation models (SEMs) to de-
scribe the causal and statistical relationships be-
tween a set of variables, predict the eﬀects of inter-
ventions and policies, and to estimate parameters
of interest (Bollen and Pearl, 2013). A linear SEM
consists of a set of equations of the form,
X = ΛX +U,
where X = [x1,...,x n]t is a vector containing the
model variables, Λ is a matrix containing the coeﬃ-
cients of the model, which convey the strength of the
causal relationships, and U = [u1,...,u n]t is a vector
of error terms, which represents omitted or latent
variables. The matrix Λ contains zeroes on the di-
agonal, and Λ ij = 0 whenever xi is not a cause of
xj. The covariance matrix of X will be denoted by
Σ and the covariance matrix over the error terms, U,
by Ω. The entries of Λ and Ω are the model param-
eters. In this paper, we will restrict our attention to
recursive models, models where the rows of Λ can
be arranged so that it is lower triangular.
When the coeﬃcients are known, then total ef-
fects, direct eﬀects, and counterfactuals can be com-
puted from them directly (Pearl, 2009; Chen and
Pearl, 2014). However, in order to be able to
compute these coeﬃcients, we must utilize domain
knowledge in the form of exclusion and independence
restrictions (Pearl, 1995, p. 704). Exclusion restric-
tions represent assumptions that a given variable is
not a direct cause of another, while independence re-
strictions represent assumptions that no latent con-
founders exists between two variables. Algebraically,
these assumptions translate into restrictions on en-
tries in the coeﬃcient matrix, Λ, and error term co-
variance matrix, Ω, to zero.
Determining whether model parameters can be
expressed in terms of the probability distribution,
which is necessary to be able to compute them from
data, is the problem of identiﬁcation. When it is not
possible to uniquely express the value of a model pa-
rameter in terms of the probability distribution, we

To our knowledge, the most general method for

ual structural coeﬃcients can be accomplished using
the single-door criterion (i.e. identiﬁcation using re-

strumental variables (Wright, 1925, 1928) (see Brito
and Pearl (2002), Pearl (2009), or Chen and Pearl
(2014) for a graphical characterization), instrumen-
tal sets (Brito and Pearl, 2002), and the general half-
trek criterion (Chen, 2015). Finally, d-separation
(Pearl, 2009) and overidentiﬁcation (Pearl, 2004;
Chen et al., 2014) provide the means to enumer-
ate testable implications of the model, which can be
used to test it against data.
Each of these methods only utilize restrictions on
the entries of Λ and Ω to zero. In this paper, we
enrich graph-based identiﬁcation and model test-
ing methods by allowing assumptions in the form
of externally evaluated coeﬃcients. The main idea
of our procedure, called edge contraction (EC), is
to perform a few simple arithmetic operations on
the model variables, transforming the original model
into a new one so as that the known coeﬃcient values
would be equal to zero. Not only does this proce-
dure allow the incorporation of scientiﬁc knowledge
to these methods that otherwise could not be used,
but it also greatly increases the power of identiﬁ-
cation methods by iteratively alternating steps of
identiﬁcation and contraction.
The intuition behind edge contraction is simple:
if the coeﬃcient from variable x toy is known, then
we would like to remove the direct eﬀect of x on y
by subtracting it from y. Simple and intuitive iden-
tiﬁcation procedures are valuable in that they allow
scientists to more easily ponder identiﬁcation issues
while designing their study. Intuitiveness and sim-
plicity are likely the reasons for the overwhelming
popularity of OLS and two-stage least squares re-
gression to identify causal eﬀects. While intuitive,
EC can greatly increase the power of existing iden-
tiﬁcation methods. To demonstrate its power, we
show that combining EC with instrumental sets sub-
sumes the relatively more complicated general half-

The notion of “removing an edge” in order to
turn a variable into an instrument was ﬁrst noted by
Shardell (2012) when attemping to identify the to-

of the independence restriction of a potential instru-

then “removing” the necessary edges into y. In this
paper, we generalize and operationalize this notion

graphical methods for identiﬁcation and enumera-
tion of testable implications.
The paper is organized as follows: Sec. 2 reviews
notation and graphical notions that will be used in
the paper. In sec. 3, we introduce and formalize
the notion of edge contraction. In sec. 4, we opera-
tionalize the identiﬁcation of coeﬃcients using both
edge contraction and instrumental sets (IS+EC),
and prove that this method subsumes g-HTC. Fi-
nally, in sec. 5, we discuss additional applications
for EC, including identifying testable implications
and z-identiﬁcation (Bareinboim and Pearl, 2012).
2 Preliminaries
The causal graph or path diagram of an SEM is a
graph, G = (V,D,B ), where V are nodes or ver-
tices,D directed edges, andB bidirected edges. The
nodes represent model variables. Directed eges en-
code the direction of causality, and for each coeﬃ-
cient Λij ̸= 0, an edge is drawn from xi to xj. Each
directed edge, therefore, is associated with a coeﬃ-
cient in the SEM, which we will often refer to as its
structural coeﬃcient. The error terms, ui, are not
shown explicitly in the graph. However, a bidirected
edge between two nodes indicates that their corre-
sponding error terms may be statistically dependent
while the lack of a bidirected edge indicates that the
error terms are independent.
If a directed edge, called ( x,y ), exists from x to
y then x is a parent of y. The set of parents of y
is denoted Pa (y). Additionally, we call y the head
of (x,y ) and x the tail. The set of tails for a set of
directed edges, E, is denoted Ta (E) while the set of
heads is denoted He(E). For a node, v, the set of
edges for which He(E) = v is denoted Inc(v). Fi-
nally, the set of nodes connected toy by a bidirected
arc are called the siblings of y or Sib(y).

A path fromx toy is a sequence of edges connect-
ing the two nodes. A path may go either along or
against the direction of the edges. A non-endpoint
nodew on a path is said to be a collider if the edges
preceding and following w both point to w.
A path between x and y is said to be unblocked
given a set Z, with x,y /∈ Z if every noncollider
on the path is not in Z and every collider on the
path is in An(Z) (Pearl, 2009), where An(Z) are
the ancestors of Z. Unblocked paths of the form
a → ... → b or a ← ... ← b are directed paths.
Any unblocked path that is not a directed path is a
divergent path.

5 Further Applications
We have formalized the edge contraction operation
and demonstrated its ability to increase the identiﬁ-
cation power of instrumental sets. In this section, we
discuss additional applications of the EC operation
as alluded to in the introduction, namely, incorpo-
rating knowledge of non-zero coeﬃcients values and
deriving new constraints over the covariance matrix
(observed data). The former can be useful to helping
identiﬁcation of new coeﬃcients, while the latter can
be useful for model testing and structural learning.
When the causal eﬀect of x on y is not identiﬁ-
able and performing randomized experiments on x
is not possible (due to cost, ethical, or other con-
siderations), we may nevertheless be able to identify
the causal eﬀect of x on y using knowledge gained
from experiments on an auxiliary set of variables Z.
The task of determining whether causal eﬀects can
be computed using surrogate experiments general-
izes the problem of identiﬁcation and was named z-
identiﬁcation in Bareinboim and Pearl (2012). They
provided necessary and suﬃcient conditions for this
task in the non-parametric settings. Considering
Figure 4a, one can immediately see that the eﬀect
of X on Y is not identiﬁable, given the unblockable
back-door path. Additionally, using BP’s condition,
one can see that the eﬀect of x on y is not identi-
ﬁable with experiments over z. Remarkably, if one
is willing to assume that the system is linear, more
can be said. The experiment over z would yield γ,
so we can apply EC and contract over γ, which en-
tails Figure 4b. The result follows immediately by
noting that α is identiﬁed from the regression of y
onx after subtracting γz fromy at each data point.
Thm. 1 guarantees that Wright’s rules can be used
on the graph after contraction. Since d-separation
can be derived from Wright’s rules in linear mod-
els,conditional independence constraints can be de-
rived from the graph after EC is applied. To see
how EC can generate new non-trivial constraints,
consider Fig. 2a. Here, z cannot be separate from
s. However, after identifying γ, we can use EC to
obtain the model in Fig. 2b. Now, z is separated
from s. As a result, we can test the model by sub-
tracting γw from z at each data point and test if z
is now independent of s. If this is not the case, then
we have reason to doubt the validity of the model.
Besides z-identiﬁcation and model testing, these
new constraints can also be used to prune the space
of compatible models in the task of structural learn-
ing. Additionally, it is also possible to envision that
the EC operation can be useful to operationalize
transportability (Pearl and Bareinboim, 2011) in lin-
ear settings, where experiments collected in one do-
main should be used to answer evaluation questions
in a diﬀerent, but somewhat related domain.
6 Conclusion
In this paper, we enriched graph-based identiﬁca-
tion and model testing methods by introducing a
new operation called edge contraction (EC). EC al-
lows existing identiﬁcation and model testing meth-
ods to incorporate knowledge of non-zero parameter
values. We demonstrated that by iteratively iden-
tifying parameters using instrumental sets and EC,
we are able to greatly increase the power of instru-
mental sets, to the extent that it subsumes the most
general criterion for linear identiﬁcation known to
date. We further discussed how EC can be useful
for the general tasks of testing and z-identiﬁcation.

Theorem 2. Given a semi-Markovian linear SEM
with graph G, if a set of edges, E, with head y, is
g-HTC identiﬁable, then it is IS+EC identiﬁable.
Proof. Since E is g-HTC identiﬁable, there exists
sequences of sets of nodes, ( Z1,...Zk), and sets of
edges, (E1,...,E k), such that
1. Zi satisﬁes the g-HTC with respect to Ei for all
i ∈ {1,...,k },
2. EZ1y1 = ∅, where yi = He(Ei) for all i ∈
{1,...,k }, and
3. EZiyi ⊆ (E1 ∪... ∪Ei−1) for all i ∈ {2,...,k }.
Now, using Lemma 6, we see that there Z1 is an
instrumental set for E1 in GEZ1 y1− = G and Ei is
identiﬁed using instrumental sets and Lemma 3 in
