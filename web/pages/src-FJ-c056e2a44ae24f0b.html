# Independent reconstruction of contact compensation

## Scope and notation

Let $F$ be a homogeneous quartic in the full nine-coefficient left-$SO(2)$-invariant planar class
$$
F=A_cu^2+B_cv^2+Cuv+Ew^2+Guw+Hvw+Lqu+Mqv+Nqw,
\qquad
u=|x|^2,\ v=|y|^2,\ w=x\cdot y,\ q=\det(x\mid y).
$$
Assume $F$ is rank-one convex and not polyconvex. Let $A\ne0$ and a nonzero rank-one $R$ be an acoustic contact, so $D^2F(A)[R,R]=0$; normalize $|R|=1$. For a mean-zero compatible gradient $X=D\varphi$, use probability-normalized averages and set
$$
H_X=\mathbb E D^2F(X),\quad b_X=\mathbb E DF(X),\quad c_X=\mathbb E F(X),
$$
$$
s=X:R,\quad Y=X-sR,\quad h=\mathbb E D^2F(X)[A,A],\quad
\beta=\mathbb E DF(X)[A],\quad g=\mathbb E D^2F(X)[A,R].
$$
The field $Y$ is the pointwise orthogonal projection transverse to $R$. The proof uses its norms and algebraic coordinates; it never assumes $Y$ is a gradient.

## The moment and contact identities

For every quartic, homogeneity and symmetry of its fourth derivative give
$$
D^2F(A)[X,X]=D^2F(X)[A,A].
$$
Rank-one convexity says the left side is nonnegative for every $X$ with $q(X)=0$. Since the determinant quadratic form on $\mathbb R^4$ is indefinite, the non-strict Finsler lemma yields
$$
D^2F(A)[X,X]=P_A(X)+\lambda_Aq(X),\qquad P_A\succeq0.
$$
For a compatible periodic or zero-boundary gradient, $\mathbb E q(X)=0$, hence
$$
h=\mathbb E P_A(X)\ge0.
$$
At contact, $P_A[R,R]=0$, so $P_AR=0$. Reciprocity also makes $D^2F(R)\succeq0$: for any $Z$, $D^2F(R)[Z,Z]=D^2F(Z)[R,R]\ge0$. Since $D^2F(R)[A,A]=D^2F(A)[R,R]=0$, positivity implies $D^2F(R)A=0$. Quartic tensor symmetry then gives the polynomial identity
$$
D^2 C_A(X)[R,R]=0,\qquad C_A(X):=DF(X)[A].
$$
Thus $C_A$, expressed in a coordinate $s$ along $R$ and transverse coordinates $Y$, contains at most one power of $s$. This is a global polynomial identity, not an identity only on a selected law.

The kernel plane $U=\ker P_A$ also has the following cubic-closure rule, used in exhausting contact types. For any $Z\in U$ with $q(Z)=0$, rank-one convexity gives $D^2F(A+tW)[Z,Z]\ge0$ for all real $t$ and every matrix $W$. Its value at $t=0$ is zero, so its derivative there vanishes. By quartic symmetry this derivative is $D^2C_A(W)[Z,Z]$. Homogeneity, taking $W=Z$, gives $C_A(Z)=0$. If $q|_U$ is indefinite, then for every $W$ the quadratic form $D^2C_A(W)|_U$ vanishes on the null cone of $q|_U$, hence is a scalar multiple $\ell(W)q|_U$. Symmetry of the cubic tensor, evaluated in a basis diagonalizing the two-dimensional indefinite form, forces the restriction of $\ell$ to $U$ to vanish; consequently $C_A|_U=0$. If $q|_U$ is semidefinite, any determinant-balanced law supported on $U$ is supported on its null line, where the preceding rank-one argument gives $C_A=0$. If $q|_U\equiv0$, every point of $U$ is rank one and $C_A|_U=0$ directly. This is the closure used below; it does not assert that $C_A$ vanishes on all of a semidefinite kernel plane.

### Low-rank lemma at rank-one matrices

The incoming note needs the fact that $\operatorname{rank}D^2F(R)\le2$ at a nonzero rank-one $R$ forces $F$ into an explicit polyconvex class. Normalize $R=e_{11}$. If $F(R)=0$, the rank-one restriction to matrices with a fixed output vector is a convex nonnegative binary quartic with a zero direction. Its fourth root is a seminorm; after an input change, the full invariant polynomial reduces to
$$
a u^2+\gamma q^2+q(Lu+Mv+Nw).
$$
The rank-one Hessians at $e_{12}$ and $e_{11}$ force $L=M=N=0$ and $\gamma\ge0$, so this is in the determinant-square convex class.

If $A_c=F(e_{11})>0$, direct differentiation gives
$$
D^2F(e_{11})=
\begin{pmatrix}
12A_c&3G&0&3L\\
3G&2C+2E&-L&N\\
0&-L&4A_c&G\\
3L&N&G&2C
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
The $(a,c)$ block is positive definite. Rank at most two forces the lower Schur complement to vanish, yielding
$$
C=(G^2+3L^2)/(8A_c),\quad E=(G^2-L^2)/(4A_c),\quad N=GL/(2A_c).
$$
Equivalently,
$$
F=A_c|x_{\mathbb C}+\kappa y_{\mathbb C}|^4+B'v^2+H'vw+M'qv,
\qquad \kappa=(G-\mathrm iL)/(4A_c).
$$
On the kernel of the first term, the rank-one acoustic direction $e_1\otimes(1,t)$ has a nonnegative quadratic whose value at $t=0$ is zero. Its derivative forces $H'(3b^2+d^2)+2M'bd=0$ for all $b,d$, hence $H'=M'=0$; a rank-one test then gives $B'\ge0$. This is a convex sum of fourth powers of norms of linear maps. Therefore, for non-polyconvex $F$, $F(R)>0$ and $\operatorname{rank}D^2F(R)\ge3$. Any singular such Hessian has a one-dimensional kernel.

## Why Type I and Type II exhaust contacts

The contact estimate depends on a complete algebraic classification, not merely on the explicit example $G$.

If $A$ is rank one, reciprocity gives $D^2F(A)\succeq0$. Contact puts $R$ in its kernel. The low-rank lemma rules out a kernel of dimension at least two for non-polyconvex $F$, so this is Type I: $P_A=D^2F(A)$ has rank three and kernel $\mathbb RR$.

For invertible $A$, fixed invertible input-coordinate changes preserve rank-one convexity and left-$SO(2)$ invariance, so set $A=I$. In conformal/anticonformal coordinates $z=i+\mathrm i j$, $w=k+\mathrm i l$, every invariant quartic has the form
$$
f|z|^4+\beta|z|^2|w|^2+g|w|^4+
\operatorname{Re}(\delta z^2\bar w^2+\epsilon|z|^2z\bar w+\eta|w|^2z\bar w).
$$
Subtract the determinant multiplier in the Finsler decomposition and call the PSD remainder $P$, with kernel $U$. The acoustic contact is in $U$. The rank-at-most-one remainder cases also need to be removed. The first two Hessian rows at $I$ are fixed by homogeneity and rotation covariance:
$$
D^2F(I)=
\begin{pmatrix}
12f&0&3a_0&3b_0\\
0&4f&-b_0&a_0\\
3a_0&-b_0&*&*\\
3b_0&a_0&*&*
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
If the determinant-corrected PSD remainder has rank at most one, comparison with a rank-one matrix plus $\lambda\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1)$ forces $a_0=b_0=0$. For $f<0$, its kernel is an indefinite determinant plane containing $I$, contradicting cubic closure and $DF(I)[I]=4f$. For $f>0$, the full polynomial reduces to $fq^2+g|w|^4+\operatorname{Re}(\eta|w|^2z\bar w)$; cubic closure on the kernel kills $\eta$, and a rank-one acoustic test gives $g\ge0$. For $f=0$, the residual is $\tau(\operatorname{Re}(z\bar w))^2+g|w|^4+\operatorname{Re}(\eta|w|^2z\bar w)$. Its value and first derivative vanish on the conformal plane; rank-one segment and acoustic tests eliminate the mixed terms and give $g\ge0$. Each case is in the explicit convex/polyconvex exceptional family. Thus the non-polyconvex invertible-background case has $\operatorname{rank}P=2$ or $3$.

For rank two, the kernel is a plane. Writing $p=12f-\lambda$, $r=4f-\lambda$, a rotation fixing $I$ makes $\epsilon=a\ge0$ real. When $p,r>0$, rank two forces
$$
\operatorname{Im}\delta=0,\quad
\beta=\frac{9a^2/p+a^2/r-2\lambda}{4},\quad
\delta=\frac{9a^2/p-a^2/r}{4},\quad
U:\ i=-3ak/p,\ j=-al/r.
$$
The remaining cases are as follows.

* If $q|_U$ is indefinite, cubic closure $C_A|_U=0$ forces the difference of two cubic coefficients to be $-4\lambda^3a^3/(r^2p^3)$. Hence $\lambda a=0$, and either alternative makes $q|_U$ semidefinite, contradicting this case.
* If $q|_U\equiv0$, the kernel equations force $p=3a$, $r=a$, $\lambda=0$, $a=4f$. Full cubic closure gives $F=f|z+w|^4+(g-f)|w|^4$, a convex member of the exceptional family.
* At $p=0<r$, the kernel contains an indefinite determinant plane and cubic closure contradicts $DF(I)[I]=4f<0$. At $r=0<p$, actual rank-one acoustic tests force the residual mixed coefficients to vanish, again reducing to the exceptional family. The $p=r=0$ kernel is the conformal plane, where determinant is definite, so it cannot contain a rank-one contact.
* The only remaining rank-two case has $q|_U$ semidefinite of rank one. Its two algebraic branches are $p=3a$ and $r=a$. In the first branch, put $V=F(-1,1)>0$ and perturb both the background and rank-one direction. The Schur-corrected second coefficient is
  $$
  -\frac{(p-3r)^4}{324r^2V}.
  $$
  It is negative unless $p=3r$; that equality degenerates to the already excluded polyconvex case. This is a test in an actual rank-one direction, not an SOS ansatz. In the surviving $r=a$ branch, the radical is the shear line $R=e_{12}$. The contact equations give $\operatorname{Im}\eta=0$, fix $\operatorname{Re}\eta$, and make the cubic restriction independent of the radical coordinate.

After the fixed coordinate changes, the two possible non-polyconvex contact types are therefore:

**Type I.** $\operatorname{rank}P_A=3$, $\ker P_A=\mathbb RR$.

**Type II.** $A=I$, $R=e_{12}$, $\lambda\ne0$, and
$$
U=\ker P_A=\{c=0,\ r=d-\lambda a=0\},\quad
P_A\succeq\nu(c^2+r^2),\quad
C_A|_U=\kappa a^3,\quad \partial_b^2 C_A\equiv0.
$$
The $b$-coordinate is the radical/contact-shear coordinate. The last two identities are both needed: the cubic on $U$ can be nonzero, while no term can contain $b^2$.

## Lemma 3.1: mixed-derivative interpolation

For a finite Fourier sum on $\mathbb R^2$ or the coordinate torus, discard modes on the frequency axes, which contribute zero to $\psi_{12}$. Define
$$
T_z\psi=|D_1|^{2z}|D_2|^{2(1-z)}\psi,\qquad 0\le\operatorname{Re}z\le1.
$$
At $\operatorname{Re}z=1$, $T_{1+it}$ is the $L^2$-isometric product of one-dimensional imaginary powers applied to $\psi_{11}$. At $\operatorname{Re}z=0$, $T_{it}$ is the product of those imaginary powers applied to $\psi_{22}$, bounded on $L^4$ by $C(1+|t|)^N\|\psi_{22}\|_4$. These one-dimensional bounds follow from Mikhlin derivative estimates; the product acts successively in the two coordinates. A fixed Gaussian factor $e^{(z-1/2)^2}$ absorbs polynomial growth on the boundary lines. Three-lines interpolation at $z=1/2$ gives
$$
\||D_1||D_2|\psi\|_{8/3}
\le C\|\psi_{11}\|_2^{1/2}\|\psi_{22}\|_4^{1/2},
$$
because $1/(8/3)=\tfrac12(1/2)+\tfrac12(1/4)$. Since $\psi_{12}$ is the product of two coordinate Hilbert transforms applied to $|D_1||D_2|\psi$, their $L^{8/3}$ boundedness gives
$$
\boxed{\|\psi_{12}\|_{8/3}^{2}\le C\|\psi_{11}\|_2\|\psi_{22}\|_4.}
$$
The bound has no Fourier-bandwidth or frequency-aspect-ratio term. The same proof applies to finite Fourier sums on the standard torus, with the coordinate imaginary-power and Hilbert-transform bounds; density gives the Sobolev version. On a general flat lattice, coordinate translation actions and multiplier transference give the same estimate. The incoming note's short large-box-cutoff sentence compresses this last transfer. On $\mathbb R^2$, the Fourier proof is direct.

## Hodge correction and estimate of the surviving cubic

In Type II write $X=D\varphi=\begin{psmallmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{psmallmatrix}$ and $r=d-\lambda a$. Set
$$
\varphi_1^0=\varphi_1+\lambda^{-1}\partial_1\Delta^{-1}r,\qquad
\varphi_2^0=\varphi_2-\partial_2\Delta^{-1}r.
$$
For zero-mean Fourier mode $(\xi,\eta)$, the Riesz symbols are $T_{ij}=\xi_i\xi_j/(\xi^2+\eta^2)$. Direct substitution gives
$$
d_0-\lambda a_0=r-(T_{22}+T_{11})r=0.
$$
Both corrected rows are still gradients, and Riesz transforms give
$$
\|X-X_0\|_p\le C_{p,\lambda}\|r\|_p,\quad p=2,4.
$$
The relation $d_0=\lambda a_0$ is the closedness condition for the stream potential $\psi$ satisfying
$$
\varphi_1^0=\psi_2/\lambda,\quad \varphi_2^0=\psi_1,\quad
a_0=\psi_{12}/\lambda,\quad b_0=\psi_{22}/\lambda,\quad c_0=\psi_{11}.
$$
Therefore Lemma 3.1 and the Hodge estimates imply
$$
\|a_0\|_{8/3}^2\le C_\lambda(\|c\|_2+\|r\|_2)\|X\|_4,
\qquad \|a_0\|_4\le C_\lambda\|Y\|_4.
$$
Interpolate $L^{8/3}$ and $L^4$ to get $\int|a_0|^3\le\|a_0\|_{8/3}^2\|a_0\|_4$. Also
$$
\int|a^3-a_0^3|\le\|a-a_0\|_2(\|a\|_4+\|a_0\|_4)^2,
$$
so
$$
|\mathbb E a^3|\le C_\lambda(\|c\|_2+\|r\|_2)\|X\|_4\|Y\|_4. \tag{A}
$$
This is the new differential input. Determinant balance alone cannot prove it: the incoming note provides a bounded balanced finite law violating the same estimate, while the Fourier/Hodge argument uses actual curl compatibility.

## Theorem 4.1: quantitative cubic compensation

For Type I, write $C_A=sQ_2(Y)+Q_3(Y)$. Since $P_A$ is positive definite transverse to $R$, $h=\mathbb E P_A(X)\ge\nu\|Y\|_2^2$. Polynomial bounds and Hölder give
$$
|\beta|\le C\|Y\|_2\|Y\|_4\|X\|_4
\le K h^{1/2}\|X\|_4\|Y\|_4.
$$
The derivative in the $R$ direction of $C_A$ is quadratic in $Y$, so
$$
|g|\le C\|Y\|_2^2\le K h^{1/2}\|Y\|_2.
$$
For Type II, $h\ge\nu\mathbb E(c^2+r^2)$. Since $C_A-\kappa a^3$ vanishes on $U$, it is divisible by $c$ or $r$; because $\partial_b^2C_A=0$, its cubic monomials have at most one $b$. Thus
$$
|C_A(X)-\kappa a^3|
\le C(|c|+|r|)|Y|(|b|+|Y|).
$$
Hölder controls its average by $C(\|c\|_2+\|r\|_2)\|X\|_4\|Y\|_4$. Apply (A) to $\kappa\mathbb E a^3$, then use the lower bound on $h$. Differentiating the error in $b$ removes $\kappa a^3$ and gives the cross estimate for $g$. Undoing fixed coordinate changes only changes the finite constant $K(F,A,R)$. For every compatible $W^{1,4}$ field this proves
$$
\boxed{|\beta|\le K h^{1/2}\|X\|_4\|Y\|_4,\qquad
|g|\le K h^{1/2}\|Y\|_2.} \tag{B}
$$
If $h=0$, these inequalities force $\beta=g=0$. The constant is contact- and integrand-dependent; no uniformity over $F,A,R$ is asserted.

## What positivity and escaping backgrounds follow

The quartic polarization identity is
$$
\mathbb E[F(B+X)-F(B)]=c_X+b_X:B+\tfrac12 B:H_XB.
$$
On the pure contact line $X=sR$, $D^2F(R)[A,A]=DF(R)[A]=0$ and $F(R)=f_0>0$, so expansion gives
$$
J_F(uR,sR)=f_0(m_4+4um_3+6u^2m_2)
\ge \frac{f_0}{4}(m_4+u^2m_2),
$$
where $m_j=\mathbb E s^j$, $m_1=0$, and $m_3^2\le m_2m_4$. To see the margin, put $x=|u|\sqrt{m_2}$ and $y=\sqrt{m_4}$. The difference after subtracting the right-hand quadratic is at least
$$
\frac{23}{4}x^2-4xy+\frac34y^2
=\frac{23}{4}\left(x-\frac{8}{23}y\right)^2+\frac{5}{92}y^2\ge0.
$$
For $X=sR+Y$, the two smallness assumptions
$$
\|Y\|_2\le\delta\|s\|_2,\qquad \|Y\|_4\le\delta\|s\|_4
$$
give, by quartic expansion,
$$
c_X=f_0m_4+O(\delta m_4),\quad
b_X:R=4f_0m_3+O(\delta\sqrt{m_2m_4}),\quad
R:H_XR=12f_0m_2+O(\delta m_2).
$$
For $B=tA+uR$, minimize first over $t$. The subtraction is
$$
\frac{(\beta+ug)^2}{2h}
\le C\bigl(\|X\|_4^2\|Y\|_4^2+u^2\|Y\|_2^2\bigr)
\le C\delta^2(m_4+u^2m_2).
$$
Choosing $\delta$ small proves strict Jensen positivity uniformly for every $t,u\in\mathbb R$:
$$
\mathbb E[F(tA+uR+X)-F(tA+uR)]
\ge \eta(\|s\|_4^4+u^2\|s\|_2^2).
$$
This is an escaping-background result on one fixed contact plane: $t,u$ are unrestricted. Its input is local in gradient geometry: $X$ must be relatively close to the one-dimensional contact line in both $L^2$ and $L^4$. It does not cover an arbitrary compatible residual after the nonlinear shear, nor an affine background with a component outside $\operatorname{span}\{A,R\}$.

## Smooth compatible sharpness of the $L^4$ factor

For the exact source quartic $G$, $A=I$, $R=e_{12}$, the seed zero-boundary profile
$$
\psi_0(s,t)=(f(s)t(1-t),\ s(1-s)t(1-t)),\quad
f(s)=\begin{cases}2s&s\le1/3,\\1-s&s\ge1/3,\end{cases}
$$
has exactly
$$
\beta_{\psi_0}=283/5670>0,\quad h_{\psi_0}=179/540>0.
$$
The seed is piecewise smooth. Since $C_A(D\psi)$ and $D^2G(D\psi)[A,A]$ are polynomials of degrees three and two, their integrals are continuous under $W^{1,4}$ convergence. Density of $C_c^\infty((0,1)^2)$ in $W^{1,4}_0((0,1)^2)$ therefore gives a fixed smooth compactly supported $\psi$ with $\beta_\psi,h_\psi$ still positive.

Choose a nonzero smooth periodic scalar $\sigma$ of mean zero that vanishes on an open interval; let $h_0'=\sigma$ and $\varphi_0(x,y)=(h_0(y),0)$. Then $D\varphi_0=\sigma(y)R$. For $G$, contact identities give $C_A(sR)=D^2G(sR)[A,A]=0$ pointwise. Put the fixed smooth $\psi$ inside a zero-gradient rectangle and define
$$
\varphi_\rho=\varphi_0+\rho^{1/2}\psi((\cdot-x_*)/\rho).
$$
Every $\varphi_\rho$ is a smooth periodic potential. Disjoint support and homogeneity give
$$
\beta_\rho=\rho^{1/2}\beta_\psi,\qquad
h_\rho=\rho h_\psi,\qquad
\frac{\beta_\rho^2}{2h_\rho}=\frac{\beta_\psi^2}{2h_\psi}>0,
$$
while
$$
\|Y_\rho\|_p=\rho^{2/p-1/2}\|Y_\psi\|_p\longrightarrow0
\quad(1\le p<4),\qquad \sup_\rho\|X_\rho\|_4<\infty.
$$
Thus no uniform version of (B) can replace $\|Y\|_4$ by $\|Y\|_p$, $p<4$, while keeping its other factors. The pointwise gradients grow as $\rho$ decreases, but each field is smooth and belongs to $W^{1,4}$. This sharpness concerns the directional estimate, not Jensen negativity.

The incoming note also contains a smooth periodic three-frequency resonant field. Independent exact Fourier averaging gives $\mathbb E C_I(D\varphi)=85/18\ne0$. Compatibility does not kill cubic resonances by a blanket cancellation rule; the Hodge interpolation, rather than an assumption of zero resonance, does the work.

## Exact boundary of the result

For a full positive-definite averaged Hessian split as
$$
H=\begin{pmatrix}h&k^T\\k&D\end{pmatrix},\qquad
b=(\beta,\gamma),\qquad D\succ0,
$$
the optimized loss contains the reduced term
$$
\frac12\frac{(\beta-k^TD^{-1}\gamma)^2}{h-k^TD^{-1}k}.
$$
The contact estimate controls $\beta^2/h$ and one cross coefficient $g=A:H:R$. It gives no lower bound on the reduced denominator $h-k^TD^{-1}k$, and no matching estimate for the moving reduced numerator. The ratio therefore cannot be substituted for the full Schur loss. This is the point where strong-$L^4$ convergence to a contact line ceases to be enough for the present proof.

In particular:

* The directional contact loss $\beta_n^2/(2h_n)$ tends to zero if $X_n\to sR$ strongly in $L^4$ (or under the equiintegrable one-line covariance hypotheses stated in the incoming note).
* Full strong-$L^4$ continuity of $\tfrac12b_n^TH_n^\dagger b_n$ remains open because the reduced denominator may be much smaller than $h_n$.
* The local contact-plane Jensen inequality holds for all escaping $t,u$, but does not control general coupled residuals after the determinant-preserving nonlinear shear.
* No compatible negative Jensen profile is produced; the exact concentration families have positive actual gaps or establish only a positive directional loss.

No fatal algebraic counterexample to the incoming argument was found. The analytic ingredients are consistent with standard multiplier interpolation, and the finite identities replay exactly. The algebraic Type I/II exhaustion and analytic arguments have not been formalized or externally reviewed, so their mathematical status remains internal/unreviewed.
