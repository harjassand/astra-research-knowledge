Preserve exact population balance
Write
\[
B_V=-\mathcal L_V=S+A_V,
\qquad A_V=-i[H(V),\cdot].
\]
Use an odd trial \(f(V)\), an even trial \(g(V)\), and impose
\[
\boxed{PA_Vf(V)=0}
\]
exactly in the translation-covariance representation. Here \(P\) projects onto occupation-diagonal operators.
Because the varied Hamiltonian term is diagonal, the balance constraint is independent of \(V\). A polynomial family of trial operators can therefore preserve it for every control value, not merely at its expansion point.
Define the corrected transport approximation
\[
\widehat D(V)=4\mathcal F(V),
\]
where
\[
\mathcal F
=
2\langle j,f\rangle_\#
-\langle f,Sf\rangle_\#
+\langle g,Sg\rangle_\#
+2\langle A_Vf,g\rangle_\#.
\]
This differs from simply evaluating \(\langle j,f+g\rangle\). Its error has an exact quadratic residual identity.
The identity that removes the unknown inverse from the error bound
For the proof, temporarily replace \(S\) by \(S+\lambda I\), with \(\lambda>0\), and normalize:
\[
T_{V,\lambda}
=
I+(S+\lambda I)^{-1/2}A_V(S+\lambda I)^{-1/2}.
\]
Let \(R\) be \(+1\) on odd observables and \(-1\) on even observables. Real \(H\) gives
\[
(RB_V)^*=RB_V.
\]
With \(r_\lambda\) the complete normalized residual,
\[
\boxed{
\kappa_{n,\lambda}-\mathcal F_\lambda
=
\langle r_\lambda,R\,T_{V,\lambda}^{-1}r_\lambda\rangle,
\qquad \kappa=D/4.
}
\]
At every real control value,
\[
\|T_{V,\lambda}^{-1}\|\le1.
\]
The normalized control perturbation obeys
\[
\left\|
(S+\lambda I)^{-1/2}A_1(S+\lambda I)^{-1/2}
\right\|
\le w,
\]
where
\[
A_1=\frac{dA_V}{dV},
\qquad
w=\frac{4J_*(H_1)}d.
\]
For the implemented next-nearest-neighbour control, \(w=1\).
These bounds are independent of the system size and of an unknown population relaxation time. The temporary \(\lambda\) is a proof device, not physical damping added to the simulation. Exact balance makes its removal legitimate.
3. A general derivative-error theorem
Represent the trial operators by finite polynomials around \(V_0\):
\[
f(V_0+s)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}f_k\frac{s^k}{k!},
\qquad
g(V_0+s)=\sum_{k=0}^{M}g_k\frac{s^k}{k!}.
\]
Generate their complete residual derivatives:
\[
r^o_k
=
Sf_k+A_Vg_k+kA_1g_{k-1}-\delta_{k0}j,
\]
\[
r^e_k
=
Sg_k+A_Vf_k+kA_1f_{k-1}.
\]
Set
\[
q_k^2
=
\|r^o_k\|_{\#,S^+}^2+
\|r^e_k\|_{\#,S^+}^2.
\]
Every \(q_k\) is calculated from the actual finite operators, including all commutator outputs outside the discovery dictionary.
Differentiating the quadratic error identity gives
\[
\boxed{
|D^{(m)}-\widehat D^{(m)}|
\le
4m!
\sum_{a+b+c=m}
\frac{q_aq_b}{a!\,b!}w^c.
}
\]
In particular,
\[
\boxed{
|D'-\widehat D'|
\le
4(2q_0q_1+wq_0^2),
}
\]
and
\[
\boxed{
|D''-\widehat D''|
\le
4\left(
2q_0q_2+2q_1^2+4wq_0q_1+2w^2q_0^2
\right).
}
\]
The proof uses
\[
\|\partial_V^cT^{-1}\|\le c!w^c
\]
and the product rule applied to the two residual factors. It requires neither an accurately represented exact inverse nor a small interaction parameter.
Why this can be more useful than a value certificate
Suppose the response residual and its first derivative are both \(O(\eta)\). Then the derivative error is \(O(\eta^2)\).
If curvature is also certified and the selected point is nearly stationary, its design regret becomes \(O(\eta^4)\), even while the absolute value interval remains \(O(\eta^2)\).
That is the consequential difference:
A physical design decision can require substantially less inverse accuracy than an equally precise absolute transport prediction.

This statement does not assume that curvature is favourable. The curvature must itself pass the second-derivative certificate.
