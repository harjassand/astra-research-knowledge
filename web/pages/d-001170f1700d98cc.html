Abstract

QMC designs were introduced in a 2014 paper by Brauchart, Saff and the present authors (Math. Comp. 83:2821–2851). They represent a novel approach to cubature on the sphere \(\mathbb {S}^d\), in which instead of requiring cubature rules to be exact for polynomials up to a certain degree, a sequence of cubature rules is a QMC design sequence if the worst-case error for functions in a Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) is of order \(\mathcal {O}(N^{-s/d})\), where N is the number of cubature points. The original paper considered only equal cubature weights, but the present paper allows positive weights that sum to 1. After reviewing the known results, the present paper presents necessary and sufficient conditions for QMC design sequences, one of which requires only a finite sum for each tested value of N. The paper also gives experimental results that aim to give insight into the design of QMC designs, and extends the known QMC designs to a 3-dimensional example.

1 Introduction

This paper is concerned with the design and analysis of cubature rules on the sphere, meaning approximations to integrals \(\mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \) over the unit sphere \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\subset \mathbb {R}^{d+1}\)

$$ \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) := \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} f({\varvec{x}}) {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) , $$

where f is a continuous function on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\), and \(\sigma _d\) is the normalized Lebesgue measure, so that

$$ \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = 1. $$

The cubature rules take the form

$$ \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) := \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} f({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}) $$

where \({\varvec{w}}_N = (w_{N,j}, j = 1,\ldots ,N)\) are real weights associated with the nodes \(X_N = ({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \in \mathbb {S}^{d}, j = 1,\ldots N)\).

Most cubature rules on the sphere rely on polynomial exactness, that is \(\mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; p\right) = \mathcal {I}\left( p \right) \) for all spherical polynomials p up to some degree. In contrast, a recent paper [9] by Brauchart, Saff and the current authors introduced a different approach, building not on polynomial exactness, but rather on the rate of decay of an infinite sequence of cubature rules

$$ \mathcal {Q} = \{\mathcal {Q}(X_{N_1}, {\varvec{w}}_{N_1}), \mathcal {Q}(X_{N_2}, {\varvec{w}}_{N_2}), \ldots , N_1< N_2 < \cdots \} $$

with a specified rate of convergence for functions in a given smoothness class specified in terms of the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\), see Section 2.3 for details.

Definition 1.1

Let \(s>d/2\). A sequence of cubature rules \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is said to be a QMC design sequence for the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) if there exists \(c_{s,d }> 0\) and an infinite sequence \(N_k \in \mathbb {N}\) such that for all \(f \in \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\)

$$\begin{aligned} \left| Q(X_{N_k}, {\varvec{w}}_{N_k};f) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| \le \frac{c_{s,d}}{N_k^{s/d}} \; \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})}, \quad k \in \mathbb {N}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (1.1)

The strength of a QMC design sequence is the supremum of the values of s for which (1.1) holds.

The paper [9] assumed all the cubature weights are equal and given by \(w_{N,j} = 1/N, j = 1,\ldots ,N\). Here we allow positive weights \(w_{N,j} \in \mathbb {R}, j = 1,\ldots ,N\), except that, for simplicity, we assume throughout that

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} = 1, \end{aligned}$$

                    (1.2)

so that the cubature rule integrates constants exactly.

By definition, the strength \(s^*\) of a QMC design sequence satisfies \(s^* > d/2\). The concept of strength is illustrated in Section 1.1 for \(d = 2\) with examples from [9], and for a \(d = 3\) example is discussed in Sections 1.3 and 5.

Remark 1.1

The condition (1.1) is far from trivial, since if \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is a QMC design sequence for \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) then for every polynomial p on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) we must have

$$ \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; p\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( p \right) \right| = \mathcal {O}(N^{-s/d}) \quad \text{ as }\quad N\rightarrow \infty , $$

(with the implied constant depending on d, s and p) because \(p \in \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) for every \(s>d/2\).

The aim of the present paper, extending the work of [9], is to further develop our understanding of the properties of QMC design sequences, and to seek reliable tests for determining the existence and the strength of QMC design sequences.

QMC design sequences are not rare. The first point to note is that essentially all of the known cubature rules that rely on polynomial exactness, see for example [13], belong to QMC design sequences because of the following theorem. It was established by K. Hesse and colleagues, first for the case \(d = 2\) and \(s = 3/2\) in [11], then extended to \(d = 2\) and all \(s > 1\) in [12], and finally to all \(d \ge 2\) and all \(s >d/2\) in [8]. That the theorem is sharp is shown by a matching lower bound established in [10].

Theorem 1.1

[8] For \(d \ge 2\) let \((\mathcal {Q}(X_{N(t)}, {\varvec{w}}_{N(t)})\) be a sequence of N(t)-point positive-weight cubature rules on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) with \(t \rightarrow \infty \), satisfying \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N(t)}, {\varvec{w}}_{N(t)}; p) = \mathcal {I}\left( p \right) \) for all \(p \in \mathbb {P}_t(\mathbb {S}^{d})\), the space of all spherical polynomials of degree at most t on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\). Then for all \(s > d/2\) there exist constants \(c_{s,d}>0\) such that

$$ |\mathcal {Q}(X_{N(t)}, {\varvec{w}}_{N(t)}; f) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) | \le \frac{c_{s,d}}{t^{s}} \Vert f\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^s(\mathbb {S}^{d})}, \quad f \in \mathbb {H}^s(\mathbb {S}^{d}). $$

Corollary 1.1

Let \((\mathcal {Q}(X_{N(t)}, {\varvec{w}}_{N(t)})\) be a sequence of rules satisfying \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N(t)},\)\( {\varvec{w}}_{N(t)}; p) = \mathcal {I}\left( p \right) \) for all \(p \in \mathbb {P}_t(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) and also satisfying \(N(t) = \mathcal {O}(t^d)\). Then the sequence is a QMC design sequence belonging to \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) for all \(s > d/2\).

The growth condition in the corollary is satisfied for essentially all practical cubature rules, including for example appropriate product Gauss rules, see [13]. The paper [9] considered the particular case of spherical t-designs, which are simply equal weight cubature rules exact for all polynomials of degree at most t. It is known from the work of Bondarenko et al.[4, 5] that for all \(t \ge 0\), spherical t-designs with \(N =\mathcal {O}(t^d)\) points exist, satisfying the growth condition in the corollary.

Our interest in the present paper is mainly in rules that do not have a polynomial exactness property. The paper [9] observed that QMC designs of this kind are also plentiful. The condition in Definition 1.1 can be restated as requiring that the worst-case error for functions in the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) (see Section 2.3 for the definitions) is of order \(\mathcal {O}(N^{-s/d})\). The worst-case error depends of course on the particular cubature rule. A trivial observation is that if for some \(s> d/2\) and a given sequence of cubature rules \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) there exists a sequence of polynomially exact cubature rules with a larger value of \(c_{s,d}\) in (1.1) then the sequence \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is also a QMC design sequence for that value of s. In particular, for every \(s>d/2\) a sequence of cubature rules that minimize the worst-case errors is necessarily a QMC design sequence.

For that reason the worst-case error is a prime tool in the study of QMC design sequences. We shall see in Section 2.3 that a concrete expression is available for the worst-case error, but that in general it involves an infinite sum, which is of itself not useful for computation. Nevertheless the paper [9] was able to identify with some certainty some candidates and strengths for practical QMC design sequences, by exploiting the fact that in some special cases (and only when \(2s - d\) is not an even integer) closed form expressions for the worst-case error are available.

As an example, the paper [9], Section 5, proved that equal weight cubature sequences that maximize the generalized sum of Euclidean distances

$$ \sum _{j = 1}^N\sum _{i = 1}^N|{\varvec{x}}_{N,j}-{\varvec{x}}_{N,i}|^{2s-d}, \quad N = 2,3,4, \ldots , $$

are QMC design sequences on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) when \(d/2<s < d/2+1\), with the proof of this result resting on the availability of a related closed form expression for the worst-case error in \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\).

1.1 Conjectured QMC Design Sequences and Strengths for \(d = 2\)

The paper [9] demonstrated examples of QMC design sequences with equal weights, while [20] discusses QMC design strength for some random configurations. The sequences studied in [9] include sequences of spherical designs (where a spherical t-design is an equal weight cubature rule that integrates exactly all spherical polynomials of degree \(\le t\)), and also specific rules not relying on polynomial exactness. Examples for \(\mathbb {S}^{2}\) include generalized spiral points from Saff and Kuijlaars [16], Rakhmanov, Saff and Zhou [15], and a modified version Bauer [2] introduced for star catalogs, which appear to be QMC designs of strength 3. Fibonacci points [1] have a similar spiral construction. Other examples are a point set chosen to maximize the sum of Euclidean distances (apparent strength 4); and a (Fekete) point set chosen to maximize the determinant with respect to a polynomial basis (apparent strength 1.5). Note that point sets chosen to minimize/maximize a criterion cannot be quickly generated for large N, and can be unreliable for estimating strengths. On the other hand fast methods such as equal area partitions and generalized spiral constructions are currently restricted to \(\mathbb {S}^{2}\), except for Leopardi’s equal area partititon points [14].

1.2 A New Focus

In the present paper we seek a test of QMC designs without the inconvenient restriction that \(2s - d\) not be an even integer. We do this by finding (see Theorem 3.2 ) a test that for each value of N requires only a finite sum.

That test is built from an easily computable quantity that will play a key role:

$$\begin{aligned} A_{N,\ell }:= A_{N,\ell }^{(d)}(X_N, {\varvec{w}}_N) := \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k = 1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}\cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) , \end{aligned}$$

                    (1.3)

where \({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}\) is the standard Euclidean inner product for elements of \(\mathbb {R}^{d+1}\), \({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \in \mathbb {S}^{d}\) for \(j=1,\ldots ,N\) are the points with weights \(w_{N,j}\) of a cubature rule \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}){}\), and \(P_{\ell }^{(d)}(z)\) is the ultraspherical (Gegenbauer) polynomial of degree \(\ell \) for \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) normalized so that \(P_{\ell }^{(d)}(1) = 1\). Thus, for \(\ell = 0\) we have from (1.2)

$$ A_{N,0} = \left( \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j}\right) ^2 = 1. $$

In particular, for the equal-weight case in which \(w_{N,j} =1/N\) the expression for \(A_{N,\ell }\) simplifies to

$$ A_{N,\ell }= \frac{1}{N^2}\sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k = 1}^N P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}\cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}). $$

To help in understanding the role of \(A_{N,\ell }\) it may be useful to note that (as proved in Proposition 2.1 below) the cubature rule \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) integrates all polynomials of degree \(t \ge 0 \) exactly if and only if \(\sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} = 1\) and \(A_{N,\ell }= 0\) for \(\ell = 1, \ldots t\). Thus for \(\ell \ge 1\) departure of \(A_{N,\ell }\) from zero is a measure of the failure to integrate polynomials exactly. The \(A_{N,\ell }\) play a critical role in the necessary and sufficient conditions for a sequence of cubature rules to be a QMC design sequence, see Section 3.

The structure of the paper is as follows. Section 2 contains preliminaries, along with a new result in which boundedness of \(c_{s,d}\) ensures that the convergence property (1.1) extends to \(s = s^*\), the strength. Section 3 gives necessary and sufficient conditions expressed in terms of \(A_{N,\ell }\) under which a sequence of cubature rules on the sphere is a QMC design sequence. In particular, Theorem 3.2 gives a necessaary and sufficient condition which is computationally feasible, in that for each N the test requires only the evaluation of a finite sum. Section 4 discusses properties of \(A_{N,\ell }\), while Section 5 reports on numerical experiments for \(d=2\) and \(d = 3\) that explore the practical ability of Theorem 3.2 to identify QMC design sequences and strengths. We shall also explore there a double cubature sum approach to integrals of zonal functions, which under suitable conditions can give a doubled convergence rate for a fixed QMC design sequences at the cost of \(N^2\) rather than N function evaluations.

2 Preliminaries

2.1 Spherical Harmonics

Let \(\{Y_{\ell ,m}\}\) be a set of orthonormal real spherical harmonics on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) satisfying

$$ \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}) Y_{\ell ',m'}({\varvec{x}}) {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = \delta _{\ell , \ell '} \delta _{m,m'}, \ \quad \ell = 0,\ldots \text{ and } m = 1, \ldots , Z(d, \ell ), $$

where \(\delta _{\ell , \ell '}\) is the Kronecker delta and \(Z(d, \ell )\), the dimension of the space of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree \(\ell \) on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\), satisfies \(Z(d,0):= 1\) and for \(\ell \ge 1\)

$$\begin{aligned} Z(d, \ell ):= (2\ell + d -1) \frac{\Gamma (\ell + d - 1)}{\Gamma (d) \Gamma (\ell + 1)} \sim \frac{2}{\Gamma (d)} \ell ^{d-1} \text{ as } \ell \rightarrow \infty . \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.1)

We shall need the addition theorem

$$\begin{aligned} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) = \frac{1}{Z(d, \ell )} \sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d, \ell )} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}) Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{y}}). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.2)

The quantity \(A_{N,\ell }\) defined in (1.3) can now be written in terms of spherical harmonics, giving

$$\begin{aligned} Z(d, \ell )A_{N,\ell }=&\sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N \sum _{m=1}^{Z(d,\ell )} w_{N,j} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}) w_{N,k} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) \nonumber \\ =&\sum _{m =1}^{Z(d, \ell )} \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; Y_{\ell ,m}\right) \right| ^2 \ge 0. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.3)

The quantity \(A_{N,\ell }\) can also be expressed in an arguably more informative form as a combination of squared cubature errors:

Proposition 2.1

Let \(\ell \ge 1\). The quantity \(A_{N, \ell }\) defined in (1.3) satisfies

$$\begin{aligned} Z(d, \ell )A_{N,\ell }= \sum _{m =1}^{Z(d, \ell )} \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; Y_{\ell ,m}\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( Y_{\ell , m} \right) \right| ^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.4)

Hence, \(\mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; p\right) \) is exact for all polynomials p on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) of degree at most t if and only if

$$ \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} = 1 \quad \text{ and }\quad A_{N,\ell } = 0, \quad \ell = 1,\ldots , t. $$

Proof

This follows immediately from (2.3) because \(\mathcal {I}\left( Y_{\ell , m} \right) = 0\) for \(\ell \ge 1\). \(\square \)

2.2 Jacobi Polynomials

In terms of the Jacobi polynomials \(P_\ell ^{(\alpha ,\beta )}\), which are orthogonal over \([-1, 1]\) with respect to the weight function \(w_{\alpha ,\beta }(z) = (1 - z)^\alpha (1+z)^\beta \), we have, with \(\alpha = \frac{d-2}{2}\),

$$ P_{\ell }^{(d)}(z) = \frac{P_\ell ^{(\alpha , \alpha )}(z)}{P_\ell ^{(\alpha , \alpha )}(1)}, $$

where [19, (4.1.1)]

$$ P_\ell ^{(\alpha ,\beta )}(1) = \frac{\Gamma (\ell +\alpha +1)}{\Gamma (\ell +1) \Gamma (\alpha +1)} . $$

Moreover, from [19, Theorem 7.32.2, p. 168], and the normalization \(P_{\ell }^{(d)}(1) = 1\),

$$ |P_{\ell }^{(d)}(z)| \le 1, \quad z \in [-1, 1]. $$

Thus, for any set of N points \(X_N \subset \mathbb {S}^{d}\) and positive weights \(w_N \in \mathbb {R}^{N}\) satisfying (1.2),

$$\begin{aligned} |A_{N, \ell }| \le \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} = A_{N,0} = 1, \quad \ell \ge 1. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.5)

Any strictly positive linear combination of \(P_{\ell }^{(d)}, \ell = 1,\ldots , t\) provides a variational characterization of spherical t-designs, see [22] for example. In particular, using [19, (4.5.3)]

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\ell =0}^t Z(d,\ell ) P_{\ell }^{(d)}(z) = b_{d,t}P_t^{\left( \frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right) }(z), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.6)

where

$$\begin{aligned} b_{d,t}:= \frac{\Gamma (\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma (d)} \frac{\Gamma (t+d)}{\Gamma (t+\frac{d}{2})} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.7)

Hence

$$\begin{aligned} 1 + \sum _{\ell =1}^t Z(d, \ell )A_{N,\ell } = b_{d,t}\sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N w_{N, j} w_{N, k} P_t^{\left( \frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right) }({\varvec{x}}_j \cdot {\varvec{x}}_k). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.8)

Thus a cubature rule applied to the Jacobi polynomial \(P_t^{\left( \frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right) }\) provides information on a strictly positive linear combination of the \(A_{N,\ell }\) for \(\ell = 1,\ldots ,t\), or cubature errors by Proposition 2.1.

As \(P_{\ell }^{(d)}\) are orthogonal with respect to the weight function \(w_{\alpha ,\alpha }(z) = (1 - z^2)^\alpha \), again with \(\alpha = \frac{d-2}{2}\), multiplying both sides of (2.6) by \(P_{0}^{(d)}(z) = 1\) and integrating gives

$$ b_{d,t}\int _{-1}^1 P_t^{\left( \frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right) }(z) \left( 1 - z^2\right) ^\frac{d-2}{2} {\,\textrm{d}}z = \int _{-1}^1 \left( 1 - z^2\right) ^\frac{d-2}{2} {\,\textrm{d}}z = \frac{\sqrt{\pi }\Gamma (\frac{d}{2})}{\Gamma (\frac{d+1}{2})} = \frac{\omega _d}{\omega _{d-1}}, $$

where \(\omega _d = |\mathbb {S}^{d}|\) is the surface area of \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\). Then, as for any \({\varvec{y}}\in \mathbb {S}^{d}\),

$$ \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) \; {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = {\left\{ \begin{array}{ll}1 &  \ell = 0\\ 0 &  \ell > 0,\end{array}\right. } $$

it follows by integrating both sides of (2.6) for \(z = {\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}\) with respect to \({\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}})\) that

$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} P_t^{\left( \frac{d}{2},\frac{d-2}{2}\right) }({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) \; {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = \frac{1}{b_{d,t}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.9)

This may also be thought of in terms of the Funk-Hecke formula

$$\begin{aligned} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} f({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) \; {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = \frac{\omega _{d-1}}{\omega _d} \int _{-1}^1 f(z) (1 - z^2)^{\frac{d-2}{2}} {\,\textrm{d}}z. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.10)

2.3 Sobolev Spaces and Worst Case Error

We now define the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) . A spherical harmonic \(Y_{\ell ,m}\) of degree \(\ell \) is an eigenfunction of the negative Laplace-Beltrami operator \(-\Delta ^*\) for \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) with eigenvalue

$$ \lambda _\ell = \ell (\ell + d -1). $$

Let \(f\in \mathbb {L}_2(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) and define the Laplace-Fourier coefficients \(\widehat{f}_{\ell ,m}:= \mathcal {I}\left( f Y_{\ell ,m} \right) \). The Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) can be defined as the space of all such functions for which

$$ \sum _{\ell =0}^\infty \sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d,\ell )} (1 + \lambda _\ell )^s \left| \widehat{f}_{\ell ,m} \right| ^2 < \infty . $$

As in [9] it is convenient to use an equivalent norm

$$\begin{aligned} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})} := \left( \sum _{\ell =0}^\infty \sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d,\ell )} \frac{1}{a_\ell ^{(s)}} \left| \widehat{f}_{\ell ,m} \right| ^2 \right) ^{1/2} , \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.11)

where \(a_\ell ^{(s)}\) are positive numbers satisfying

$$\begin{aligned} a_\ell ^{(s)}\asymp (1 + \lambda _\ell )^{-s} \asymp (1 + \ell )^{-2s} . \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.12)

Here we write \(a_\ell \asymp b_\ell \) to mean that there exist positive constants \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) independent of \(\ell \) such that \(c_1 a_\ell \le b_\ell \le c_2 a_\ell \) for all \(\ell \). Note, in particular, that (2.11) implies

$$\begin{aligned} \Vert Y_{\ell ,m}\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a_\ell ^{(s)}}}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.13)

Some functions lying in specified smoothness classes \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) are given by Brauchart [6].

The space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) is well known to be a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with reproducing kernel

$$ K^{(s)}({\varvec{x}}, {\varvec{y}}) = 1 + \sum _{\ell = 1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)}\sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d,\ell )} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}) Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{y}}). $$

The worst-case error \(e[\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}); \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})]\) of the cubature rule \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) in the space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) is defined by

$$ e[\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}); \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})]$$

$$:= \textrm{sup}\{ |\mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) | : f \in \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d}),\; \Vert f\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})}\le 1\} . $$

The worst-case error is expressible in terms of the reproducing kernel,

$$\begin{aligned} e[\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}); \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})]^2 = \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N w_{N.j} w_{N,k} \mathcal {K}^{(s)}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}, {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}), \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.14)

where

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {K}^{(s)}({\varvec{x}}, {\varvec{y}}) := K^{(s)}({\varvec{x}}, {\varvec{y}})-1 = \sum _{\ell = 1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)}\sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d,\ell )} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}) Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{y}}). \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.15)

Using (2.4), the squared worst-case error can also be expressed as

$$\begin{aligned} e[\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}); \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})]^2&= \sum _{\ell = 1}^\infty \sum _{m = 1}^{Z(d,\ell )} a_\ell ^{(s)}\left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; Y_{\ell ,m}\right) \right| ^2 \nonumber \\&= \sum _{\ell = 1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)}\, Z(d,\ell )\, A_{N,\ell }. \end{aligned}$$

                    (2.16)

2.4 The Constant \(c_{s,d}\)

Thus far we have not paid any attention to the constant \(c_{s,d}\) in the definition of QMC design sequences, see (1.1). It is natural to expect that \(c_{s,d}\) will increase as s approaches the strength \(s^*\). In the special case in which s is bounded in a neighborhood of \(s^*\) then the following (new) result shows that the QMC design sequence property extends to \(s = s^*\).

Theorem 2.1

Assume that a sequence of cubature rules \(Q(X_N, {\varvec{w}}_N)\) on \(\mathbb {S}^d\) is a QMC design sequence of strength \(s^*>d/2\), with the property that in some neighborhood of \(s^*\) the constant \(c_{s,d}\) is bounded by \(c_d>0\) independently of s. Then

$$\begin{aligned} \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| \le \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \; \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}, \quad f \in \mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d}), \; N \in \mathbb {N}. \end{aligned}$$

Proof

Let \(s \in [s_0, s^*)\), where \([s_0, s^*)\) is an interval in which \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is a QMC design sequence for \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^d)\) with \(c_{s,d}\) bounded above by \(c_d >0\). Then

$$\begin{aligned} \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| \le \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s/d}} \; \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s}({\mathbb {S}^d})}\le \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s/d}} \; \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}. \end{aligned}$$

Thus

$$\begin{aligned}&\left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| - \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}\\&\quad \le \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s/d}} \; \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})} -\frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}\\&\quad = c_{d} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}\left( \frac{1}{N^{s/d}} - \frac{1}{N^{s^*/d}}\right) . \end{aligned}$$

A trivial consequence is

$$ \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| - \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})} \le \frac{c_{d} \Vert f\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}}{N^{s/d}} \le \frac{c_{d} \Vert f\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}}{N^{s_0/d}}, $$

in the last step using \(s > s_0\). It follows that given \(\epsilon > 0\) we have

$$\begin{aligned}&\left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| - \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})} \le \epsilon \\&\text{ for } \text{ all } N \ge N_0 := \Bigg \lceil \left( \frac{c_d \Vert f\Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}}({\mathbb {S}^d})}{\epsilon }\right) ^{d/s_0}\Bigg \rceil . \end{aligned}$$

On the other hand for \(N < N_0\) we already have

$$ \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| - \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})} = c_{d} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})}\left( \frac{1}{N^{s/d}} - \frac{1}{N^{s^*/d}}\right) , $$

in which the right-hand side converges to zero as \(s \rightarrow s^*\) for each N in the finite set \(\{1, \ldots , N_0\}\), thus there exists \(\delta \) such that for all N we have, for \(s^*-s < \delta \),

$$ \left| \mathcal {Q}\left( X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}; f\right) - \mathcal {I}\left( f \right) \right| - \frac{c_{d}}{N^{s^*/d}} \Vert f \Vert _{\mathbb {H}^{s^*}({\mathbb {S}^d})} < \epsilon . $$

Since \(\epsilon \) is arbitrary, the result follows. \(\square \)

Remark 2.1

In all the numerical experiments in [9] the condition in Theorem 2.1, that there is a bounding constant \(c_d\) independent of s in a neighborhood of \(s^*\), appears to be satisfied.

For a particular test function on \(\mathbb {S}^{2}\) the Bauer and Fibonacci spiral points have squared cubature errors decaying like \(2.8 N^{-3.0}\) and \(8.9 N^{-3.0}\) respectively, both corresponding to strength \(s^* = 3\), but with different constants \(c_{s,d}\), illustrating that different QMC design sequences of the same strength can have different coefficients \(c_{s,d}\).

3 QMC Sequence: Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

In this section we give necessary and sufficient conditions for a sequence of point sets to be a QMC design sequence of strength at least s.

We first note a simple necessary condition on \(A_{N,\ell }\) that is a consequence of Proposition 2.1.

Corollary 3.1

Let \(s > d/2\). A necessary condition for a sequence of cubature rules \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) to be a sequence of QMC designs for \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) is

$$ A_{N,\ell } \le c_\ell N^{-2s/d}, \quad \ell \in \mathbb {N}, $$

for some positive sequence \(c_\ell \).

Proof

This follows immediately from (2.4) in Proposition 2.1 and Remark 1.1 with \(p = Y_{\ell ,m}, m = 1,\ldots ,Z(d, \ell )\). \(\square \)

Note that an explicit expression for \(c_\ell \), namely \(c_\ell = \frac{1}{a_\ell ^{(s)}} c_{s,d}^2\), where \(c_{s,d}\) is as in (1.1), follows by using (2.13) in (1.1).

The following theorem, generalizing a result in [9] to general positive weights, gives necessary and sufficient conditions that must hold for every QMC design sequence.

Theorem 3.1

(Necessary and sufficient conditions) Let \(s > d/2\). A sequence of cubature rules \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is a QMC design sequence for the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) if and only if for some \(c_{s,d}>0\)

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k = 1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k}\mathcal {K}^{(s)}{({\varvec{x}}_{N,j},{\varvec{x}}_{N,k})} \le c_{s,d}^2\, N^{-2s/d}, \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.1)

or equivalently, if and only if

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\ell = 1}^{\infty } a_\ell ^{(s)}\, Z(d, \ell )\, A_{N,\ell }\le c_{s,d}^2\, N^{-2s/d}. \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.2)

Proof

The first result follows immediately from (2.14), the second from (2.16). \(\square \)

3.1 A Modified Necessary and Sufficient Condition

The condition (3.2) in Theorem 3.1 for a given N involves a sum over an infinite number of \(\ell \) values. The following modified theorem below provides a necessary and sufficient condition involving only a finite number of \(\ell \) values. It can still not assert definitively that a given cubature sequence is a QMC design sequence because it involves an infinite number of values of N, but it can settle the question for a bounded range of N values.

Theorem 3.2

Let \(s > d/2\), and define L(N) by

$$\begin{aligned} L(N):= L_{s,d}(N) := \Big \lceil N^{2s/(d(2s-d))} \Big \rceil . \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.3)

A sequence of cubature rules \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is a QMC design sequence for the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) if and only if for some \(C_{s,d}>0\)

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {R}_{s,d}(X_N, {\varvec{w}}_N):= \sum _{\ell = 1}^{L(N)} a_\ell ^{(s)}\, Z(d, \ell )\, A_{N,\ell }\le C_{s,d}^2\, N^{-2s/d} \end{aligned}$$

                    (3.4)

holds for some \(C_{s,d}\) independent of N, in which case (3.1) holds for some \(c_{s, d}\) independent of N.

Proof

It is trivial that the condition (3.4) is necessary, as it is clearly a weaker condition than (3.2). To prove sufficiency, since \(A_{N,\ell }\le 1\), from (2.16) we have

$$\begin{aligned} e[\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N}); \mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})]^2&= \sum _{\ell = 1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)} Z(d,\ell )A_{N,\ell }\\&= \sum _{\ell = 1}^{L(N)} a_\ell ^{(s)} Z(d,\ell )A_{N,\ell }+ \sum _{\ell = L(N)+1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)} Z(d,\ell )A_{N,\ell }\\&\ \le \frac{C_{s,d}}{N^{2s/d}} + \sum _{\ell = L(N)+1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)} Z(d,\ell ). \end{aligned}$$

The second sum is bounded, using (2.1) and (2.12), by

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{\ell =L(N)+1}^\infty a_\ell ^{(s)} Z(d,\ell )&\le c\sum _{\ell = L(N)+1}^\infty \ell ^{-(2s-d+1)} \\&\le c \frac{1}{2s-d}(L(N))^{-(2s-d)} \\&\le c' N^{-2s/d}, \end{aligned}$$

where we used the integral test for sums. Putting the two bounds together completes the proof. \(\square \)

The exponent of N in (3.3) is illustrated in Figure 2 for \(d = 2, 3, 4\) and a range of \(s > \frac{d}{2}\). Note that \(L_{s,d}(N) < N\) (clearly computationally desirable) only when

$$ s > \frac{d^2}{2(d-1)}. $$

In particular, for \(d = 2\), \(L_{s,2}(N) < N\) only when \(s > 2\). Also, \(\lim _{s\rightarrow \infty } L_{s,d}(N) = N^{1/d}\), so the lowest values of \(L_{s,d}(N)\) occur for largest values of the smoothness parameter s.

For smaller values of s, in particular when \(L_{s,d}(N) > N\), see Figure 2, it is preferable to use a closed form for the worst-case error when this is available. Such a closed form expression is provided by the negative of the generalized distance kernel \(|{\varvec{x}}- {\varvec{y}}|^{2s - d} = (2(1-{\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}))^{s - d/2}\) (after subtracting the integral of the kernel), see [3, 18, 21]. In particular, as pointed out in the Introduction, points that maximize \(\sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N |{\varvec{x}}_{N,j} - {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}|^{2s-d}\) are known to be QMC designs for all \(\frac{d}{2}< s < \frac{d}{2} + 1\). This can be extended to the case \(s > \frac{d}{2} + 1\), but with the unavoidable limitation that \(2s-d\) is not be an even integer – unavoidable because when \(2s-d\) is an even integer the generalized sum of distances is a polynomial in \({\varvec{x}}\) and \({\varvec{y}}\); see [9] for details. (When \(2s - d\) is an even integer the addition of a logarithmic factor allows an infinite expansion rather than a polynomial, see [7].)

4 Properties of \(A_{N,\ell }\)

In this section we explore properties of \(A_{N,\ell }\) defined in (1.3), and represented as a double cubature sum in (2.4),

$$ A_{N,\ell }= \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k = 1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}\cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}). $$

In this form \(A_{N,\ell }\) can be seen as an approximation to the double integral

$$ \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}})\, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}})\, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{y}}), $$

or as an \(N^2\)-point cubature approximation to the 1-dimensional integral (from the Funk-Hecke formula (2.10))

$$ \frac{\omega _{d-1}}{\omega _d} \int _{-1}^1 P_{\ell }^{(d)}(z) \left( 1 - z^2\right) ^\frac{d-2}{2} dz . $$

The integrals are of course equal, both having the value zero if \(\ell \ge 1\).

The cubature points are all the inner products \(({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}\cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k})\) for \(j = 1,\ldots ,N\), \(k = 1,\ldots , N\). For the double sum to be small it is clear that distribution of those inner products must be rather special, but it is not clear what makes for a good distribution.

To gain insight into that question, we consider in the next theorem the base case of N points randomly and independently distributed uniformly on the sphere \(\mathbb {S}^2\).

Proposition 4.1

Assume that N points \({\varvec{x}}_1, \ldots , {\varvec{x}}_N\) are distributed independently and uniformly on the sphere \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\subset \mathbb {R}^{d+1}\). Then the inner products \(z = {\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}\) for distinct points have probability density function

$$ \rho _d(z) = \frac{\Gamma \left( \frac{d+1}{2}\right) }{\sqrt{\pi }\Gamma \left( \frac{d}{2}\right) } \left( 1 - z^2\right) ^{\frac{d-2}{2}}, \quad -1 \le z \le 1. $$

In particular, for \(d = 2\), the inner products are uniformly distributed on \([-1, 1]\).

Proof

This is a standard result as uniformly distributed points \({\varvec{x}}\in \mathbb {S}^{d}\) correspond to normalized points \({\varvec{x}}= \frac{{\varvec{u}}}{\Vert {\varvec{u}}\Vert }\), where \({\varvec{u}}\sim N(0, I)\) follows the multivariate normal distribution in \(\mathbb {R}^{d+1}\). Rotational invariance then allows \({\varvec{y}}\) to be chosen as the unit vector \({\varvec{e}}_1 \in \mathbb {R}^{d+1}\), so

$$ {\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}= \frac{{\varvec{u}}\cdot {\varvec{e}}_1}{\Vert {\varvec{u}}\Vert } = \frac{u_1}{\left( \sum _{i=1}^{d+1} u_i^2\right) ^{1/2}} $$

has the specified distribution. \(\square \)

Figure 3 shows the empirical PDF and CDF of the inner products of \(10^4\) randomly distributed distinct pairs of points on \(\mathbb {S}^{2}\) and \(\mathbb {S}^{3}\). Clearly the empirical distributions are consistent with Theorem 4.1. But given that randomly distributed points on the sphere do not give QMC design sequences for any \(s>d/2\) (see Theorem 7 of [9]), it follows that for a cubature sequence to be a QMC design sequence it is not enough that the distribution of inner products of distinct points is similar to that in Proposition 4.1 or the figure.

We now turn to the dependence of \(A_{N,\ell }\) as a function of N and \(\ell \). From Corollary 3.1 a necessary condition for a sequence of point sets to be a QMC design of strength at least s is that

$$\begin{aligned} A_{N,\ell }\le c_\ell \; N^{-2s/d}, \quad \ell \ge 1 \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.1)

for some positive sequence \((c_\ell )\). That this is a nontrivial requirement can be seen from the fact that the diagonal terms of the double sum on their own sum to 1/N if \(w_{N,j} = 1/N, j = 1, \ldots , N\), which converges to zero too slowly for QMC design status.

Example 4.1

If \(d = 2\) and \(s = 3\) then the necessary condition is

$$ A_{N,\ell }\le c_\ell N^{-3}. $$

The following result, which closely follows the lines of [9, 17], shows that randomly distributed points with equal weights do not satisfy (4.1) for any \(s > d/2\).

Theorem 4.1

Let \(\ell \ge 1\). For fixed weights \({\varvec{w}}_N\) the expected value of \(A_{N,\ell }\) for N random points \({\varvec{x}}_{N,j}, j = 1,\ldots ,N\) that are chosen independently and identically distributed with respect to the uniform measure on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) is

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}\left[ A_{N,\ell }\right] = \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j}^2. \end{aligned}$$

                    (4.2)

In particular, for equal weights \(w_{N,j} = \frac{1}{N}, j = 1,\ldots ,N\), the expected value is \(\mathbb {E}[A_{N,\ell }] = \frac{1}{N}\).

A plot of \(A_{N,\ell }\) for sets of N randomly distributed points is shown in Figure 4.

Proof

Following the proof of [9, Theorem 20], for fixed \({\varvec{w}}_N\) and \(\ell \ge 1\), the expected value of \(A_{N,\ell }\) is, by separating out the diagonal terms in the definition (1.3) of \(A_{N,\ell }\), using the addition theorem (2.2) and the fact that the integrals over \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) of any spherical harmonic \(Y_{\ell ,m}\) of degree \(\ell \ge 1\) is zero,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb {E}[A_{N,\ell }]&:= \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \cdots \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} A_{N,\ell }(X_N, {\varvec{w}}_N) \; {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,1}) \cdots {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,N}) \\&= \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \cdots \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \left[ P_{\ell }^{(d)}(1)\sum _{j=1}^N {\varvec{w}}_{N,j}^2 + \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{{\mathop {k\ne j}\limits ^{k=1}}}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) \right] {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,1}) \cdots {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,N}) \\&= \sum _{j=1}^N {\varvec{w}}_{N,j}^2 + \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{{\mathop {k\ne j}\limits ^{k=1}}}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} P_{\ell }^{(d)} ({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) \; {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,j})\, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) \\&= \sum _{j=1}^N {\varvec{w}}_{N,j}^2 + \frac{1}{Z(d, \ell )} \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{{\mathop {k\ne j}\limits ^{k=1}}}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} \sum _{m=1}^{Z(d, \ell )} \left( \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} Y_{\ell ,m}({\varvec{x}}){\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) \right) ^2 \\&= \sum _{j=1}^N {\varvec{w}}_{N,j}^2 \end{aligned}$$

The equal weight case \(w_{N,j} = \frac{1}{N}, j = 1,\ldots ,N\) is then immediate. \(\square \)

5 Numerical Experiments

5.1 Using Theorem 3.2 to Estimate Strengths (\(d = 2\) and \(d = 3)\)

The aim here is to explore the use of Theorem 3.2 to estimate strengths \(s^*\) of QMC design sequences. This will be valuable especially in cases where the strength is suspected (as for the \(d = 3\) example below) to be such that \(2s^* - d\) is close to an even integer, so making the closed form expression for the worst-case error unavailable or unreliable.

The plan is to evaluate the finite sum \(\mathcal {R}_{s,d}(X_N, {\varvec{w}}_N)\) defined in (3.3) and (3.4) for a particular sequence of cubature rules and a variety of values of s, then to check if these values achieve the order of convergence \(\frac{2 s}{d}\) required by Theorem 3.2. The desired rate should be achieved if \(s < s^*\), but not if \(s > s^*\). We first give a \(d=2\) case as a check, and then consider a fresh \(d = 3\) case. In these experiments we used the explicit expression \(a_\ell ^{(s)} = (1 + \lambda _\ell )^{-s}\), with \(\lambda _\ell = \ell (\ell +d-1)\), see Section 2.3.

5.2 Cubature Errors for Zonal Integrands

This section considers zonal functions on the sphere, and the approximation of their integrals both using a single cubature sum and a double cubature sum. Experiments can be done with any function on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\) which lies in the Sobolev space \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\) with known smoothness s and known integral.

Consider a zonal kernel

$$ G({\varvec{x}}, {\varvec{y}}) = g({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}), \qquad {\varvec{x}}, {\varvec{y}}\in \mathbb {S}^{d}$$

and suppose that

$$ g({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) = \sum _{\ell =0}^\infty b_\ell \, a_\ell ^{(s)}\, Z(d, \ell )\, P_{\ell }^{(d)}({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) , $$

where \(a_\ell ^{(s)}\) satisfies (2.12) and \(\sum _{\ell =0}^\infty b_\ell Z(d, \ell ) <\infty \). For arbitrary \({\varvec{y}}\in \mathbb {S}^{d}\), with \(f_{{\varvec{y}}}({\varvec{x}}):= g({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}})\), the integral

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {I}\left( f_{\varvec{y}} \right) = \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} f_{{\varvec{y}}}({\varvec{x}}) \, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) = a_0^{(s)} b_0 \end{aligned}$$

                    (5.1)

is independent of \({\varvec{y}}\), while the double integral of G has the same value,

$$\begin{aligned} \mathcal {I}\left( \mathcal {I}\left( G \right) \right) := \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} \int _{\mathbb {S}^{d}} G({\varvec{x}},{\varvec{y}}) \, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{x}}) \, {\,\textrm{d}}\sigma _d({\varvec{y}}) = a_0^{(s)} b_0 . \end{aligned}$$

                    (5.2)

Using the addition Theorem (2.2)

$$\begin{aligned} g({\varvec{x}}\cdot {\varvec{y}}) = \sum _{\ell =0}^\infty b_\ell \, a_\ell ^{(s)}\, \sum _{m=1}^{Z(d,\ell )} Y_{\ell , m}({\varvec{x}}) Y_{\ell , m}({\varvec{y}}) . \end{aligned}$$

                    (5.3)

Now consider estimating the integral (5.1) using the double cubature sum,

$$ \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} \; g({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}). $$

Because \(\sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} = 1\) the cubature error is

$$\begin{aligned} \sum _{j=1}^N \sum _{k=1}^N w_{N,j} w_{N,k} \; g({\varvec{x}}_{N,j} \cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,k}) - b_0 a_0^{(s)} = \sum _{\ell =1}^\infty b_\ell \sum _{m=1}^{Z(d,\ell )} \left( \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} \sqrt{a_\ell ^{(s)}} Y_{\ell , m}(x_{N,j}) \right) ^2 . \end{aligned}$$

                    (5.4)

Recall that \(\mathcal {I}\left( Y_{\ell , m} \right) = 0\) for \(\ell \ge 1\) and \(\left\| \sqrt{a_\ell ^{(s)}} Y_{\ell , m} \right\| _{\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})} = 1\) from (2.13). If the cubature rule \(\mathcal {Q}(X_{N}, {\varvec{w}}_{N})\) is a QMC design for \(\mathbb {H}^{s}(\mathbb {S}^{d})\), then

$$ \left| \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} \sqrt{a_\ell ^{(s)}} Y_{\ell , m}(x_{N,j})\right| \le c_{s,d} \, N^{-\frac{s}{d}}. $$

Finally, as \(\sum _{\ell =0}^\infty b_\ell Z(d,\ell ) < \infty \), the double sum cubature error (5.4) has the doubled rate \(\mathcal {O}(N^{-\frac{2s}{d}})\). This gives us two possible approaches to the numerical integration of zonal polynomials: either to approximate the single integral by a direct cubature rule with N points, or to approximate the double integral using \(N^2\) points but achieving a doubled rate of convergence.

Although the integral \(\mathcal {I}\left( f_{\varvec{y}} \right) \) is independent of \({\varvec{y}}\), the cubature error

$$ \sum _{j=1}^N w_{N,j} f_{\varvec{y}}({\varvec{y}}\cdot {\varvec{x}}_{N,j}) - \mathcal {I}\left( f_{\varvec{y}} \right) $$

can vary depending on \({\varvec{y}}\), especially for modest N. Instead of estimating the cubature error for a particular zonal test function and comparing with the QMC design rate of \(N^{-\frac{s}{d}}\), as in Figure 1, it proved more reliable to estimate the double sum cubature error using (5.4) and compare the observed convergence rate with \(N^{-\frac{2s}{d}}\). If this rate is obtained it suggests the strength of the cubature scheme is at least s. On the other hand if the strength \(s^*\) of the cubature scheme is less than s then this will limit the observed order of convergence.

6 Conclusion

QMC design sequences achieve a specified rate of convergence for the worst case error of numerical integration of functions in a Sobolev space on \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\), \(d \ge 2\). This paper focuses on tools to help analyze potential QMC design sequences, especially when \(d > 2\).

Proving the strength of a QMC design sequence remains elusive, even for \(d = 2\). New numerical experiments with equal area partition points with equal weight on \(\mathbb {S}^{3}\) are discussed. Also the behavior of the strength as the dimension d increases is not known.

Fast generation of good point sets and weights for cubature over \(\mathbb {S}^{d}\), \(d > 2\) and large N that achieve a fast rate of convergence remains a challenge.
