# Cycle 2: sharp bounds from marginal daughter fates

Linked first-cycle report: [FINAL_REPORT.md](FINAL_REPORT.md). This note extends its two-daughter witness and retains its status boundary: exact mathematics for stated branching models; no biological validation or novelty claim.

## 1. Family unsafe-fate probability from one-daughter marginals

For one mother division, let `U_i` indicate that daughter slot `i` is unsafe, for `i=1,...,b`. Assume the vector is exchangeable and each daughter has marginal `P(U_i=1)=epsilon`. Put `K=sum_i U_i`. Then `E[K]=b epsilon`. If `r=P(K>0)`, then

`E[K]/b <= r <= min(1,E[K])`,

so the sharp interval is

`epsilon <= P(any unsafe daughter) <= min(1,b epsilon)`.

Equivalently,

`max(0,1-b epsilon) <= P(no unsafe daughter) <= 1-epsilon`.

Both endpoints are attainable by exchangeable laws. For the lower unsafe probability, take `K=b` with probability `epsilon` and `K=0` otherwise. For the upper endpoint when `b epsilon<=1`, take `K=1` with probability `b epsilon` and `K=0` otherwise. When `b epsilon>=1`, choose `K` on the two adjacent integers around `b epsilon`; then `K>=1` always. Conditional on `K=k`, choose the `k` unsafe daughters uniformly among the `b` slots. This makes each marginal exactly `epsilon` and the daughter vector exchangeable.

Thus the sibling dependence can change one-division risk by as much as the factor `min(1,b epsilon)/epsilon` when `epsilon>0`. For small `epsilon<=1/b`, that factor is `b`. This family bound needs only the marginals and the cap `b`; it is not a new result.

## 2. Sharp full-tree finite-generation unsafe-fate bounds

Assume a single safe progenitor starts generation 0; every safe progenitor divides once per generation into exactly `b` daughter slots; unsafe daughters do not need to be modeled further for a first-hit safety event. Let the unsafe marginal for every daughter, conditional on the **complete prior all-safe history** and the current safe parent state, equal `epsilon`. No independence between sisters or between different mother divisions is assumed. Exchangeability is required among the `b` daughters of each mother. By generation `T`, the unique all-safe path contains

`H_T = 1+b+...+b^(T-1) = (b^T-1)/(b-1)`

mother divisions. Order these divisions in any parent-before-child order. Conditional on all earlier divisions having produced no unsafe daughter, each next division has probability of no unsafe daughter between `a=max(0,1-b epsilon)` and `c=1-epsilon`. The chain rule therefore gives the sharp bounds

`a^H_T <= P(no unsafe fate through generation T) <= c^H_T`,

or, for the risk of at least one unsafe fate,

`1-(1-epsilon)^H_T <= P(any unsafe through T) <= 1-max(0,1-b epsilon)^H_T`.

Sharpness follows by applying the single-family extremizers at each all-safe-history division: common-fate sisters attain the upper no-unsafe bound; the one-unsafe-slot law (when `b epsilon<=1`) attains the lower no-unsafe bound. If `epsilon>1/b`, a division can be forced to contain an unsafe daughter with probability one, so the lower bound on no-unsafe probability is zero.

For a **mere upper cap** of `H` divisions, when a safe trajectory can stop before using all `H` divisions, the same assumptions give the sharp universal risk upper bound `1-max(0,1-b epsilon)^H`, but no positive risk lower bound without a minimum number of attempted divisions: a controller can stop safely before dividing. If each safe trajectory is guaranteed to attempt at least `h` divisions, risk is at least `1-(1-epsilon)^h`. If per-daughter marginals are only pooled/unconditional across realized daughters, the product bounds do not follow; selection into later generations can change the conditional hazard. Then use the robust union bound `P(any unsafe) <= min(1,H b epsilon)` for at most `H` parent divisions, and retain `UNKNOWN` for a lower bound beyond a guaranteed first division.

The product bounds assume a stylized branching tree in which every safe progenitor divides at each remaining generation. A carrying capacity, safe quiescence/death, time-varying rates, multiple safe states, or interaction between lineages changes the number and type of at-risk divisions. In a controlled model with generation-varying conditional margins `epsilon_g`, the full-tree formulas sharpen to

`P(no unsafe) in [product_g max(0,1-b epsilon_g)^(b^g), product_g (1-epsilon_g)^(b^g)]`,

for `g=0,...,T-1`, under the same exact full-tree/conditional-history interface.

## 3. Sharp clone-level regenerative survival bounds

Now let `K` be the number of regenerative-state daughters from one regenerative parent, where `0<=K<=b`, sisters are exchangeable, and each daughter is regenerative with marginal `p`. Thus `E[K]=mu=bp`. Assume offspring vectors from distinct parent cells are independent conditional on their parent state (the Galton-Watson branching property); within a family, daughter fates can have arbitrary exchangeable dependence. Let `G(z)=E[z^K]` be the offspring PGF. Starting from one progenitor, let `q_T` be the probability of no regenerative cell in generation `T`. Standard branching recursion gives

`q_0=0`, `q_(t+1)=G(q_t)`, and clone survival `s_T=1-q_T`.

For each `z` in `[0,1]`, the sequence `k -> z^k` is convex on integer `k`. Among all laws on `{0,...,b}` with mean `mu`, its expectation is minimized by the nearest-integer law and maximized by the endpoint law. Write `a=floor(mu)`. The two extremal PGFs are

`G_min(z)=(a+1-mu) z^a + (mu-a) z^(a+1)`

and

`G_max(z)=1-mu/b + (mu/b) z^b = 1-p+p z^b`.

For integer `mu`, the nearest-integer law is the point mass at `mu`. Every such count law is compatible with exchangeability by selecting a uniformly random subset of `K` daughters to be regenerative. Since PGFs are nondecreasing on `[0,1]`, recursion and induction yield the **sharp** finite-generation interval

`1-(G_max)^(T)(0) <= s_T <= 1-(G_min)^(T)(0)`.

The low-survival endpoint is common-fate all-or-none offspring, matching the first-cycle model. The high-survival endpoint keeps offspring count as concentrated as the integer constraint permits. If `mu>=1`, the nearest-integer law has `K>=1` always and the upper survival bound is 1. If `mu<1`, this law has `K=1` with probability `mu` and 0 otherwise, so its upper survival bound is `mu^T`.

For the cycle-one witness with `b=2,p=3/5`, `G_max(z)=2/5+(3/5)z^2`, which gives the lower-survival recursion `q_(t+1)=2/5+(3/5)q_t^2`; its limiting survival is `1/3`. The independent-sister model has `G(z)=(2/5+(3/5)z)^2` and limiting survival `5/9`. But the sharp finite-generation upper survival is 1: the admissible exchangeable law `K=1` with probability `4/5`, `K=2` with probability `1/5` never loses its regenerative cell. Hence the two cycle-one examples do not span the full uncertainty left by a marginal transition graph.

The Galton-Watson recursion does **not** follow if different mothers' offspring vectors share an unmodeled clone-level random environment or tissue-wide feedback. In that case the PGF per mother is insufficient; one must model/observe the joint across-mother law or use a robust finite-count bound. Density dependence and finite niches can also invalidate the unbounded-tree prediction after clone sizes cease to be small.

## 4. Minimal family-level statistic

For a single division with unsafe count `K_U`, the extra scalar needed beyond the daughter marginal to identify the chance of any unsafe daughter is `P(K_U=0)` (equivalently `P(K_U>0)`). Pairwise covariance is not the primitive statistic for `b>2`.

For a binary family (`b=2`), the daughter marginal `p` and one pair statistic `c=Cov(I_1,I_2)` identify the full count law:

`P(K=2)=p^2+c`,

`P(K=1)=2p(1-p)-2c`,

`P(K=0)=(1-p)^2+c`.

Thus for `b=2`, covariance plus marginal is sufficient for all finite-generation scalar branching survival predictions. For `b>=3`, it is not. Example at `b=3,p=1/2`:

- Independent daughters: `P(K=0,1,2,3)=(1,3,3,1)/8`.
- A different exchangeable law: `P(K=0,1,2,3)=(1,9,3,3)/16`.

Both have `E[K]=3/2` and `E[K(K-1)]=3/2`, so every daughter pair has covariance zero and is pairwise independent. Yet `P(K=0)` is `1/8` versus `1/16`, giving one-generation clone persistence `7/8` versus `15/16` and different PGFs. The second law is exchangeable by choosing a uniformly random subset of size `K` after drawing `K`.

For a prespecified one-step safety or survival event, measure the corresponding zero-count probability. For multi-generation prediction under the branching property, the reusable sufficient family-level object is the full offspring-count law/PGF; higher moments or a pair-correlation summary can leave distinct PGFs and different extinction predictions. For one fixed `T`, the recursion uses only the `T` evaluations `G(q_t)`, but these depend on the unknown law and do not provide a stable model under changed controls or starting states.

## 5. Literature coverage and remaining control implication

The central candidate is covered by established branching-process lineage models. Klein et al. (2020), “Lineage reconstruction from clonal correlations,” explicitly defines each parent type's **joint** daughter-state distribution `D_i=P(X_j, j child of i | one parent of type i)`, allowing symmetric, asymmetric, and stochastic fates. Its branching premise makes the offspring vector from each parent iid, and the paper explicitly discusses limitations from cell-cell interactions, self-renewal, and cell death. The scalar one-type PGF recursion and the convex-order bounds above are standard branching theory; no new theorem or priority claim is made here.

A current control platform may help test the missing family information. An et al. (2026), “Synthetic circuits for cell ratio control,” reports recombinase-based fate branching with output ratios from 0.1% to 99.9% in bacteria, yeast, and HEK293FT cells; the paper also demonstrates series/parallel programs from a founder. It supports feasibility of programmable fate ratios, but does not show an intervention pair that independently changes sister dependence while holding the marginal ratio fixed, nor does it report the PGF-based safety guarantees here.

**Remaining proposed control test:** in a tractable yeast or mammalian-cell synthetic circuit, compare two induction schedules/topologies titrated to the same daughter fate marginal and mean population ratio: an early irreversible founder decision inherited by descendants, and a delayed daughter-level decision that can produce mixed sisters. Barcode founders and image divisions; estimate `P(K=0,1,...,b)` and clone-size/survival distributions. Then evaluate whether a controller trained only on the matched one-cell matrix predicts both clonal distributions. The predicted control implication is that timing of fate resolution (and measured offspring law) can matter even when the population ratio is matched. This is a proposed stress test, not a demonstrated capability or a regeneration intervention. For tissue relevance, validate a successful model later in the cerebral-organoid depletion/rescue assay from cycle one; current evidence does not identify a clinically usable actuator.
