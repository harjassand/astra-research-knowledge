N14 — PHASE II NATURAL-SCIENCE INVESTIGATION
Protein allostery through local kinetic control, path response, and hidden states
Frozen record: v1
Date: 2026-10-09 (Australia/Brisbane)

STATUS AND SCOPE

The mathematical identity below is an internally derived specialization of standard Markov-process response theory, not a priority claim. The finite-state computation is finite-tested and synthetic. No protein-specific prediction has been empirically tested here. Prior art already establishes equilibrium allostery, dynamics-mediated allostery, kinetic response theory, hidden conformational states, and reproducibility methods for network pathways. The candidate contribution is the combined operational criterion: measure causal finite-time remote output odds after a local intervention, and, when a complete kinetic model is available, decompose that effect into local transition traffic weighted by future functional committor differences. This criterion makes explicit what remains measurable when metastable states are hidden and what cannot be inferred from finite observation.

The task is: find when a localized molecular perturbation can cause reliable long-range switching, including ligand-binding response, without relying on equilibrium covariance as a proxy for kinetics. The operational response is explicitly conditioned on the initial ensemble, ligand protocol, readout, and time horizon. A barrier-only intervention may have a large finite-time effect and exactly zero equilibrium effect; therefore a claim of "allostery" must state which of these meanings is intended.

MATHEMATICAL CHARACTERIZATION

Let a finite state CTMC X_t describe the complete microscopic state, including ligand occupancy and any metastable conformations. Use row-generator convention Q_theta,c, so p_theta,c(T)=mu exp(T Q_theta,c) f for a readout f:X→[0,1]. Here f may indicate a remote active conformation, a ligand-bound state, an open channel, or a productive reaction by time T. The initial distribution mu is held fixed when differentiating the post-intervention parameters; this is implemented experimentally by preparing one common ensemble and randomizing a fast intervention at time zero. Assume finite state space, nonexplosive rates, differentiable rates, and finite T. Irreducibility is useful for stationary comparisons but is not needed for the finite-time identity.

Write rho_s=mu exp(sQ_theta,c) and u_{T-s}=exp((T-s)Q_theta,c)f, where u_tau(x) is the probability of the remote readout at horizon tau when starting from full state x. Matrix differentiation gives the Duhamel formula

  d p_theta,c(T)/d theta
    = integral_0^T rho_s Q'_theta,c u_{T-s} ds
    = integral_0^T sum_x rho_s(x) sum_{z != x}
          q'_theta,c(x,z) [u_{T-s}(z)-u_{T-s}(x)] ds.                 (1)

The second equality uses the row-sum-zero diagonal Q'_{xx}=-sum_{z!=x}q'_{xz}. If theta changes only rates on a local molecular region/edge set L, every summand outside L vanishes. Equation (1) is the exact local mechanism decomposition: the molecule must occupy a state from which the intervention changes an outgoing rate, and the rate change matters only to the extent that the destination has a different probability of producing the remote readout by the remaining time. A frequent local transition can be functionally irrelevant when its future committor gap is zero; a low-occupancy edge can matter if its committor gap is large. Opposing route contributions can cancel or change sign with T. A single shortest path or equilibrium correlation coefficient does not encode these quantities.

For a finite intervention theta_0→theta_1, integrating (1) yields the exact nonlinear response

  p_theta1,c(T)-p_theta0,c(T)
    = integral_theta0^theta1 d eta integral_0^T ds
        sum_x rho_s^eta(x) sum_{z!=x} q'_eta,c(x,z)
          [u^eta_{T-s}(z)-u^eta_{T-s}(x)].                         (2)

This avoids treating a large perturbation as linear. For p strictly between 0 and 1, the corresponding log-odds susceptibility is (d p/d theta)/(p(1-p)). A finite operational effect is

  R_a(T,c;delta) = logit P(F_T=1 | do(theta_a+delta), c, mu)
                    - logit P(F_T=1 | do(theta_a), c, mu).           (3)

The causal estimand (3) is directly measurable by randomized intervention and readout even if the conformational state is hidden. A proposed robust screen for a desired sign s∈{−1,+1} is to require a simultaneous lower confidence bound on s R_a(T,c;delta) above a prechosen margin rho for all (T,c) in a prespecified operating window W. A design ranking could maximize the worst-window effect per matched perturbation cost. The time/concentration window, desired output, perturbation dose, and rho are scientific choices; they cannot be selected after looking at the observed trajectories. This criterion identifies an operationally controlling site under tested conditions, not a unique residue-by-residue communication route.

The path likelihood makes the link to transitions explicit. For a path omega with jumps x_{j-1}→x_j at times t_j and escape rate lambda(x)=sum_{z!=x}q(x,z),

  log P_theta(omega) = log mu_theta(x_0)
      + sum_j log q_theta(x_{j-1},x_j)
      - integral_0^T lambda_theta(X_s) ds.

Let S_theta=partial_theta log P_theta(omega) be the path score. When the initial law is independent of theta,

  partial_theta logit P_theta(F_T=1)
      = E[S_theta | F_T=1] - E[S_theta | F_T=0].                    (4)

Equation (4) follows from partial_theta E[1_F]=E[1_F S_theta], then differentiating log(p/(1-p)). It is a conditional difference in local path evidence between successful and unsuccessful trajectories, not a state covariance. If theta multiplies all rates in a local edge family L by exp(theta),

  S_theta = N_L(T) - integral_0^T lambda_L(X_s) ds,                (5)

where N_L counts jumps on those directed transitions and lambda_L is their total escape hazard. The compensator term is essential: counting crossings alone biases toward states with long observation times or high attempt rates.

For ligand activity, let ell=ln(c/c0), where c is a post-pulse ligand concentration and association rates obey mass action q_on ∝ c. With the pre-pulse initial law fixed independently of post-pulse c,

  S_ell = N_on(T) - integral_0^T lambda_on(X_s) ds.                (6)

For a general two-parameter path family, the mixed derivative is

  partial_ell partial_theta logit P(F_T=1)
    = [ E[H_theta,ell | F_T=1] + Cov(S_theta,S_ell | F_T=1) ]
      -[ E[H_theta,ell | F_T=0] + Cov(S_theta,S_ell | F_T=0) ],     (7)

where H_theta,ell=partial_ell partial_theta log P(omega) is the mixed derivative of the FULL path log density. It includes the initial-law mixed derivative, the mixed jump log-rate terms, and the negative mixed escape-rate integral. It is not enough that partial_theta partial_ell log q=0: for q∝exp(theta+ell), log q has zero mixed derivative but q itself has nonzero mixed derivative and therefore the waiting-time compensator contributes. The covariance-only reduction of (7) is valid only when H_theta,ell=0 pathwise. Disjoint rate supports with fixed initial law, as in the synthetic model below, are a sufficient condition. This caveat was added after adversarial mathematical review.

At equilibrium, if theta changes only transition barriers/conductances and leaves energies fixed, the stationary distribution is unchanged, so every equilibrium state expectation and covariance remains unchanged. More generally, for a reversible chain with target equilibrium pi, set symmetric edge conductances C_xy=pi(x)q(x,y)=pi(y)q(y,x). Any positive symmetric C gives a reversible generator with the same pi. Rescaling a local conductance changes kinetic pathways and relaxation times without changing the free-energy distribution. This proves the structural gap: equilibrium covariance information alone cannot determine a finite-time response. In a distinct case where theta changes state energy U_theta=U_0+theta V, ordinary equilibrium linear response is exact: partial_theta <f>_pi=-beta Cov_pi(f,V). The pathwise formula supplements, rather than invalidates, this thermodynamic result.

The same energy/mobility split has a continuous reversible analogue. With density pi(x) proportional to exp[-beta U(x)] and reflecting/no-flux boundaries, the overdamped generator

  L_D f = pi^{-1} div(D(x) pi(x) grad f)

has invariant pi for every positive diffusivity/mobility field D, because integral pi L_D f dx is a boundary flux. Changing D only in a local region preserves equilibrium but changes transition timing and path probabilities. This is a mathematical mechanism for barrier/friction control; it does not assert that an arbitrary protein mutation changes only D while leaving U fixed.

SYNTHETIC REVERSIBLE MODEL AND NUMERICAL PREDICTIONS

Use conformations i∈{0,1,2}, ligand occupancy y∈{0,1}, and dimensionless concentration c. The full state is (i,y). Set energies in kBT units

  U(i,y;c)=epsilon_i-y(a_i+ln c),
  epsilon=(0,0,2),  a=(0,0,ln 30).

The bound ligand has stronger affinity in conformation 2. Binding rates are q[(i,0),(i,1)]=k c and q[(i,1),(i,0)]=k exp(-a_i), k=0.30. For adjacent conformations i and i+1, define Delta epsilon_i=epsilon_{i+1}-epsilon_i and Delta a_i=a_{i+1}-a_i, with base attempt rates gamma=(1,0.08):

  q[(i,y),(i+1,y)] = gamma_i exp(-Delta epsilon_i/2 + y Delta a_i/2),
  q[(i+1,y),(i,y)] = gamma_i exp(+Delta epsilon_i/2 - y Delta a_i/2).

These satisfy q(x,z)/q(z,x)=exp[-(U(z)-U(x))]. The invariant measure is

  pi_c(i,y)=Z_c^{-1} exp[-epsilon_i+y(a_i+ln c)].               (8)

The local barrier intervention multiplies both directions of the 1↔2 conformational edge by m=exp(theta), for both y values. Thus the change is local in transition space; it changes no energy and leaves (8) invariant at every c. The hidden state 1 is an intermediate between a low-activity basin and the ligand-favored active basin 2. Start in the identical equilibrium ensemble pi at c_pre=0.03. At time zero, step c to c_post and compare sham m=1 with local barrier perturbation m=3 or 10. Readouts are f_active=1{i=2} and f_bound=y.

The script uses uniformization of the six-state generator and checks stationarity and propagation residuals in executable assertions. On a ligand step 0.03→3, the m=10 prediction is:

  T       active, sham     active, m=10     bound, sham     bound, m=10
  4          0.2440           0.5307          0.7940           0.8563
  10         0.4071           0.6038          0.8427           0.8937
  15         0.4854           0.6061          0.8632           0.8948
  100        0.606187         0.606212        0.894885         0.894891

At T=10, the finite log-odds effects are 0.7975 for activity (odds ratio 2.22) and 0.4512 for ligand occupancy (odds ratio 1.57). The active-state probability crosses a 0.5 threshold in the perturbed arm while the sham remains below 0.5 until roughly T=17. A 3-fold barrier acceleration also crosses 0.5 at T=10: sham p=0.4071, perturbed p=0.5624, bound fractions 0.8427 and 0.8813. The difference decays with time. At the common equilibrium for c=3, active fraction is 0.60621221446 and bound fraction is 0.89489138093 independent of m. Thus finite-time ligand-triggered function and binding kinetics switch while the equilibrium response of the barrier-only perturbation is exactly zero. This phenomenon depends on the non-equilibrium concentration pulse: if the system starts at pi(c_post) and the barrier perturbation alone is applied, it stays at pi(c_post) and no population response occurs.

The model is a mathematical counterexample to covariance-only kinetic inference, not a model fitted to a named protein. It establishes that a hidden intermediate plus a local transition-state change can alter the timing and probability of a remote active/bound readout. It does not prove that a particular real allosteric site acts by barrier control. The model's Markov states, energies, barriers, concentrations, and rate units are supplied assumptions, not acquired molecular data.

HIDDEN-STATE BOUNDARY AND FAILURE MODES

1. The measured intervention effect (3) is invariant to how unobserved conformations are represented, because it is a contrast in observable distributions. The internal decomposition (1), committor u, and path score (5) require the full state/rate model or a validated estimate. A coarse observable process can be non-Markov and can hide path-dependent waiting times. Splitting a hidden state into observationally identical clones can change latent path attribution without changing any measured output. Therefore, direct assay can establish a controlled functional effect at measured times; it cannot identify a unique microscopic route from output data alone.

2. No finite observation horizon certifies long-run behavior when hidden traps are unrestricted. Add an unobserved state H to a functional state A with rates q(A,H)=epsilon and q(H,A)=epsilon exp(-M). Detailed balance gives pi(H)/pi(A)=exp(M), so pi(H) tends to one for large M. Starting outside H, P(hit H by time T)≤1-exp(-epsilon T)≤epsilon T. For any finite T and detection tolerance, epsilon can be chosen so H is virtually never observed while M makes H dominate the eventual equilibrium. The same construction can hide an arbitrarily slow response mode. Thus estimates are robust only over sampled horizons and under an explicit bound on unseen timescales or a demonstrated equilibration protocol.

3. A local edge may have no causal effect if its forward and reverse committor-weighted contributions cancel; high covariance or graph centrality alone cannot prevent cancellation. With parallel pathways, the sign can vary with T and c. A site should be reported with its operating window rather than assigned a timeless binary label.

4. The synthetic barrier intervention preserves equilibrium. Real mutations, tethered ligands, and photoswitches can change energies, rates, solvent, or protein integrity simultaneously. The direct causal contrast then remains a valid empirical outcome if randomization and controls are sound, but the barrier-only explanation does not follow. The model also assumes a finite CTMC. For continuous Langevin dynamics or non-Markov memory, the exact endpoint causal contrast remains definable; applying the finite-state generator decomposition requires a justified approximation or the corresponding path-space likelihood ratio.

5. Comparing different candidate residues without matching perturbation strength is not a fair site-ranking experiment. A mutation changes several physical parameters and can destabilize the fold; site-specific labelling/tethering may have off-target or global-unfolding effects. Include unlabeled/matched controls, structural integrity checks, a nonallosteric site control, concentration- and time-matched sham arms, and multiple independent constructs. A negative screen does not rule out a site if the probe fails to bind or perturb it sufficiently.

EXPERIMENTAL TEST AND COST ACCOUNTING

For an experimentally testable version, identify a protein where a localized intervention can be synchronized and distal function and ligand occupancy can both be measured. Prepare one common pre-pulse ensemble at c_pre; randomize local intervention versus sham at t=0 while applying the same ligand concentration step; record distal binding and function at a preregistered set of lag times spanning the expected relaxation. Photoisomerization is a plausible synchronization device, as demonstrated in the PDZ studies, but a new construct must verify that its local perturbation is sufficiently localized and characterize whether it changes barriers, state energies, or both. For enzymes, use an output such as productive-turnover occurrence/count over a fixed interval; for ion channels, use opening/current; for binding, use a fluorescence or spectroscopic occupancy reporter. Do not infer ligand response solely from the activity reporter.

Estimate (3) directly from randomized arms at each (T,c). If outcomes are binary and independent, a first-order planning approximation is

  Var(logit(p_hat_1)-logit(p_hat_0))
      ≈ (1/n)[1/(p_0(1-p_0))+1/(p_1(1-p_1))],

for n independent samples per arm. At the synthetic 10-fold, c=3, T=10 point, this gives about 50 samples per arm for a nominal two-sided 95% Wald interval excluding zero for activity, or about 103 per arm for 80% power at alpha=.05; ligand occupancy needs about 341 and 696 per arm, respectively, because it is near saturation and its log-odds effect is smaller. These are idealized counts under the synthetic model, with no multiple-time/concentration correction, measurement error, molecule-to-molecule clustering, construct variability, or effect-estimation uncertainty. They are not empirical sample-size recommendations. Use independent biological/trajectory units, cluster bootstrap or hierarchical models for repeated times, a simultaneous confidence procedure for the worst-window criterion, and concentrations near the unsaturated response region when feasible.

The assay cost scales at least with number of independently prepared samples × treatment arms × concentrations × independent protein constructs, plus time-resolved instrument time. If the same molecule is followed at several times, those readouts are correlated and do not count as independent replicates. No named target, intervention yield, instrument schedule, or monetary cost was supplied, so no real-world cost number is claimed. Simulation cost for the six-state demonstration is negligible and is not a proxy for atomistic MD or experimental acquisition. An atomistic prediction would need pinned force field/ligand conditions, adequate sampling of metastable states, transition-rate uncertainty, and held-out kinetic validation; a simulation replay alone would remain model evidence.
