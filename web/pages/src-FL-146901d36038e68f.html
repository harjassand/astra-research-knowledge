# FL molecular recovery: same-regime audit

**Finding: `NOT_ACHIEVED`.** Molecular excitation can in principle be returned to a phase-controlled optical mode by reversing stimulated Raman transitions. The primary literature contains coherent molecular emission and high-finesse rotational Raman sidebands, but no experiment I found measures reusable net work recovery from rotational or vibrational excitation in the same physical regime that produces useful chemistry. The strongest recovery-adjacent demonstration is coherent, externally triggered two-photon emission from vibrationally coherent para-hydrogen. Its partner channel is only about $10^{-4}$ of the trigger energy, the optical preparation and trigger work were not closed in a full balance, and no chemical reaction was involved. The proposed high-J CO₂ route remains rejected even under its ideal recovery limit.

The result is scoped to experiments and calculations reviewed here. It is not a no-go theorem for molecular work extraction. All calculations are in `derive.py` and `CALCULATIONS.json`; literature statements below are kept separate from those calculations.

## Candidate mechanism and work definition

For a molecule with Hamiltonian H and state ρ, the ideal ergotropy is

\[
\mathcal W(\rho,H)=\operatorname{Tr}(\rho H)-\min_U\operatorname{Tr}(U\rho U^\dagger H).
\]

This is the largest energy removable by a unitary on that molecule, with its eigenvalues fixed. A pure high-$J$ rotor can have nearly all its energy above the lowest allowed state as ergotropy. A passive thermal distribution has zero ergotropy. Neither rotational energy nor a nonzero ergotropy value guarantees that a practical optical interaction can extract it. A measured Raman coherence is not by itself a measurement of ergotropy.

A concrete candidate is a phase-matched sequence of stimulated rotational anti-Stokes Raman transitions, $J\to J-2$, driven with a seeded probe or a cavity mode. If a probe photon at frequency $\omega$ exits at $\omega+\Delta E_J/\hbar$, the useful energy is the *increment* $\hbar\Delta\omega=\Delta E_J$, after subtracting the incoming seed. For high-$J$ CO₂, this would require returning the rotational ladder through many steps. A time-reversed optical-centrifuge waveform is a second possible implementation: drive the polarization chirp backwards, with the molecular phase and the field phases controlled, so the rotor exerts negative torque on the field. In either version, the extracted sideband must be directed into a stored, reusable mode and reduce the replenishment work in a subsequent matched cycle.

The required closed balance is

\[
\Delta E_{\rm rotor}+\Delta E_{\rm vib}+\Delta E_{\rm chem}+\Delta E_{\rm translational}
+\sum_b (E_{b,\rm out}-E_{b,\rm in})+Q_{\rm bath}=0.
\]

All incident and outgoing fields must be counted, including seeds, pump light, sidebands, fluorescence, and cavity loss. A bright blue-shifted photon can be coherent while its energy comes mostly from the applied pumps. A red-shifted Stokes photon ordinarily deposits energy in the molecule. Spontaneous photons and CARS probe signals do not establish a reusable field. A loss of Raman coherence does not by itself show loss of ergotropy; population-resolved molecular energy and bath heating are needed too.

## Quantitative bounds and examples

The CO₂ centrifuge study observed a broad rotational distribution with a high-energy tail at $J=364$, $E_{\rm rot}=6.14\,\mathrm{eV}$ per molecule. It reports 4 or 7 mJ pulse energy at the sample in the experimental conditions reviewed here and a 10 Hz amplifier rate. The article does not report the number of molecules in the high-$J$ tail or the fraction of that pulse energy transferred to them, so both are `UNKNOWN` here. At 380 Torr and an assumed 300 K, the ideal-gas number density is $1.22\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$. The reported hot vibrational bands appear after about 500 ps, or three mean collision times, giving an inferred mean collision interval of about 167 ps. These are timing constraints, not measurements of ergotropy survival: the hot bands show rotational-to-vibrational transfer, while collisions may also dephase without destroying all extractable energy.

The source reports the largest CO₂ rotational states exceed the ground-state bond dissociation energy, but a centrifugal barrier prevents those molecules from dissociating. Its quasi-classical trajectory calculations find rotation-to-vibration transfer is strongest around moderate excitation: up to 25% probability per collision for one bending quantum at $J=100$, with little contribution from $J>200$. Thus the very high energy tail that makes the largest activation packet is not the state range most effective at transferring energy into vibration. At high $J$, rigid-rotor transition energies also fail: the paper reports rigid-rotor Raman-shift errors as large as 61.5 cm⁻¹ (0.29 eV). The script's $J=364\to362$ gap is explicitly only a low-J-constant scale estimate, not a high-J prediction.

The ergotropy code includes a deliberately idealized state-space example. For a rigid rotor with all $m$ states accessible, a uniform mixture over the $2J+1$ magnetic substates at one $J$ has a passive rearrangement energy of only 1.74% of its initial energy at $J=57$, and 0.59% at $J=170$. The corresponding ideal rearrangement ceilings are therefore 98.26% and 99.41%. This is not a measured ensemble ergotropy, an extraction efficiency, or a yield prediction. It assumes all $m$ substates within the same electronic and nuclear-spin block can be unitarily rearranged. The SiO⁺ sample's actual density matrix and an allowed extraction protocol are not measured.

The SiO⁺ experiment quotes the $j\approx170$ internal-rotation scale as roughly $100k_BT$ at 300 K, or 2.59 eV per ion. This substantial stored-energy scale is still not a recovery result: the actual optical input, fraction stored in the rotor, and energy returned to an optical or microwave mode were not measured.

At 380 Torr, a Poisson collision model using the inferred 167 ps interval gives probabilities of no collision of 94.2%, 74.1%, 54.9%, and 36.7% during 10, 50, 100, and 167 ps respectively. This is a timing exposure estimate only. `derive.py` also reports a toy deceleration result that assumes every collision destroys all remaining extractable work while the rotor's angular momentum is lowered linearly. That model is explicitly adverse and unvalidated; its return fractions are not evidence about real CO₂ recovery. No empirical unloading rate, state survival curve, or collision-to-ergotropy-loss hazard was found.

If one 6.14 eV activation packet were required for each CO molecule, its ideal energy floor would be 592.4 kJ/mol or 5.875 kWh/kg CO. This is calculated from the reported high-$J$ tail, not measured process consumption. It already exceeds the ISPT/TNO 2023 modelled SOEC system consumption of 3.3 kWh/kg CO in its Air Liquide use case by a factor of 1.78, before laser efficiency, excitation fraction, unloading, separation, or nonproductive collisions. The SOEC figure is a feasibility model, not a demonstrated plant result. The comparison rejects this one-packet, one-CO route even if energy recovery from every unchanged reactant were perfect.

The reaction-rate experiment on SiO⁺ reports $0.18(3)\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ when pumped to extreme rotation versus $0.05(2)\,\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ without optical pumping. At the pumped rate, the single-ion mean reaction interval is about 333 s. The H₂ density was $7(1)\times10^6\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, equivalent at 300 K to about $2.2\times10^{-10}$ Torr and roughly $1.75\times10^{12}$ times below the CO₂ 380-Torr density. The reaction occurs in a dilute ion trap; continuous optical pumping prepares a narrow distribution, and the experiment measures a rate enhancement, not an energy-recovery cycle. The authors' ion-count proxy, laser drift, and mass resolution also limit direct product identification. Conditional renewal arithmetic in `CALCULATIONS.json` shows why high recovery alone is insufficient at low productive probability; it is not a reconstruction of the ion-trap experiment, which does not provide the one-attempt survivor return and holding-work data the model would need.

## Demonstrations, and what each actually establishes

| Primary experiment | Observed physical output | Missing for molecular work recovery in useful chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| CO₂ optical centrifuge, 380 Torr, 300 K study (2023) | Coherent rotational CARS ladder resolved to $J=364$, collision-dependent hot bands and rotational-to-vibrational transfer. The detected CARS is a probe/readout. | No useful CO production, calibrated molecule count in high-$J$ states, controlled unloading, net optical gain, or replenishment-work reduction. Hot bands appear after about 500 ps. |
| SiO⁺ + H₂, optical pumping in an ion trap (2023) | Extreme rotational excitation up to $j=170$ raises the measured reaction rate by about 3.6×. | No optical energy input/recovery ledger or repeated work-recycling cycle. Ultra-dilute reagent gas, slow single-ion reaction rate, and incoherent pump preparation do not match the dense neutral centrifuge regime. |
| Para-H₂ coherent two-photon emission (2014/2015) | Vibrational coherence plus an external mid-IR trigger produces a directed, coherent partner at 5.048 μm. The 2015 article estimates $6\times10^{11}$ photons/pulse; this gives 23.6 nJ/pulse at that wavelength. The partner is about $1.6\times10^{-4}$ of the 150 μJ trigger energy from the reported photon count; the paper itself reports the ratio as approximately $10^{-4}$. | No chemistry, no total balance across all fields, and no cycle proving that output reduces the work needed for a later operation. The two visible preparation fields are 5 mJ each; first anti-Stokes intensity at 1577 nm is reported about 50× the partner intensity, so partner-channel power is not a complete output ledger. The estimated photon count uses an assumed equal detector responsivity at 5048 and 4587 nm. |
| D₂ CW rotational Raman cavity (2011) | High-finesse cavity generates coherent rotational Stokes/anti-Stokes sidebands; the authors report up to seven sidebands and over 50 mW combined at 10 Torr. | The Stokes ladder pumps molecules into rotation; the paper's 60 μW first anti-Stokes output is 0.34% of the reported 17.5 mW pump output, not a recovery efficiency. The 77 K D₂ cavity is not a high-$J$ CO₂ reaction platform. |
| CO₂/O₂ dense-gas thermalization (2015 PRX; oxygen rather than the CO₂ reaction sample) | A 0.9-bar O₂ sample was centrifuged with 30 mJ pulses; rotation-to-translation relaxation was tied to a thermal channel. The model estimates 2% of molecules reach $J\approx91$, each with 1.5 eV, and a 140 K bulk gas rise. | This is direct evidence that rotational work can become bath heat in a dense gas, not evidence that it cannot first be extracted. It measures refractive, birefringence, density and acoustic signals, not reusable light. |
| Helium-droplet centrifuge deceleration, arXiv v1 (25 Aug 2026) | Molecular alignment follows the rotating drive and returns during the deceleration sweep; the authors interpret the dynamics as rapid molecule-to-superfluid thermalization. Effective model times are about 10 ps for CS₂ and 100 ps for OCS. | The measured signal is fragment-angle alignment, not a captured optical output mode. The paper studies molecule–bath coupling; the observed reversal does not measure returned work. It is a superfluid droplet regime, not the chemistry regimes above. |

The 2015 para-H₂ demonstration is the strongest physically observed example of energy from a prepared molecular coherence appearing in a coherent emitted channel. The estimated partner energy is about 24 nJ/pulse, or 0.24 μW at 10 Hz. The two visible preparation fields together are 10 mJ/pulse, or 100 mW average; the external trigger is 150 μJ/pulse, or 1.5 mW average. These comparisons are not efficiencies because there are other sidebands and the full energy flow through the target was not measured. They do establish a real, controlled coherent output path whose practical limitation is presently severe. The system is p-H₂ at 78 K and 60 kPa, with a calculated ideal-gas density of $5.57\times10^{19}\,\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$: dense gas, but a different molecule, temperature, excited degree of freedom, and no chemical output.

The phase and noise conditions also do not transfer automatically. The CO₂ experiment used phase-locked optical components and a 65 ps CARS probe, but the spectra were averaged over hundreds to thousands of shots; a spectroscopic fringe is not a cycle-level phase-referenced power measurement. In p-H₂, the preparation fields were synchronized to better than 50 ps, the trigger jitter was about 0.8 ns, the coherence estimate was 0.04 against a stated maximum of 0.5, and the Raman HWHM used for decoherence was 65 MHz. The authors measured output dependence on trigger timing and energy, which establishes control of the emission process, but not persistent phase lock to a work-storage load. The SiO⁺ reaction rate was inferred through a Ba⁺ fluorescence proxy and secular excitation; the authors report that their mass resolution cannot separate SiO⁺ from SiOH⁺ and that laser drift contributes noise. The D₂ study's high-finesse cavity required active locking and alignment, while its generated sidebands were not tested as a stored input for a later cycle.

The primary papers therefore occupy incompatible corners. CO₂ supplies high density and an optically prepared high-$J$ distribution but no chemistry or extraction. SiO⁺ supplies a real reaction-rate change but at ultrahigh vacuum with low event rate and continuous incoherent pumping. p-H₂ supplies controlled coherent emission in dense gas but no reaction. D₂ supplies cavity sidebands but Stokes pumping and a different rotational regime. The droplet result is a bath-coupling study. Combining the best density, reaction rate, coherence, and recovery number from these separate systems would not describe any demonstrated apparatus.

## Attempted go/no-go and decisive scope

The physical mechanism is plausible in an isolated, phase-prepared rotor: state-selective stimulated anti-Stokes transitions can transfer molecular rotational energy into an optical sideband, and a cavity can capture that sideband. The optical-centrifuge loading itself is a coherent Raman operation, so its ideal unitary reverse is not forbidden. This is the credible candidate, not a claimed invention or achieved technology.

The same-regime gate is not passed. In dense CO₂ the only demonstrated return-window proxy is rotational coherence and energy-transfer dynamics, not work survival; the collision interval is about 167 ps and vibrational hot bands are visible by 500 ps. The centrifuge test uses tens of mJ-scale laser systems and 10 Hz operation, while no count of activated molecules or calorimetric net field gain was reported. In the only chemistry experiment, rotation was continually prepared in a trapped ion and the observed reaction was rare; no recovery pulse, coherent sideband, recovered work, or holding-power cost was measured. There is no basis for assigning one set of $p,r,h,\eta$ to either experiment.

A decisive next discriminator would stay in one CO₂ or reaction-compatible apparatus and perform paired loading/unloading runs with calibrated field-energy measurements across every incident and output mode. It would use correctly tuned, detuned, phase-reversed, no-load, and no-unload controls; independently measure the full state-resolved rotational and vibrational populations; measure gas heating; and show that the collected sideband replaces part of the next-cycle pump at fixed product amount and selectivity. For chemistry, product identity and carbon/oxygen balance must be measured independently. Until that is done, coherent spectroscopy, a reversed alignment trace, or light escaping a cavity counts as output observation, not reusable work.
