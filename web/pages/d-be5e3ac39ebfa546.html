Abstract.
Guo, Fang, and Lu recently proved the Komlós conjecture: for vectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}  of Euclidean norm at most one, there are signs
\varepsilon_{j}\in\{-1,1\}  with  \|\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq 3\sqrt{2\pi} .
We give a short proof of the bound  3\pi  that keeps the geometric
lifting framework of [10] while replacing the analytic core by a quadratic Dirichlet
energy. Moreover, using a more fine-grained analysis of the stability of the product-cosine function of [13] under translations, we further sharpen the bound to below  6.9013 .
On the algorithmic side, based on the polynomial-time construction of [9] we give a deterministic algorithm that finds a coloring of discrepancy at most  37.54  using at most  \widetilde{O}(mn+n^{4})  arithmetic operations.
1. Introduction
The Komlós problem (erstwhile conjecture) asks for an absolute constant  C  such that for all vectors  v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}  with  \|v_{j}\|_{2}\leq 1  there are
signs  \varepsilon_{j}\in\{-1,1\}  with  \|\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq C .
Guo, Fang, and Lu (GFL) [10] recently resolved Komlós conjecture with
C=3\sqrt{2\pi} . They use a convex-body transform and signing recursion
that originate with Banaszczyk [2], and a
cosine/Fisher initialization originating with Smirnov and
Vershynin [13]. We refer to other nice expositions of their
proof by [3, 11].
The first part of this paper studies the existence of  C . We retain the
geometric lifting framework of [10] but replace its
nonlinear analytic implementation by an argument based on a quadratic
Dirichlet energy.
The result is a short, expository proof that gives the looser bound  C=3\pi
(Theorem 2.1); we present it first because it is the simplest
form of our argument and introduces the objects that the sharper argument
refines. Specifically, we study an energy argument that controls the overlap
between a density and its translates through a single derivative
estimate, and this estimate is what determines the discrepancy bound. In particular, using a more fine-grained analysis of the stability of the overlap between the product-cosine density and its translates (rather than crudely estimating it by the energy argument),
we obtain the tighter bound  C\leq 6.9013
(Theorem 2.4 in Section 2.3), which improves
on  3\sqrt{2\pi}\approx 7.52  of [10].
The second part of this paper studies the algorithmic side of Komlós. Guo, Fang, and
Lu [9] gave the first polynomial-time algorithm for the Komlós problem, with a discrepancy bound  C=8892  and a
running time of  \widetilde{O}(n^{1}0) . The first version of this
paper [1] and, announced in parallel,
Li [12] obtained an algorithm with improved complexity and sharper constants; Li did so by screening rows, by a clock that schedules
when a row must be inspected, and by a coupled estimate for the Gram
factor and the low-spectrum resolvent, and Sections 3.4
and 4 build on all three. Section 1.1
records the chronology and the bounds.
Starting from this spectral framework, we give a deterministic
algorithm that returns a signing with  \|A\varepsilon\|_{\infty}<37.54  using
O(mn)+\widetilde{O}(n^{4})  arithmetic operations and no fast matrix
multiplication (Theorem 3.1); up to logarithmic factors, this
cost is below Li’s  n^{\omega+2}  for every attainable exponent
\omega>2 . The walk moves a fractional signing to the
boundary of the cube while barriers protect every row, and it must keep
enough free directions to do so. Our analysis counts the row constraints
and the positive curvature of a spectral potential against one budget,
and this joint count is what improves the constant. Finite-step
estimates then bound the number of moves, and certified row screening
bounds their cost, so that the work beyond one scan of the input does
not depend on  m . The proved step sizes are far too small for practice,
so Section 5 adds a routine that tries a few random
signings and returns one only when an outward-rounded interval
computation certifies its discrepancy; Appendix F
compares it with the deterministic walk on matched inputs.
Section 2 proves Theorems 2.1
and 2.4, with regularity details and the numerical
certificate in Appendix A. Section 3
states Theorem 3.1, explains the construction, and proves its
discrepancy bound, with the deferred verifications and the
finite-precision and finite-arithmetic details in
Appendices B–E;
Section 4 proves its running time; and
Section 5 gives the checked routine and discusses
limitations and open problems. The code, the experiment records, and the exact rational
certificates behind every numerical inequality in the paper are available
in the repository https://github.com/suvrit/komlos.
1.1. Related work
Komlós conjecture itself dates back to the 1980s. Early works by [6] and Spencer [14] were state-of-the-art for almost three decades. Banaszczyk [2]
proved the bound  O(\sqrt{\log n})  through studying the Gaussian measure of
convex bodies; his backward recursion of convex sets is the framework underlying the recent breakthrough work
of Guo, Fang, and Lu
[10]; Karingula and
Lovett [11] and
Bandeira [3] then gave simplified proofs, and
Section 2 gives another, with the
constants  3\pi  and  6.9013 . Guo, Fang, and Lu then gave a
polynomial-time algorithm [9]: a deterministic spectral
walk algorithm achieving discrepancy  8272  and having cost  \widetilde{O}(mn^{9}+n^{10}) , where
here and below  \widetilde{O}  suppresses polylogarithmic factors in  m
and  n . The
first version of this paper [1, Appendix B] improved this algorithm obtaining discrepancy  135  using
\widetilde{O}(mn^{3}+n^{4})  ordinary arithmetic operations, or
\widetilde{O}(mn^{\omega}+n^{\omega+1})  with faster linear algebra routines. Announced in parallel, Li [12] obtained discrepancy below  99  with  \widetilde{O}(mn+n^{\omega+2})  operations and polynomial bit complexity, through row screening, a motion clock, and a coupled Gram–resolvent
estimate. The present paper follows this line of work:
from Li’s algorithm we
adopt the row-screening mechanism and the motion clock, which determine
which rows need to be inspected at a given step. Our main new ingredient is a more refined
dimension-counting argument for the space of admissible update directions.
Our algorithm achieves a discrepancy bound
37.54  using  \widetilde{O}(mn+n^{4})  arithmetic operations.
2. Existence: a quadratic-energy proof and a sharper constant
We retain the backward propagation of convex sets from [10]
and certify that the sets stay nonempty with a witness function whose
quadratic energy is bounded uniformly in all directions. This gives the
constant  3\pi ; Section 2.3 then refines the overlap
estimate key to the argument and lowers the constant to
6.9013 .
Theorem 2.1.
For  v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}  with  \|v_{j}\|_{2}\leq 1 , there are
signs  \varepsilon_{j}\in\{-1,1\}  such that
\bigl\|\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\bigr\|_{\infty}<3\pi .
2.1. Proof mechanism
We start by summarizing the high-level mechanism behind the proof of Theorem 2.1. Let  K_{n}=(-C,C)^{d}  be the cube of side-length  2C ; for us  C=3\pi  suffices. Solving Komlós means finding a vector  z_{n}=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}  that lies in  K_{n} . We can try to reach  K_{n}  step-by-step. Say we begin with  z_{0}=0\in K_{0} ; then, at the  j -th step we choose  z_{j}\leftarrow z_{j-1}+\varepsilon_{j}v_{j} . If we can ensure that such a choice is always possible, then eventually one obtains  z_{n}=\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\in K_{n} . How should we ensure this? The key idea here is to propagate feasibility backwards. Given a valid  K_{j} , the set of states from which we can reach  K_{j}  in one step is  (K_{j}-v_{j})\cup(K_{j}+v_{j}) . But iterating these unions can produce an exponentially large “sign tree.”
We can tame this exponential blowup if we can instead arrange that
K_{j-1}\subseteq(K_{j}-v_{j})\cup(K_{j}+v_{j}) . Then, if the backward propagation
finally yields  0\in K_{0} , we may follow these inclusions forward and
claim existence of a feasible coloring. There is, however, a wrinkle: the exact
one-step feasible set  (K-v)\cup(K+v)  is typically nonconvex, which makes
it hard to analyze. We therefore seek a convex subset of this union
that is still “large enough” to be useful for sign recovery. GFL’s transform
is precisely such a convexified update:
(2.1)
\mathcal{T}_{v}K:=\bigl((K-v)\cap(K+v)\bigr)+(-2,2)v.
Indeed,  \mathcal{T}_{v}K\subseteq(K-v)\cup(K+v) : if  y=z+tv  with
z\pm v\in K  and  |t|<2 , convexity gives  y-v\in K  for  t\geq 0  and
y+v\in K  for  t\leq 0 . Thus every retained point has a valid signed
move into  K . The transform also preserves openness, boundedness,
convexity, and central symmetry. Set  K_{j-1}=\mathcal{T}_{v_{j}}K_{j}  for
j=n,\ldots,1 : we build the sets backwards and recover the signs forwards.
2.2. The crux
The crux to realizing the above existence mechanism is to ensure that convex approximations (2.1) never lead to empty sets during the propagation  K_{j-1}=\mathcal{T}_{v_{j}}K_{j} , and also that  0\in K_{0} .
A practical way to certify nonemptiness is to view each convex set  K  through a witness  \psi  that vanishes outside  K ; then, the normalization  \|\psi\|_{2}=1  certifies that  K\neq\emptyset . A bigger challenge is to maintain such densities even after updates via (2.1): and this is where the “quadratic-energy” idea central to our exposition steps in.
Formally, let  \psi_{j}\in H_{0}^{1}(K_{j})  be a witness that ensures  \|\psi_{j}\|_{2}=1 , and where  H_{0}^{1}(K)  denotes the space of square-integrable functions on a bounded, open, convex set  K  that are zero outside  K  and have square-integrable (weak) first derivatives. The corresponding quadratic-energy is defined as
(2.2)
\mathcal{E}(\psi_{j}):=\int\nabla\psi_{j}\nabla\psi_{j}^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq aI,\qquad\text{for some }\,a=1/36.
For a unit vector  e , write  \partial_{e}\psi=e\cdot\nabla\psi , so that  e^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{E}(\psi)e=\|\partial_{e}\psi\|_{2}^{2} . The uniform (matrix-order) bound  \mathcal{E}(\psi_{j})\preceq aI  limits how  \psi  varies in each direction. This uniformity is the key invariant that we target, at each step, to construct a new witness  \psi_{j-1}  on  K_{j-1}  that also satisfies (2.2).
We must now show that this invariant can indeed be propagated. Suppose therefore that a
convex set  K  carries a witness  \psi  satisfying (2.2); for
\|v\|_{2}\leq 1 , we must construct a new witness on  \mathcal{T}_{v}K . There is no obvious way to obtain such a witness directly from  \psi , especially since  \mathcal{T}_{v}  contains several translations.
Following [10], we encode the translations by introducing
an auxiliary coordinate. If  s  records displacement in the direction
v , then the translates of  K  are the horizontal sections of
B:=\{(y,s)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}:|s|<3,\ y+sv\in K\}.
For each fixed  y , the allowed values of  s  form an interval (a fiber). Center each
such interval at zero without changing its length, and call the
resulting convex body  B^{*} . As we show below, its section at  s=1  is precisely  \mathcal{T}_{v}K . Thus, we first lift  \psi  to  B , rearrange it along the fibers, and then obtain a new witness on the section at  s=1  of  B^{*} . The following lemma supplies this last step: it passes from a
witness on a symmetric convex body to one on a prescribed section.
It adapts [10, Lemma 3.4] to quadratic energy.
Lemma 2.2.
Let  B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}  be bounded, open, convex, and
invariant under  s\mapsto-s , with  D_{s}=\{y:(y,s)\in B\} .
Let  \Phi\geq 0  vanish outside  B , satisfy  \|\Phi\|_{2}=1  and have
square-integrable weak derivatives in  y  on  \mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R} .
Suppose also that
M:=\textstyle\int|s|\Phi^{2}\geq 1,\qquad E_{y}:=\textstyle\int\nabla_{y}\Phi\nabla_{y}\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq aI.
Then,  D_{1}  carries a witness of energy  a  satisfying (2.2).
Proof.
Following the strategy of [10, Lemma 3.4], we first bound the energy on  D_{1}  and then obtain one witness that controls every direction. First fix a positive definite weight matrix  Q\succ 0 ,  \trace Q=1 , and consider the energy minimization problem,
(2.3)
\lambda_{Q}(D):=\inf\,\{\trace(Q\mathcal{E}(f))\mid\ f\in H_{0}^{1}(D),\ \ \|f\|_{2}=1\}.
Using  [7, Theorem 6.2], we have as in [10] that  s\mapsto\lambda_{Q}(D_{s})  is convex, even,
and nondecreasing for  s\geq 0 .
Let  p(s)=\int\Phi(y,s)^{2}\,dy , using Jensen’s inequality we obtain,
(2.4)
\lambda_{Q}(D_{1})\leq\lambda_{Q}(D_{M})\leq\textstyle\int p(s)\lambda_{Q}(D_{s})\,ds\leq\trace(QE_{y})\leq a.
Let  0<\alpha<1 . The minimizing witness may depend on  Q . To obtain one witness controlling every direction, we now maximize  \lambda_{Q}(D_{1})  over  Q\succeq\alpha I/d ,  \trace Q=1 . Let  Q_{\alpha}  be a maximizer, and let
u_{\alpha}  be a minimizer of (2.3).
Moreover, for any unit  e  denote
Q_{e}=(1-\alpha)ee^{\mathsf{T}}+\alpha I/d . Observing that  D\lambda_{Q_{\alpha}}[H]=\trace(H\mathcal{E}(u_{\alpha})) , since  Q_{\alpha}  is a maximizer, we then obtain the upper bound
(2.5)
(1-\alpha)e^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{E}(u_{\alpha})e\leq\trace(Q_{e}\mathcal{E}(u_{\alpha}))\leq\trace(Q_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}(u_{\alpha}))=\lambda_{Q_{\alpha}}(D_{1})\leq a.
Thus,  u_{\alpha}  satisfies
\mathcal{E}(u_{\alpha})\preceq(1-\alpha)^{-1}aI , and also  \|u_{\alpha}\|_{2}=1 . Now, a standard limiting argument with  \alpha\downarrow 0 , whose details are given in Appendix A.1, yields a witness
u\in H_{0}^{1}(D_{1})  satisfying (2.2).
∎
The next lemma shows that condition (2.2) is stable under the update  \mathcal{T}_{v} : once a witness is available at one stage of the backward iteration, it can be propagated to the next stage.
Lemma 2.3.
Let  K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}  be nonempty, bounded, open, and convex, and let
\|v\|_{2}\leq 1 . If  K  carries a witness satisfying (2.2), then
\mathcal{T}_{v}K  is nonempty and carries a witness satisfying (2.2).
Proof.
The key idea underlying the construction is to show that small energy means that translating the witness changes it slowly, so translates overlap substantially. This overlap can then be turned into sufficient spread in  s , while preserving the energy bound, allowing us to invoke Lemma 2.2.
Consider therefore a witness  \psi  satisfying (2.2). Replacing it by
|\psi| , we may assume  \psi\geq 0  without changing its norm or energy.
GFL’s lift [10, Lemma 4.1] is
B=\{(y,s):|s|<3,\ y+sv\in K\} , with
\Phi(y,s)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{6}}\psi(y+sv)\mathbf{1}_{(-3,3)}(s) .
It preserves the norm and energy in  y :
\|\Phi\|_{2}=1  and
\int\nabla_{y}\Phi\nabla_{y}\Phi^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{E}(\psi)\preceq aI .
To make  \mathcal{T}_{v}K  the section at  s=1 , center each fiber
J_{y}=\{s:(y,s)\in B\}  without changing its length  \ell(y) .
This gives  B^{*}=\{(y,s):2|s|<\ell(y)\} .
Likewise, define the symmetric rearrangement  \Phi^{*}  of  \Phi  by replacing each superlevel set
\{s:\Phi(y,s)>r\}  with the centered interval of equal length, that is  \Phi^{*}(s):=\sup\{t\geq 0:\mu(t)>2|s|\}  with  \mu(t):=\bigl|\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\Phi(s)>t\}\bigr| . Then,  \Phi^{*}  vanishes outside  B^{*}  and  \|\Phi^{*}\|_{2}=1 .
Moreover, since  B  is convex,  \ell  is concave on its domain, and
B^{*}  is bounded, open, convex, and symmetric.
Its section at  s=1  is
(B^{*})_{1}=\{y:\ell(y)>2\}=\mathcal{T}_{v}K.
Indeed,  \ell(y)>2  means that  J_{y}  contains
[\tau-1,\tau+1]  for some  |\tau|<2 , or equivalently
y+(\tau\pm 1)v\in K . This is exactly the condition in (2.1).
Let  f:s\mapsto\Phi(y,s)  and  g:s\mapsto\Phi(y+te,s) . Then, as in the contraction argument [10, Lemma 4.2], we obtain, using  \|f^{*}-g^{*}\|_{2}\leq\|f-g\|_{2} , that
\|\partial_{e}\Phi^{*}\|_{2}\leq\|\partial_{e}\Phi\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{a} . Thus,  \Phi^{*}  has the energy bound  \int\nabla_{y}\Phi^{*}\nabla_{y}{\Phi^{*}}^{\mathsf{T}}=\mathcal{E}(\psi)\preceq aI  as required by Lemma 2.2.
It remains to show that  \textstyle\int|s|(\Phi^{*})^{2}\geq 1 . We relate this
quantity to the energy bound by counting pairs of  s -values in the lift.
Write  \rho=\psi^{2}  and let
O(tv):=\textstyle\int\min\{\rho(x),\rho(x+tv)\}\,dx
be the mass shared by the density and its translate.
Then, using  [10, Lemma 4.3] we obtain,
(2.6)
H:=\textstyle\int|s|(\Phi^{*})^{2}=\tfrac{1}{12}\textstyle\int_{0}^{6}(6-t)O(tv)\,dt.
Moreover, using
\partial_{v}\rho=2\psi\partial_{v}\psi  and Cauchy–Schwarz, we obtain as in [10, Lemma 3.1],
O(tv)=1-\tfrac{1}{2}\|\rho-\rho(\cdot+tv)\|_{1}\geq 1-\tfrac{t}{2}\|\partial_{v}\rho\|_{1}\geq 1-t\|\partial_{v}\psi\|_{2}.
Our choice  a=1/36  now pays off: since  \|v\|_{2}\leq 1 ,
we have  \|\partial_{v}\psi\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{a}=1/6 , and hence
(2.7)
H\geq\tfrac{3}{2}-3\|\partial_{v}\psi\|_{2}\geq 1.
Lemma 2.2, applied to  B^{*}  and  \Phi^{*} , now shows the existence of
a witness on  (B^{*})_{1}=\mathcal{T}_{v}K  satisfying (2.2).
∎
Proof of Theorem 2.1.
Let  K_{n}=(-3\pi,3\pi)^{d} . As a witness for  K_{n}  we use the
product-cosine function of Smirnov–Vershynin [13],
\psi_{n}(x)=(3\pi)^{-d/2}\prod\nolimits_{i}\cos(x_{i}/6)\mathbf{1}_{K_{n}}(x),
which satisfies  \|\psi_{n}\|_{2}=1  and  \mathcal{E}(\psi_{n})=aI .
Now, Lemma 2.3 shows that all  K_{j}  are nonempty, so that in particular  0\in K_{0} .
Therefore, using (2.1), we obtain for  j=1,\ldots,n  signs  \varepsilon_{j}  with
z_{j}=z_{j-1}+\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\in K_{j} , so that  \sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}=z_{n}\in(-3\pi,3\pi)^{d} .
∎
2.3. A sharper constant
In the proof of Lemma 2.3, we used the
estimate  1-O(tv)\leq t\|\partial_{v}\psi\|_{2}
to bound  H  in (2.6). For the product-cosine
witness rescaled to  (-C,C)^{d} , this gives  H\geq\frac{3}{2}-\frac{3\pi}{2C} .
To guarantee  H\geq 1  uniformly over directions  v  using this
estimate, we therefore need  C\geq 3\pi . We obtain a smaller constant
by estimating the overlap of  \psi^{2}  with its translates directly,
rather than through the Dirichlet energy.
Theorem 2.4.
Assume that the hypotheses of Theorem 2.1 hold. Then, there is a coloring  \varepsilon\in\{\pm 1\}^{n}
such that
\left\|\sum\nolimits_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\right\|_{\infty}<C_{\mathrm{cos}}<6.9013,
where  C_{\mathrm{cos}}\in(6.9012574,6.9012575)  is the unique solution of the equation
(2.8)
\frac{3\sqrt{2\pi}}{C_{\mathrm{cos}}}\int_{0}^{3}(1-r/3)^{2}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}r^{2}}{2C_{\mathrm{cos}}^{2}}\right)\,dr=1.
2.3.1. Averaged sensitivity under translations
For a probability density  p  on  \mathbb{R}^{d}  and a vector
u\in\mathbb{R}^{d} , define
(2.9)
\mathcal{D}_{p}(u):=\frac{1}{12}\int_{0}^{6}(6-t)\|p-p(\cdot-tu)\|_{1}\,dt.
For  p=\psi^{2} , the identity (2.6) then becomes
H=\textstyle\int|s|(\Phi^{*})^{2}=\frac{3}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}_{p}(v).
Thus  \mathcal{D}_{p}(v)\leq 1  is equivalent to the moment condition
H\geq 1  required by Lemma 2.2.
The criterion below is closely related to the “near invariance to
signed sums” argument of Karingula and Lovett [11]. They work
with the shift distance  \Delta_{p}(h):=d_{\mathrm{TV}}\!\bigl(p,p(\cdot-h)\bigr)=\frac{1}{2}\|p-p(\cdot-h)\|_{1}  at a single translation and show that their
splitting operation does not increase this distance in the remaining
directions. Here the height identity  (2.6) naturally leads instead to the
weighted integral of shift distances in (2.9).
Lemma 2.5 (Signing from integrated translation loss).
Let  K\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}  be bounded, open, and convex, and let  p  be
an even log-concave probability density, positive on  K  and zero
outside  K , with  \int_{K}p|\log p|<\infty .
If  \mathcal{D}_{p}(v_{j})\leq 1  for every  j , then
\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}v_{j}\in K  for some signs  \varepsilon_{j}\in\{-1,1\} .
Proof.
We first encode the density as the open convex epigraph of its log-potential,
\mathcal{C}_{0}=\{(x,z):x\in K,\ z>-\log p(x)\},\qquad d\nu_{0}=e^{-z}\,dx\,dz.
Then, we have that  \nu_{0}(\mathcal{C}_{0})=1  and that
its  x -barycenter is zero  \pi_{x}(b_{\mathcal{C}_{0}})=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{0}}x\,d\nu_{0}=\int_{K}x\,p(x)\,dx=0 .
Moreover, note that,
\nu_{0}\bigl(\mathcal{C}_{0}\triangle(\mathcal{C}_{0}+(w,0))\bigr)=\|p-p(\cdot-w)\|_{1},
where  \triangle  denotes symmetric set difference.
Now, consider auxiliary coordinates  \eta\in\mathbb{R}^{m}  and use
d\nu=e^{-z}\,dx\,dz\,d\eta .
For an open convex set  \mathcal{C}  of finite mass and finite first
moment, write  b_{\mathcal{C}}  for its barycenter and, whenever  u  has zero
z  coordinate, let
\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(u)=\frac{1}{12\nu(\mathcal{C})}\int_{0}^{6}(6-t)\nu\bigl(\mathcal{C}\triangle(\mathcal{C}+tu)\bigr)\,dt.
We show by induction that  \mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(u_{j})\leq 1  for all  j  implies that
b_{\mathcal{C}}+\sum_{j}\varepsilon_{j}u_{j}\in\mathcal{C} .
More specifically, we prove the assertion by induction on the number  m  of vectors.
If  m=0 , the desired conclusion is
simply  b_{\mathcal{C}}\in\mathcal{C}.
Indeed, if the barycenter did not belong to the open convex set
\mathcal{C} , a separating or supporting hyperplane would give a linear
functional  \ell  and a constant  a  such that
\ell(y)<a  for every  y\in\mathcal{C} , while
\ell(b_{\mathcal{C}})\geq a . But this would contradict  \ell(b_{\mathcal{C}})=\frac{1}{\nu(\mathcal{C})}\int_{\mathcal{C}}\ell(y)\,d\nu(y)<a.
Now suppose  m\geq 1  and set  u=u_{m} .
Let  y  denote a point in the current ambient space of  \mathcal{C} , and
define
J_{y}:=\{s\in(-3,3):y+su\in\mathcal{C}\},\qquad\ell(y):=|J_{y}|,
together with the lifted set
T:=\{(y,h):0<h<\ell(y)\}.
Then, as in the proof of Lemma 2.3 we have that  \ell  is concave and lower semicontinuous, and therefore  T
is open and convex.
Equip  T  with  d\widetilde{\nu}=d\nu\,dh . Then, we have that,
\widetilde{\nu}(T)=6\nu(\mathcal{C}),\qquad b_{T}=(b_{\mathcal{C}},h_{*}),\qquad h_{*}=\frac{\int\ell(y)^{2}\,d\nu(y)}{12\nu(\mathcal{C})}.
Moreover, note that,
\int\ell^{2}\,d\nu=2\int_{0}^{6}(6-t)\nu\bigl(\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathcal{C}+tu)\bigr)\,dt.
Using  \nu(\mathcal{C}\cap(\mathcal{C}+tu))=\nu(\mathcal{C})-\tfrac{1}{2}\nu(\mathcal{C}\triangle(\mathcal{C}+tu)) , we obtain  h_{*}=3-\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(u)\geq 2.
Now, for every  w  with zero  z  coordinate, we obtain,
\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}\bigl(T\triangle(T+(w,0))\bigr)
\displaystyle=\int|\ell(y)-\ell(y-w)|\,d\nu(y)
\displaystyle\leq 6\nu\bigl(\mathcal{C}\triangle(\mathcal{C}+w)\bigr),
and therefore
\mathcal{D}_{T}((w,0))\leq\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(w) .
For each  j<m , extend  u_{j}  to  \bar{u}_{j}:=(u_{j},0)
in the enlarged space containing  T . Since the lift does not
increase the average shift distance  \mathcal{D}_{T}(\bar{u}_{j})\leq\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{C}}(u_{j})\leq 1  for  \qquad j=1,\ldots,m-1 ,
we may therefore apply the induction hypothesis in  T  to collection of vectors
\bar{u}_{1},\ldots,\bar{u}_{m-1} . Therefore, using that  b_{T}=(b_{\mathcal{C}},h_{*}),
there are signs  \varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{m-1}  such that

3.2. How the algorithm works
We first give an informal description of the algorithm. Starting from the initial signing  x=0 , the algorithm moves inside the cube  [-1,1]^{n}  until every coordinate reaches  \pm 1 . At each move, it chooses a direction  h  subject to linear constraints that protect rows whose discrepancy is close to its allowed bound. The main difficulty is to ensure that these constraints do not use up too many dimensions. Our spectral potential is designed precisely for this purpose: it controls how many rows can be protected simultaneously, while still leaving sufficiently many directions along which the signing can move.
To organize these constraints, we classify rows according to their remaining squared mass on the active coordinates. A row with large remaining mass is kept fixed by requiring its retained part to be orthogonal to every update direction. There can be only few such rows, since the sum of the remaining squared masses over all rows is at most the number of active coordinates. Once a row is no longer large, we remove any coefficient that accounts for too large a fraction of its remaining squared mass. After these removals, the retained part of the row is sufficiently diffuse for the spectral estimates used later. The removed coefficients are not discarded from the analysis: their possible contribution to the final discrepancy is tracked separately, and a telescoping argument bounds the resulting error.
For the retained coefficients, a concave profile measures how much
rounding remains; combining the resulting energy with the current row sum
gives a concave barrier. Because the barrier is concave, a direction
tangent to a nearly tight barrier keeps it feasible for either sign of
the move. Exponential weights place all the barriers in one positive
semidefinite matrix, whose top eigenvalue does two jobs: it limits the
number of nearly tight barriers, and it supplies the spectral part of
the potential.
The difficulty is that the top eigenvector changes during a move. Its
response adds positive curvature, so the concavity of the row barriers
does not by itself produce a useful direction. We therefore normalize
the matrix to separate the diagonal contribution of the large rows,
cancel the most sensitive spectral components, and bound the remaining
response by a joint inertia argument. This joint count is the source
of our improvement: it charges the row restrictions and the directions of
positive spectral curvature to one budget, instead of giving each kind of
loss its own worst-case allowance. While  s>336  coordinates remain
active, the resulting count leaves more than  s/400  free dimensions.
Once  s\leq 336 , a weighted quadratic rounding rule controls both the
remaining row error and the displacement of the signing, and together
with the tracking bound it gives the constant  37.54 .
A good local direction is only the first part of an algorithm: we must
also take finite steps, bound their number, and pay for maintaining the
rows. Spreading each direction across the active coordinates controls how
much the potential can change along a segment, and moving orthogonally to
the current signing makes its squared norm a measure of progress. Most
rows, finally, need not be evaluated at every move, because certificates
identify rows whose weights remain negligible until a computable time.
Our construction builds on the spectral framework
of [9], which in turn rests on the energy regularization
of Bansal and Jiang [5] and on the concave
row barriers and restricted-concavity lemma of Guillen and
Kobzar [8], as Guo, Fang, and Lu
acknowledge. From Li [12] it takes the warm and
cold screening of rows, the motion clock that schedules their inspection,
and the aggregate row statistics with their elementary identities
(Sections 4.3, 5, and 9 of [12]); Li’s coupled
Gram–resolvent inequality (Lemma 2.4 there) is the starting point of
the response bound in Section 3.4, where we prove the
form of it that we need. What is new here is the joint inertia
calculation, its normalization, and the common dimension budget for
protected pairs, large rows, and spectral response; in place of Li’s
quadratic potential charge, we control the tracked-sum energy, the sum
of the squared tracked row sums, directly by two additional families of
direction constraints (Section 4.2). The argument below is self-contained, with the finite arithmetic details deferred to
Appendices C–E; the exact rational
certificates of the numerical inequalities it uses are in the
repository https://github.com/suvrit/komlos.
3.3. Controlling the rows during rounding
We first describe what the walk keeps from each row and what it allows
itself to discard. The distinction matters at the end, because the two
parts are controlled differently: a barrier controls the retained sum,
whereas the total absolute mass of the discarded coefficients controls
their contribution. Throughout,  \|\cdot\|  denotes
the Euclidean norm or its induced matrix norm,  \circ  denotes
coordinatewise multiplication, and  t_{+}=\max(t,0) .
3.3.1. The input scan
A row with  \|a_{i}\|_{1}\leq 37  is safe for every signing, because
|(A\varepsilon)_{i}|\leq 37<C_{*} , so the walk retains only the other  m_{0}  rows.
Few rows survive: for every survivor, Cauchy–Schwarz gives
\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}>37^{2}/n , while summing the column norm assumptions gives
\sum_{i}\|a_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq n . Thus
(3.1)
m_{0}<n^{2}/37^{2}.
The scan costs  O(mn)  field operations and comparisons, and the
surviving matrix still has column norms at most one. Terminal rounding
and all later row operations use this matrix only, and the omitted rows
never need to be checked again.
3.3.2. Retained sums and the cost of deleting a coefficient
The walk starts at  x=0\in[-1,1]^{n} . A coordinate is active while
|x_{j}|<1 , and once it reaches the boundary it stays fixed; write  V  for
the active set and  s=|V|  for its size. Each row  i  retains a set
T_{i}\subseteq V  and an offset  c_{i} , initially  T_{i}=V  and  c_{i}=0 .
Write

Thus  q_{i}  is the remaining squared mass and  d_{i}  is the tracked sum.
When a retained coefficient is removed, its current contribution
a_{ij}x_{j}  moves into  c_{i} , which preserves the current value of  d_{i} .
If the coordinate is frozen, it never changes again and this transfer
accounts for it exactly; if it is still active, the removed coefficient
may contribute a later error.
The column norm assumption gives  \sum_{i}q_{i}\leq s . We hold a row fixed while  q_{i}>R^{2}  by imposing
b_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}h=0  on every direction  h ; these large rows keep
d_{i}=0  and cost fewer than  s/R^{2}  equations. A row is medium
when  0<q_{i}\leq R^{2}  and completed when  q_{i}=0 . Since
coefficients only leave the retained sets, a row can only move down
through these classes.
In a medium row we repeatedly delete an entry with
p_{ij}>\Delta q_{i} , updating  q_{i}  each time and choosing the eligible
entry with the least original column index. The resulting row satisfies the diffuseness condition
\max_{j}p_{ij}\leq\Delta q_{i} ; Section 3.4 uses this condition to bound the positive
curvature of the row’s exponential weight. The price of enforcing it is
a tracking error, which the next lemma bounds. Let  D_{i}  be the
deliberately deleted coordinates of row  i , and let  x_{j}^{\rm del}  be
the value of  x_{j}  when its coefficient was deleted.
Lemma 3.2 (Tracking with the remaining mass retained).
Put  A_{\Delta}=(1+\sqrt{1-\Delta})/\sqrt{\Delta} ; since

v^{\mathsf{T}}M^{\prime\prime}[h,h]\,v=h^{\mathsf{T}}(\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{D})h . When the cold Gram
terms are held fixed (Section 3.3), the sums defining
\mathcal{U}  and  \mathcal{D}  run over the warm pairs only; this is the
form used below.
The ridge makes the top eigenvalue simple, with gap at least  \eta :
the matrix  M-\eta vv^{\mathsf{T}}  remains entrywise nonnegative and has the
positive eigenvector  v  with eigenvalue  L-\eta , which must therefore
be its spectral radius, while every other eigenvalue of  M  is
unchanged. The
eigenvector equation also gives  Lv_{i}\geq\eta\sum_{j}v_{j}\geq\eta , so
\nu:=\min_{i}v_{i}\geq\eta/2\qquad\text{while }L<2.
Because medium rows are diffuse,  z_{rj}\leq\Delta=d_{0}^{2}  and
|(g_{r})_{j}|\leq\sqrt{z_{rj}}(1+d_{0}|f^{\prime}(x_{j})|) . The profile satisfies
1+d_{0}|f^{\prime}(x)|=\frac{3}{3-2|x|},\qquad-f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{6}{d_{0}(3-2|x|)^{2}},
which gives the exact identity
\frac{(1+d_{0}|f^{\prime}(x)|)^{2}}{-f^{\prime\prime}(x)}=\frac{3d_{0}}{2}=\frac{d_{0}}{c_{0}}=\frac{78}{173}.
With  \rho=\beta d_{0}/c_{0}=1326/4325 , it yields the diagonal comparison
\mathcal{U}_{jj}\leq\rho\mathcal{D}_{jj} . Moreover,  \|g_{r}\|\leq 3
and  \beta\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\Delta=5304/4325 ; the appendices use these
bounds in the forms  \beta\|g_{r}\|\leq 51/25<25/8  and
\beta\|f^{\prime\prime}\|_{\infty}\Delta<9/4 . Since the profile is twice continuously
differentiable with Lipschitz second derivative, the third-derivative
estimate in (3.14) below holds almost everywhere, including
across a coordinate’s zero crossing.
This diagonal comparison limits the positive directions of
\mathcal{U}-\mathcal{D} , but it covers only the direct contribution to
the curvature of  L . The motion of the Perron vector contributes
another nonnegative form,  2(M^{\prime}[h]v)^{\mathsf{T}}(LI-M)^{\dagger}M^{\prime}[h]v , which is
large near the top eigenspace; we call  M^{\prime}[h]v  the forcing, and
after the normalization below split it into a high part, on
normalized eigenvalues at least  18/25  of the top one, and a
low part. Li’s coupled estimate [12, Lemma 2.4]
bounds the low response by a scalar multiple of  \mathcal{U} . We instead
cancel the high forcing by linear equations and keep the low response
explicitly, because the scalar bound would lose the information that the
joint count uses.
3.4.2. Normalizing the response
The large rows are fixed by exact equations, so we normalize their
deposit  B  away: at an anchor with  L>\ell_{0}  put
D_{L}=LI-B,\qquad S_{L}=D_{L}^{-1/2},\qquad K_{L}=S_{L}(W+\eta\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}})S_{L}.
The column norm bound gives  B\preceq B_{0}I , and  B_{0}\leq 7/8<\ell_{0} , so
D_{L}\succ 0 . The normalized matrix  K_{L}  is PSD and entrywise positive,
has Perron eigenvalue one, and has the positive unit Perron vector
\tilde{v}=D_{L}^{1/2}v/t_{0} , where  t_{0}=\|D_{L}^{1/2}v\| . Its gap below one
is at least  \eta/L>\eta/2 , by Courant–Fischer applied to
I-K_{L}=S_{L}(LI-M)S_{L}  with  \|D_{L}\|\leq L .
The normalization preserves the response: if  y\perp v , then
(3.7)
y^{\mathsf{T}}(LI-M)^{\dagger}y=(S_{L}y)^{\mathsf{T}}(I-K_{L})^{\dagger}(S_{L}y).
To check this without a congruence rule for pseudoinverses, note that
S_{L}y\perp\tilde{v} . The vector  S_{L}(I-K_{L})^{\dagger}S_{L}y  solves
(LI-M)z=y , and it differs from the reduced solution only by a multiple
of  v , whose inner product with  y  is zero.
For the analysis, factor  W+\eta\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}=CC^{\mathsf{T}}  with one column
\sqrt{a_{r}}z_{r}  for each row pair and the ridge column  \sqrt{\eta}\mathbf{1} .
For a varying warm pair, let the corresponding row of  G  be
\sqrt{a_{r}}\beta(z_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}v)g_{r}^{\mathsf{T}} , and use zero rows for the ridge and
for frozen cold terms. These factors appear only in the argument, so
their square roots are not arithmetic primitives. On the curve with
frozen cold terms, the forcing is  M^{\prime}[h]v=CGh , and  G^{\mathsf{T}}G=\mathcal{U}
is the warm-pair form, with diagonal negative term  \mathcal{D} . Since
the normalized Gram matrix is  C_{L}C_{L}^{\mathsf{T}}=K_{L} , with  C_{L}=S_{L}C , the
reciprocal Gram count of Lemma 3.4 below applies to the
normalized low response.
3.4.3. The computed curvature form
The algorithm works with rational approximations to these matrices. The
top eigenvalue of  M  is
reported with error at most  \delta  (Appendix C). If the
reported value is at most  1-\delta , then the true value satisfies

L>1-2\delta>\ell_{0} , and the high branch computes a normalized

Appendix C specifies the approximations and proves their

approximates  S_{L} , and  \bar{W}  is formed from the approximate warm

matrix  \bar{K}=\bar{S}(\bar{W}+\eta\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}})\bar{S}  and lies within

R_{K}=(V_{K})_{F^{c}}\operatorname{diag}((k_{*}-(D_{K})_{jj})^{-1})(V_{K})_{F^{c}}^{\mathsf{T}}.
Using the computed Perron vector  \bar{v}  of  M  and nonnegative weights
\bar{a}_{r} , form  \bar{U},\bar{D}  by the formulas for

The equations  (V_{K})_{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{S}\bar{B}_{1}h=0  cancel the computed high

The column with the largest reported eigenvalue approximates  \tilde{v}

3.4.4. Counting cancellation and positive curvature together
Two lemmas explain why retaining the response helps. The first charges
positive curvature according to the reciprocal eigenvalues of its Gram
multiplier; the second applies this charge to the low response and adds
the number of high spectral equations.
Lemma 3.4 (Reciprocal Gram inertia).
Let  D\succeq 0  be an  s\times s  diagonal matrix, let  G  be a real
matrix with  s  columns, and suppose  (G^{\mathsf{T}}G)_{jj}\leq\rho D_{jj}  for
every  j . Let  \Gamma\succeq I  be symmetric, of size equal to the
number of rows of  G , with eigenvalues  \gamma_{1}\geq\gamma_{2}\geq\cdots .
If  G^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma G-D  has  k  positive eigenvalues, then
\rho s\geq\operatorname{tr}(D^{\dagger/2}G^{\mathsf{T}}GD^{\dagger/2})\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma_{j}^{-1},
with strict inequality in the second comparison when  k>0 .
Proof.
A zero diagonal entry of  D  forces the corresponding column of  G  to
vanish, so both matrices vanish on that coordinate and it can be
removed. Since congruence by  D^{-1/2}  preserves inertia, we may put
Y=GD^{-1/2}  and choose an orthonormal matrix  X  spanning the
positive eigenspace of  Y^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma Y-I . Then  \Sigma^{2}=X^{\mathsf{T}}Y^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma YX\succ I
for a positive definite  \Sigma , the columns of
\Omega=\Gamma^{1/2}YX\Sigma^{-1}  are orthonormal, and
\operatorname{tr}(Y^{\mathsf{T}}Y)\geq\|YX\|_{F}^{2}=\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma^{2}\Omega^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma^{-1}\Omega)>\operatorname{tr}(\Omega^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma^{-1}\Omega)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\gamma_{j}^{-1}.
Here the diagonal hypothesis bounds the first trace by  \rho s , and the
last inequality is the variational principle for the sum of the  k
smallest eigenvalues of  \Gamma^{-1} .
∎
Lemma 3.5 (A rational joint count).
Suppose  N=UDU^{\mathsf{T}}\succeq 0 , where  U  is exactly orthogonal,  D  is
diagonal,  \lambda_{*}=\max_{j}D_{jj}>0 , and  CC^{\mathsf{T}}\preceq N+\epsilon I .
Let the high set  F_{0}  consist of the indices  j  with
D_{jj}\geq(18/25)\lambda_{*} . Put
\mathcal{R}=U_{F_{0}^{c}}\operatorname{diag}((\lambda_{*}-D_{jj})^{-1})U_{F_{0}^{c}}^{\mathsf{T}},\qquad Q=G^{\mathsf{T}}G-D_{0}+2G^{\mathsf{T}}C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{R}CG,
where  G  has  s  columns,  C  has as many columns as  G  has rows,
N  has as many rows as  C ,  D_{0}\succeq 0  is diagonal, and
(G^{\mathsf{T}}G)_{jj}\leq\rho(D_{0})_{jj} . If  Q  has  k  positive eigenvalues,
then
|F_{0}|+k\leq\frac{25}{23}\rho s+\frac{32}{23}\frac{\operatorname{tr}N}{\lambda_{*}}+\frac{50}{23}\frac{\epsilon s}{\lambda_{*}},
with strict inequality when  k>0 .
Proof.
We apply Lemma 3.4 with  D_{0}  in place of  D  and
\Gamma=I+2C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{R}C , so that  G^{\mathsf{T}}\Gamma G-D_{0}=Q . By the
stated PSD domination and the variational principle, the nonzero
eigenvalues of  \mathcal{R}^{1/2}CC^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{R}^{1/2} , which are those
of  C^{\mathsf{T}}\mathcal{R}C , are bounded, in decreasing order, by the numbers
(D_{jj}+\epsilon)/(\lambda_{*}-D_{jj})  with  j\notin F_{0} ; this is the
comparison behind Li’s coupled estimate [12, Lemma 2.4],
retained eigenvalue by eigenvalue instead of being reduced to its largest
value. Consequently each relevant reciprocal eigenvalue of  \Gamma  is at
least
\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda+2\epsilon/\lambda_{*}}\geq\frac{1-\lambda}{1+\lambda}-\frac{2\epsilon}{\lambda_{*}}\geq\frac{23}{25}-\frac{32}{25}\lambda-\frac{2\epsilon}{\lambda_{*}},\qquad\lambda=D_{jj}/\lambda_{*}.
The last inequality is the supporting tangent at  \lambda=1/4 : its
difference from the left-hand rational function is exactly
32(\lambda-1/4)^{2}/[25(1+\lambda)]\geq 0 . Write
\operatorname{tr}N_{\rm low}=\sum_{j\notin F_{0}}D_{jj}  and
\operatorname{tr}N_{\rm high}=\sum_{j\in F_{0}}D_{jj} . Padding the low
list by zeros if  k  exceeds its length and summing gives
\frac{23}{25}k<\rho s+\frac{32}{25}\frac{\operatorname{tr}N_{\rm low}}{\lambda_{*}}+\frac{2\epsilon s}{\lambda_{*}}.
On the high list  \lambda\geq 18/25>23/32 , so
(23/25)|F_{0}|\leq(32/25)\operatorname{tr}N_{\rm high}/\lambda_{*} , and
adding the two inequalities proves the claim. When  k=0 , the high-list
bound and the nonnegativity of all the other terms suffice.
∎
The tangent in this proof gives the coefficients  a_{t}=\frac{25}{23} , and  b_{t}=\frac{32}{23}  of  \rho s  and  \operatorname{tr}N/\lambda_{*} ; its zero  23/32  lies
below the cutoff  18/25 , which is what the high-list bound uses. In the
high branch of the algorithm, Lemma 3.5 is applied to the
computed objects of Section 3.4:  N=N_{K} ,  U=V_{K} ,
F_{0}=F ,  \mathcal{R}=R_{K} ,  \lambda_{*}=k_{*} , and  \epsilon=\delta ; the
matrix  G=\bar{G}  has the row  \sqrt{\bar{a}_{r}}\beta(z_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{v})g_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}
for each warm pair and zero rows otherwise;  C=\bar{S}\bar{C} , where
\bar{C}  has the columns  \sqrt{\bar{a}_{r}}z_{r}  and  \sqrt{\eta}\mathbf{1} ; and
D_{0}=\bar{D} . Then  \bar{G}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{G}=\bar{U} ,  C\bar{G}=\bar{S}\bar{B}_{1} ,
and  Q=\bar{Q} , while Appendix C checks that
CC^{\mathsf{T}}=\bar{K}\preceq N_{K}+\delta I .
3.4.5. Charging row protection to the same matrix
To decide which barriers to protect, approximate each warm row-sign
pair’s barrier with absolute error at most  1/1000 , and protect the pair
when the computed value is at least  -1/200 ; every protected pair
receives the exact equation  g_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}h=0 . A protected pair then has true
u_{r}\geq-1/100 , and an unprotected warm pair has  u_{r}<-1/250 .
Lemma 3.6 (Joint charge for protected pairs, large rows, and response).
Suppose the anchor lies in the high branch, so that  \ell_{0}<L<L_{*} . Let
m_{L}  be the number of large rows,  p  the number of protected pairs,
and  k  the number of positive eigenvalues of  \bar{Q} , and let
c_{p}=\frac{\theta^{4}}{A_{0}(1-\beta/100)} . If
(3.9)
B_{0}R^{2}\left(c_{p}+\frac{b_{t}\Delta}{\ell_{0}-B_{0}}\right)\leq 1,
then
m_{L}+p+|F|+k\leq\left(\frac{1}{R^{2}}+a_{t}\rho+b_{t}\Delta+c_{p}(L_{*}-B_{0})\right)s+\frac{s}{10000},
where the last term is the precision and ridge allowance of
Appendix C.
Proof.
A protected pair  r=(i,\sigma)  has  u_{r}\geq-1/100  and
\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}z_{r}=q_{i}/r_{i}^{2}>\theta^{-2} , so it contributes at least
A_{0}\theta^{-4}(1-\beta/100)=1/c_{p}  to  \mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}W\mathbf{1} . Hence
p\leq c_{p}(Ls-\operatorname{tr}B-\eta s^{2}),\qquad\operatorname{tr}B\geq B_{0}R^{2}m_{L}.
Diffuseness gives  \|z_{r}\|^{2}\leq\Delta(\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}z_{r})^{2} , so
\operatorname{tr}W\leq\Delta\,\mathbf{1}^{\mathsf{T}}W\mathbf{1} ; with  D_{L}\succeq(L-B_{0})I
this gives
\operatorname{tr}K_{L}\leq(\Delta(Ls-\operatorname{tr}B)+\eta s)/(L-B_{0}) .
Lemma 3.5, applied as above, and the trace comparison
\operatorname{tr}N_{K}/k_{*}\leq\operatorname{tr}K_{L}+1024\delta s  of
Appendix C then give
|F|+k\leq a_{t}\rho s+b_{t}\frac{\Delta(Ls-\operatorname{tr}B)+\eta s}{L-B_{0}}
up to the diagonalization error. We add  p  and  m_{L} , discard the negative ridge
term, and use the trace lower bound; the coefficient of  m_{L}  is then
1-B_{0}R^{2}\left(c_{p}+\frac{b_{t}\Delta}{L-B_{0}}\right)\geq 0,
by the deposit condition (3.9) and  L>\ell_{0} . It is therefore valid to
substitute  m_{L}\leq s/R^{2} , after which the two terms with denominator
L-B_{0}  combine to  b_{t}\Delta s  and the protected charge becomes
c_{p}(L-B_{0})s . Finally we use  L<L_{*} . The positive ridge remainder
b_{t}\eta s/(L-B_{0})\leq b_{t}\eta s/(\ell_{0}-B_{0})  and the diagonalization
error fit the allowance  s/10000  of Appendix C.
∎

two tangency equations, and a small set of tracked-sum constraints used
for screening, and the fraction left over will exceed  1/400  whenever
s>336 .
Numerically,
c_{p}=\frac{\theta^{4}}{A_{0}(1-\beta/100)}=0.161740798067016\ldots,

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1}{R^{2}}-a_{t}\rho-b_{t}\Delta-c_{p}(L_{*}-B_{0})-\frac{1}{10000}-\frac{2}{337}-\frac{1}{4094}

3.5. Finite steps and termination
The dimension count guarantees freedom at a point, but a finite
algorithm needs a direction that remains useful over a nonzero interval,
and it needs a way to finish once few coordinates remain. We first
construct an exactly feasible rational basis and then choose in its span
a vector whose coordinates and row derivatives are small. Such a vector
controls the Taylor remainder, and it yields a step whose length can be
charged to the growth of  \|x\|^{2} . Section 3.6 then
rounds the remaining at most  336  coordinates and proves the
discrepancy bound.
3.5.1. An exactly feasible negative basis
Approximate eigenvectors need not satisfy the row equations exactly, so
we impose the equations with an exact projector before selecting the
negative subspace, and we project the computed basis once more
afterward. The equations are the large-row equations, every protected
gradient equation,  x_{V}^{\mathsf{T}}h=0 , the tracked-sum tangency
(4.2), and the high row-Gram equations of
Section 4.2; in the high branch we add
(V_{K})_{F}^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{S}\bar{B}_{1}h=0 . Extract independent constraint rows into
E_{0}  and form the exact projector  P=I-E_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}(E_{0}E_{0}^{\mathsf{T}})^{-1}E_{0} ,
with  P=I  when there are no rows. Let
Q_{\rm neg}=P(\bar{Q}-\eta I)P+(I-P)
in the high branch and  Q_{\rm neg}=-\eta P+(I-P)  in the low branch.
By Lemma 3.6 and the dimension count
(3.10),  Q_{\rm neg}  has more than  s/400  eigenvalues at most  -\eta . We
diagonalize  Q_{\rm neg}  as in (C.1) with accuracy
\xi=\eta/2^{24} , obtaining  \hat{V}\hat{D}\hat{V}^{\mathsf{T}}  with  \hat{V}
exactly orthogonal, and retain the columns reported at most  -3\eta/4 .
They form a matrix  V_{-}  with  k>s/400  columns and reported eigenvalues
D_{-} , and we put
(3.11)
Z=PV_{-},\qquad T=Z/(1+\xi).
The projected basis  T  is exactly feasible and satisfies
(3/4)I_{k}\preceq T^{\mathsf{T}}T\preceq I_{k} . For the lower bound, the residual
identity  (I-P)V_{-}(I-D_{-})=(I-P)(Q_{\rm neg}-\hat{V}\hat{D}\hat{V}^{\mathsf{T}})V_{-}
and  I-D_{-}\succeq I  give  \|(I-P)V_{-}\|\leq\xi . Since the retained columns satisfy
(V_{-}a)^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\rm neg}V_{-}a\leq(-3\eta/4+\xi)\|a\|^{2} , projection
preserves negativity:
(Za)^{\mathsf{T}}(\bar{Q}-\eta I)(Za)=(V_{-}a)^{\mathsf{T}}Q_{\rm neg}(V_{-}a)-\|(I-P)V_{-}a\|^{2}<0.
Thus every nonzero  h\in\operatorname{im}T  satisfies
h^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{Q}h<\eta\|h\|^{2} .
This basis gives negative curvature for the full potential. Let
\phi(L)=(L-1)_{+}^{3} ; since  L<L_{*} , we have  \phi^{\prime}(L)\leq 3/65536  and
\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)\leq 3/128 . For exactly feasible  h  in the high branch,
Appendix C shows that  |L^{\prime}[h]|\leq 2^{30}\delta\|h\|/\eta
and, in its response-error bound (C.2), that
|L^{\prime\prime}[h,h]-h^{\mathsf{T}}\bar{Q}h|\leq 2^{40}\delta\|h\|^{2}/\eta^{3}=\eta^{2}\|h\|^{2}/2^{60} .
Since
\Phi^{\prime\prime}[h,h]=\phi^{\prime}(L)L^{\prime\prime}[h,h]+\phi^{\prime\prime}(L)L^{\prime}[h]^{2}-2\eta\|h\|^{2} , these
bounds give, for every  h\in\operatorname{im}T ,
(3.12)
\Phi^{\prime\prime}[h,h]\leq-\frac{19}{10}\eta\|h\|^{2}.
In the low branch the first two derivatives of  \phi(L)  vanish, which
gives this conclusion directly.
3.5.2. Spreading the direction and choosing a finite step
A vector concentrated on a few coordinates could change a row weight too
rapidly, even if its curvature at the anchor were favorable, so we use
the dimension of  \operatorname{im}T  to spread the direction.
The finite conditional-expectation construction of
Appendix E is applied to the test vectors  e_{j}  and
(1-c_{0})g_{r}=g_{r}/3  for warm pairs, all of norm at most one. With
\kappa=\lceil\log_{2}(8(n+2m_{0}))\rceil=O(\log(2+n)),
which bounds the logarithm of the number of tests, it returns
h_{0}\in\operatorname{im}T  with  3/4\leq\|h_{0}\|\leq 1  and every test
bounded by  3\sqrt{\kappa/k} , where  k>s/400  is the number of columns
of  T . Put  h=h_{0}/16 , and use  K=1  if
9\kappa/k\geq 1 ; otherwise halve  K  from one until the next square
would fall below  9\kappa/k . Then
(3.13)
\frac{1}{32}\leq\|h\|\leq\frac{1}{16},\quad 16\|h\|_{\infty}\leq K,\quad|\beta g_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}h|\leq K,\quad\eta\leq K\leq 1,\quad K^{2}\leq\min(1,14400\kappa/s).

and the profile derivatives give
(3.14)
\ell_{r}(0)=0,\quad|\ell_{r}^{\prime}(0)|\leq K,\quad-K^{2}/2\leq\ell_{r}^{\prime\prime}\leq 0,\quad|\ell_{r}^{(3)}|\leq K^{3}/4\quad\text{almost everywhere}.

Appendix C give these bounds directly.

the spectral term along a whole segment. For a curve  M(t) , the
matrices  K_{L}  and  S_{L}  are formed at  t=0  as in
Section 3.4, and  P_{(3/4,1]}(K_{L})  denotes the spectral

Lemma 3.7 (Finite Perron estimate).
Let  M(t)=B+C_{0}C_{0}^{\mathsf{T}}+\sum_{r}a_{r}e^{\ell_{r}(t)}z_{r}z_{r}^{\mathsf{T}}  be a PSD,
entrywise nonnegative Gram curve, with  B  diagonal,
0\preceq B\preceq(7/8)I , and  C_{0},z_{r}\geq 0 , and assume the bounds
(3.14) with  K\leq 1 . At  t=0  assume  \ell_{0}\leq L\leq 9/8 ,

minimum entry  \nu , and put  k_{v}=1+\log(1/\nu) . Suppose that the high
normalized forcing satisfies
e=\|P_{(3/4,1]}(K_{L})S_{L}M^{\prime}(0)v\|\leq K\zeta\nu/2^{16}.
Then, for  |t|\leq\sqrt{\zeta}/(4096Kk_{v})  in the interval on which the
curve is defined, the function
\Phi(t)=(\lambda_{1}(M(t))-1)_{+}^{3}-\pi(t) , with  \pi  any quadratic
polynomial, satisfies
\Phi(t)\leq\Phi(0)+\Phi^{\prime}(0)t+\tfrac{1}{2}\Phi^{\prime\prime}(0)t^{2}+2^{16}K^{3}|t|^{3}+2^{20}K^{4}t^{4}/\zeta.
The following schedule makes this remainder smaller than the quadratic
decrease.
Put  c_{*}=2^{-56}  and  H=\lceil\log_{2}(4/\eta)\rceil , and choose the
largest dyadic  \tau  satisfying
(3.15)
\tau\leq c_{*},\qquad K^{2}\tau\leq c_{*}\eta,\qquad K^{2}H^{2}\tau^{2}\leq c_{*}^{2}\eta.
These are rational tests, and  \tau\geq c_{*}\eta/(2H) . We apply
Lemma 3.7 with  \zeta=\eta , the gap of  M .

4. Running time: screening and the arithmetic bound
The number of moves is now bounded, but their cost still depends on how
many rows are evaluated. Forming every row’s Gram contribution at every
anchor would cost  O(m_{0}s^{2})  per move, and since the input scan can
leave  O(n^{2})  rows, this estimate does not give the quartic bound. We
therefore evaluate only the rows whose weights may matter and certify how
long the others can wait; a bound on the total squared tracked sum limits
the number of rows that require immediate work. The screening, the
clock, and the maintained statistics follow
Li [12, Sections 4.3, 5, and 9], including their elementary
identities; the tracked-sum constraints of Section 4.2
take the place of Li’s quadratic potential charge.
4.1. Certifying that a row can wait
We certify that both weights of a row are negligible from an upper
bound on its barrier. Let  \eta_{0}=1/(2^{40}Jn) ,  \delta_{0}=\eta_{0}^{5}/2^{100} , and
set
(4.1)

4.3. When a cold row must be inspected
To schedule the inspection of cold rows, we use Li’s motion
clock [12]: maintain a rational clock  \vartheta , initially zero, that adds  |t|
for every move  th ; since  \|h\|\leq 1/16 , the clock dominates path
length. If a row was certified at time  \vartheta_{i}  with score  \chi_{i}
and scale  r_{i}>0 , then while it remains cold,
\chi_{i}(\vartheta)\leq\chi_{i}(\vartheta_{i})+r_{i}(\vartheta_{i})(\vartheta-\vartheta_{i}).

cleanup preserves that sum and only lowers its scale. We store the
rational expiration time

valid through support deletion and scale decrease; completed rows leave
the queue, and a row newly demoted from warm receives a new key. The
hysteresis rules leave every cold renewal at least one unit of score
slack, so successive due inspections of a row with entry scale
r_{i}^{\rm ent}  are at least  1/r_{i}^{\rm ent}  apart in clock time,
even when an intervening warm period occurs.

phase of initial size  S , the exact constraint  x_{V}^{\mathsf{T}}h=0  and

O(SJ^{2}\kappa^{2})  moves, so Cauchy–Schwarz, including the short moves, gives

that  s  is nonincreasing along the clock.

\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\rm ent}\leq\theta\sqrt{m_{0}n} . For two consecutive due

We now combine the three costs: forming directions from warm rows,

With  |\mathcal{W}|  warm rows at an anchor, each anchor costs

5.1. A checked proposal routine
The finite construction proves termination, but its prescribed step
sizes are far smaller than those used in successful numerical runs: at
m=n=10^{4} , in the first phase with  K=1 , the step schedule (3.15) gives
\tau=2^{-114}  and a displacement of at most  2^{-118} . A direct test of
the final signing lets inexpensive candidates be accepted without
following that schedule.
Algorithm 5.1 (Checked proposal algorithm).
For a finite binary64 matrix  A , use the following routine.
(1)

Select the candidate with the smallest observed maximum absolute row sum,
breaking ties by proposal order.
(2)

Return the signs only if the resulting bound is strictly below  1877/50 .
(3)

The entry point is the function verified_sign in the
repository https://github.com/suvrit/komlos.
The endpoint fallback is a floating-point implementation of the walk
with the profile of Section 3.3. It uses the normalized
proxy (3.8), the tracked-sum constraints, and the weighted
terminal rule of Section 3.6, but not the proved step
schedule: it tries the longest move allowed by the cube and the
barriers, evaluates the barriers and the spectral potential numerically
at the endpoint of the move, accepts the move if they satisfy the
invariants, and otherwise halves it.
It does not implement lazy warm/cold screening, and
because it has no exact finite fallback, Algorithm 5.1
is a verified partial solver: it can report failure on an admissible
input.
5.2. What the output check proves
For a selected signing, multiplication by a sign is exact, so only the
additions need care. Starting from  \underline{s}_{i}=\overline{s}_{i}=0 , add
each signed entry in round-to-nearest arithmetic and then move the result
one unit in the last place outward with nextafter, toward
-\infty  for the lower endpoint and toward  +\infty  for the upper
endpoint. Under IEEE binary64 arithmetic with gradual underflow, each
update encloses the exact sum of its two represented operands, and
induction gives
\underline{s}_{i}\leq\sum_{j}a_{ij}\varepsilon_{j}\leq\overline{s}_{i}.
Let  C_{-}  be a binary64 number rounded downward from  1877/50 . The
check accepts only if
\max_{i}\max(|\underline{s}_{i}|,|\overline{s}_{i}|)<C_{-}.
This proves the strict bound for the exact sums of the represented
input, and nonfinite inputs and overflow cannot pass. The check needs no
unit-column assumption, no model of BLAS summation error, and no
assumption about the candidate’s trajectory. Conversion to binary64
defines the matrix being certified, so the check does not certify an
unrepresented real matrix.
With eight candidates, proposal generation, evaluation, and this check
cost  O(mn+n)  on acceptance; we prove no uniform acceptance
probability. Replacing step 3 by Algorithm 3.8 would give a
complete algorithm with the same  37.54  guarantee and
O(mn)+\widetilde{O}(n^{4})  worst-case arithmetic cost. This is the
mathematical reason to allow a bounded practical attempt before the
finite fallback, even though the available code has not yet implemented
that exact fallback.
5.3. Outcome on matched inputs
On a suite of 48 matched cases, in which every method receives the same

5.4. Discussion and open problems
The existence bound of Theorem 2.4 and the algorithmic
bound of Theorem 3.1 differ by a factor of more than five, and
the two arguments have little in common: the existence proofs propagate
convex sets and densities backward through the vectors, whereas the walk
moves a fractional signing forward under row barriers and a spectral
potential. The constant  37.54=H_{0}+13R  is the sum of the barrier
height, which protects the retained row sums, and the tracking allowance
for deleted coefficients. We do not know whether either existence
argument can be made algorithmic, or how far the constant of a spectral
walk can be lowered.
The arithmetic bound is quartic because every anchor builds a dense
feasible negative basis and the coupled response, at cost
\widetilde{O}(ns^{2}+s^{3}) , and there are  \widetilde{O}(n)  anchors. Two
partial routes to a faster bound were explored during this work and are
recorded, with their proofs and checks, in the repository. A randomized
variance clock removes the maintenance of the tracked-sum Gram matrix  \mathsf{Q}  and
gives  O(mn)+\widetilde{O}(n^{3})  expected work for row processing alone,
but the direction construction remains quartic, so it isolates a possible
optimization rather than proving a cubic signing theorem. An earlier
version of the construction, with discrepancy below  67 , admits the
bound  O(mn)+\widetilde{O}(m_{66}n^{2}+n^{\omega+1})  if fast spectral and
rank primitives and exact square roots are assumed as black boxes, where
m_{66}  counts the rows with  \ell_{1}  norm above  66  and  \omega  is
the matrix multiplication exponent; these fast primitives are not known
to preserve the exactly orthogonal normalized reconstruction that the
dimension count of Section 3.4.6 requires, so we do not
attach that exponent to Theorem 3.1. Whether the  37.54
construction runs in  \widetilde{O}(n^{\omega+1})  or  \widetilde{O}(n^{3})
operations is open, as is a polynomial bound on the bit length of its
rational states, which Li [12] proves for that
algorithm.
On the practical side, the proved step schedule is far smaller than the
steps that succeed numerically, and the available implementation takes
large endpoint steps that it accepts by numerical tests; the exact finite
construction has not been implemented, so Algorithm 5.1
is a verified partial solver. Installing the exact construction as its
fallback would make it a complete solver with the worst-case guarantee of
Theorem 3.1 and, whenever a proposal is accepted, linear cost.
Every matched trajectory in Appendix F stayed in the

We maintain  \mathsf{Q}  and  \mathsf{u}  so that the gradient

|r-L-at-\tfrac{1}{2}L^{\prime\prime}(0)t^{2}|\leq 1024K^{3}|t|^{3}.
Composition with  (u-1)_{+}^{3} , whose second derivative is 6-Lipschitz,
costs less than  2^{16}K^{3}|t|^{3} , and since all scalar arguments are
below two, replacing  r  by the top eigenvalue costs at most
3\cdot 2^{16}K^{4}t^{4}/\zeta<2^{20}K^{4}t^{4}/\zeta .
∎
Appendix E Finite arithmetic primitives
The construction calls for scalar approximations, spectral approximations,
and a direction with small inner products against a prescribed test list.
We give finite field-operation procedures for all three, so none of the
operations in the main algorithm requires an exact transcendental or
spectral oracle.
E.1. Profile and scalar evaluations
The row constraints use  f^{\prime}  and the proxy uses  f^{\prime\prime} , both rational in
rational  x , so only the weights and the decisions about protected
pairs require values of  f . For  z\in[1,3] , let
t=(z-1)/(z+1)\in[0,1/2] . Then
\log z=2\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{t^{2k+1}}{2k+1}+R_{N},\qquad 0\leq R_{N}\leq\frac{2t^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)(1-t^{2})}.
Thus  O(\log(1/\xi))  rational operations give any absolute accuracy
\xi . We compute the profile at each active coordinate and then form
the rounding energies  E_{i} ; since  \sum_{j}z_{ij}\leq 1 , profile error is not
magnified in  E_{i}/r_{i}^{2} , and the other term  (\sigma d_{i}-H_{0})/r_{i}  is
computed exactly. Positive weight approximants are computed with the
total error required in Section 3.5. Very negative
exponentials can be set to zero after certifying that their weight is
below the allocated error; for the rest, argument reduction and a
positive Taylor series suffice. The required precisions have logarithms
O(\log(2+m_{0}+n))=O(\log(2+n)) , and no logarithm or exponential oracle
is assumed. Tiny scales cause no loss of absolute weight accuracy,
because the matrix is formed with  a_{i\sigma}z_{i}z_{i}^{\mathsf{T}} , not with
unnormalized  r_{i}^{-4}  coefficients.
E.2. An explicit field-operation diagonalization interface
For a symmetric matrix of bounded norm, we maintain an exactly
orthogonal product of rational plane rotations. In a pivot block with
diagonal  a,c  and off-diagonal  b , use  \cos t=(1-z^{2})/(1+z^{2})  and
\sin t=2z/(1+z^{2}) ; the rotated off-diagonal numerator is then
b(1-6z^{2}+z^{4})+2z(1-z^{2})(c-a) , and it has a zero between  0  and
-\tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{sgn}(b(c-a))  (if  a=c , use the endpoint
1/2 ). Bisection reduces the pivot magnitude by at least a factor of
two, and choosing a largest pivot reduces the squared off-diagonal
Frobenius norm  E  by at least  3E/(2s(s-1)) , so after
O(s^{2}\log(s/\xi))  rotations the off-diagonal norm is below  \xi . A
tournament structure updates the largest pivot in  O(s\log s)  work per
rotation, and while termination is not yet reached, the pivot is at
least an inverse-polynomial multiple of  \xi , so each bisection takes
O(\log(s/\xi))  iterations. This proves (C.1) in
\widetilde{O}(s^{3})  arithmetic operations; after scaling its bounded
norm, the same procedure also handles  Q_{\rm neg} .
The exact projector  P  of Section 3.5 is formed by
extracting independent constraint rows with exact elimination;
elimination and projection cost  O(s^{3}) , and the model allows
arbitrarily small nonzero pivots and makes no conditioning assumption.
E.3. Finite deterministic flattening
We implement the flattening step by fixing signs successively in a
conditional-expectation bound. For unit-norm test vectors
a_{1},\ldots,a_{N} , write  c_{ij}=a_{i}^{\mathsf{T}}Te_{j} ; then
\sum_{j}c_{ij}^{2}\leq 1 .
Let  \lambda  be the least positive integer with  \lambda^{2}\geq\kappa .
For uniform independent signs  \varepsilon_{j} ,
\mathbb{E}\sum_{i}\cosh\!\left(\lambda\sum_{j}c_{ij}\varepsilon_{j}\right)\leq Ne^{\lambda^{2}/2}.
We fix the signs greedily, one at a time, using positive relative
approximations to the two conditional expectations. Scalar relative
errors of at most  1/(100k^{2})  make each conditional expectation
accurate within  1/(20k)  and keep the accumulated multiplicative loss
below two, so the resulting signs have  |\sum_{j}c_{ij}\varepsilon_{j}|\leq 3\sqrt{\kappa}
for every  i , since  \log(4N)+\lambda^{2}/2\leq 3\kappa  and  \lambda\geq\sqrt{\kappa} .
Choose by bisection a positive dyadic rational  r_{0}  with
3/4\leq r_{0}^{2}k\leq 1 , and put  h_{0}=r_{0}T\varepsilon ; the Gram bounds on  T  then
give  3/4\leq\|h_{0}\|\leq 1  and the claimed test bounds.
Each conditional expectation is a positive sum of products of
\cosh(\lambda c_{ij})  and a current partial-sum factor, so suffix
products and partial sums reduce the work to  O(Nk)  scalar
evaluations. Their arguments have size at most  2\sqrt{\kappa k} , and
halving, Taylor approximation, and repeated squaring implement each
relative approximant in  O(\log(2Nk))  operations.
Appendix F Experiments
This appendix records the measurements summarized in
Section 5: the matched suite and the outcome of
Algorithm 5.1 on it, the verification of the code, and a
comparison with the earlier walks on the same suite.
F.1. Measured execution
The matched suite has 48 cases: six matrix families, two seeds, and
(n,m)\in\{(640,80),(640,640),(640,2560),(1024,1024)\} .
Every method receives the same matrices and seeds, and the execution
order rotates. Times include construction, terminal work, and output
verification, but not input generation, and each BLAS runtime uses one
thread.
All 48 executions of Algorithm 5.1 returned at the proposal
stage, with median time  0.0077  seconds and discrepancy between  0.257
and  11.006 . The median paired speedup over our earlier quadratic walk with
Gram–Schmidt-walk directions and its original finish, the first row of
Table F.1, was about 630, but the discrepancy of our routine was
lower in only five cases, equal in four, and higher in 39. The speed
advantage therefore concerns the time to reach the specified  37.54
target, not the minimum observed discrepancy; Table F.1
records the complete comparison.
Eighteen further proposal runs at  n=2048  and  4096 , on square and
wide matrices, all passed without fallback, taking  0.008 – 0.231
seconds with discrepancy at most  15.431 . Because they have no matched
timing for the old walk, they are excluded from the speed ratio. Across
all seven variants and the larger checks, 354 executions passed the
strict interval certificate. These single-run timings prove neither an
asymptotic exponent nor an acceptance guarantee outside the tested
cases.
F.2. Verification and reproducibility
The repository https://github.com/suvrit/komlos contains the code, the checksummed
proof-certificate package, and the raw records. Six certificate checkers
were rerun:
exact scalar and budget certificates, protected-charge and randomized
row-processing checks, and response audits covering 300 inertia examples,
480 normalized-response identities, and 464 finite curves.
The current implementation has 16 unit tests, covering terminal
decisions, intervals checked against exact dyadic sums, cleanup,
filtering, the normalized proxy, and explicit fallback and failure
paths. A test on a supplied state exercises the high branch, but every
matched trajectory stayed in the low branch, so the timings do not
measure the new high-branch response.
Raw records, source hashes, timing metadata, and the generated comparison
are in the repository. The largest recorded
tracking residual was  1.74\cdot 10^{-14} , and the largest large-row
residual was  9.75\cdot 10^{-12} . These trajectory residuals are only
numerical diagnostics; the output certificates come from the independent
final interval checks. The exact finite construction has not been
implemented as an exact solver, and its correctness and cost are the
mathematical claims proved above.
