# Chemical-state-conditioned catalyst operations: feasibility and competitive failure

**Status:** a literature-grounded competitive failure for the selected methylbenzene-selectivity target. No new chemical-state tag, selective high-temperature poison, spectroscopic pump, or physical experiment was established. This is not a universal impossibility theorem.

**Source pin:** repository baseline in `work/FO_PIN.json`, main and checkout `1c45f77e32d6fbd72483aed5014061c7bda4f372`; publication namespace FO. This report owns only `work/FO-chemical-state-interface/`.

## Decision

For the concrete question “can changing the trapped methylbenzene population improve propylene relative to ethylene?”, the generic new-operation case fails competitively. An author-reported HSAPO-34 study already combines (i) carbon-position-specific isotope tracing of trapped methylbenzenes and (ii) a physical intervention that homologates the trapped pool, then measures the working MTO product selectivity. The operation is slow and transient, and does not establish an industrial yield/lifetime benefit, but it reaches the decision variable directly. A new temporal isotope code has no demonstrated additional decision-relevant information on this target.

This result narrows the missing capability. A useful new experiment must either answer a different consequential question not already resolved by the HSAPO-34 intervention, or show a matched advantage in selectivity, total olefin yield, catalyst lifetime, acquisition time, or cost. None of those advantages is acquired here.

## HSAPO-34: state manipulation and site-specific tracing already exist

Hwang, Johnson, and Bhan’s 2019 paper concerns **HSAPO-34 with CHA topology**, whose cages retain methylbenzenes. It must remain separate from medium-pore H-ZSM-5. The final paper is *Mechanistic Study of Methylbenzene Dealkylation in Methanol-to-Olefins Catalysis on HSAPO-34*, Journal of Catalysis 369, 86–94, DOI [10.1016/j.jcat.2018.10.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcat.2018.10.022). The journal page was accessible at abstract level; the complete author dissertation chapter corresponding to this work is available from the [University of Minnesota Conservancy](https://conservancy.umn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/a6a07143-baa0-452b-9837-e2276d106eed/content). I read Chapter 3, including its tables and methods.

The source distinguishes a side-chain route from a paring route by which carbons form the olefin. During MTO on HSAPO-34, it switched 13CH3OH to 12CH3OH, quantified whole-molecule isotopologue distributions by GC/MS, separated the recovered methylbenzene mixture by reverse-phase flash chromatography, and used quantitative 13C NMR to discriminate aromatic-ring from aromatic-methyl labels. The catalyst was quenched, dissolved in 4 M HCl, neutralized, filtered, and the organics separated before NMR: this is a destructive post-reaction inventory measurement, not an operando selective tag. The authors infer that tetramethylbenzene predominantly gives ethylene by the side-chain route, while penta- and hexamethylbenzene predominantly give propylene by paring. They explicitly note that multiple pathways could mix isotope contributions; the isotope match is corroborated by the following pool intervention rather than treated as a complete reaction-network proof.

The intervention is unusually pertinent. In the sequential bed protocol, an initial 1.8 ks (30 min) MTO stage at 598 K and 12 kPa MeOH produced a trapped-pool ratio of 0.6. The authors then fed 12 kPa methanol at 423 K or 448 K for 72 ks (20 h), at 100 mol MeOH per mol H+ per ks, to homologate the retained pool; a 0.6 ks (10 min) He purge at 598 K preceded the subsequent MTO run at 598 K. The low-temperature treatment therefore corresponds to a calculated 7,200 mol MeOH per mol H+ feed, and the initial MTO plus conditioning stages to 7,380 mol/mol H+ if the stated space velocity was maintained. The documented initial-MTO, conditioning, and purge sequence totals 74.4 ks (20 h 40 min), excluding ramps, stabilization, and the following turnover-series run. Absolute methanol moles are UNKNOWN because the bed acid-site inventory is not reported for this sequence. The measured trapped-pool ratio (pentamethylbenzene + hexamethylbenzene)/tetramethylbenzene increased from 0.6 to 1.0 and 1.4 for the manipulated conditions. The initial carbon-basis propylene/ethylene selectivity ratio increased from 1.8 to 2.5 and 5.6, respectively: calculated relative changes are 1.39x and 3.11x. With further turnover the manipulated selectivity ratio trends back toward 1.8 as the pool approaches the unmanipulated distribution.

This is an already realized, chemically conditioned perturbation followed by a measured outcome under MTO reaction conditions. It treats the catalyst bed and its retained inventory; it is neither a molecule-selective poison nor a chemically neutral tag. It does not isolate every collateral state change caused by the temperature/history treatment, nor report an industrial process comparison or catalyst-lifetime benefit. The supported conclusion is narrower: a lack of physically accessible chemical-state intervention is false for this HSAPO-34 selectivity target, and the pool-homologation operation is not new in that scope.

The tracing method’s cost and access boundary is also material. It requires a ≥99 atom-% 13C methanol feed, isotope switching, rapid thermal quench, acid dissolution and workup, flash separation, a 14.1 T instrument with a 5 mm cryoprobe, inverse-gated decoupling and 10^4–10^5 NMR scans. At the reported 2 s delay, the repetition-delay contribution alone is at least 5.6–55.6 h per spectrum before acquisition overhead and workup. The source does not report full study cost, isotope-neutrality controls, total instrument hours, or a process-economic comparator. It does report the inventory fractions and treatment response; these are not free information.

## H-ZSM-5: FASPA and isotope switching provide a different, complementary baseline

Liu et al. 2023 studied **H-ZSM-5 at 400 °C (673 K)**, not HSAPO-34. Their ACS Catalysis paper, DOI [10.1021/acscatal.3c00168](https://doi.org/10.1021/acscatal.3c00168), and its 19-page [supporting information](https://acs.figshare.com/articles/journal_contribution/Revealing_Main_Reaction_Paths_to_Olefins_and_Aromatics_in_Methanol-to-Hydrocarbons_over_H-ZSM-5_by_Isotope_Labeling/22424060), were read in full. The experiment used a 100 mg, Si/Al 25 H-ZSM-5 bed, 150–212 μm particles, 1 bar, 20 mL NTP/min helium and 7.6 μmol carbon per methanol pulse, at pulse intervals of 3.75 or 1.25 min. The authors report five 13C-methanol pulses to reach a quasi-steady surface response before switching to 12C-methanol. FASPA reconstructed temporal product responses at 1 s steps over 1–225 s using repeated pulses; these are sequential time points assembled across pulses, not 225 independent product histories from a single transient.

The article’s method is already a strong competitor to a basic residence-time curve: FASPA GC resolves a broad transient product spectrum, and online MS tracks label fragments. But the measurement boundary matters. For aromatics, the reported C7 isotopologue distribution is inferred from m/z 91–99 fragment signals through a least-squares fragmentation model, combining contributions from toluene, xylenes and trimethylbenzenes. For aliphatics, m/z 39–47 contains C3+ olefin/paraffin fragments from multiple molecules; the supporting information says the fitting assumes similar isotope contributions across those components and that further refinement was not possible. The table’s average C3 isotope fractions therefore are not an intact, propylene-specific C3 count histogram or a direct readout of the trapped HCP’s chemical identity.

Under the explicit accounting assumption of one pulse at each of the 225 scanned delays, a single 1–225 s scan uses 225 × 7.6 μmolC = 1.71 mmolC. At 3.75 or 1.25 min pulse spacing, elapsed scan time is about 14.1 or 4.69 h, respectively, before any stabilization pulses. These are derived from the paper’s protocol, not source-reported total campaign times. Separately, the supporting information reports five 13C pre-switch pulses, or 38 μmolC at the stated pulse dose; the total dose for the full isotope-switch series is not given.

The measured C3 isotope estimates illustrate both information and limits. In the first 12C pulse after the switch, at 3.75 min interval the reported fractions are 0.81 ± 0.06 for 12C3, 0.17 ± 0.06 for 12C2 13C, and 0.02 ± 0.05 for 12C 13C2; at 1.25 min they are 0.33 ± 0.06, 0.41 ± 0.07, 0.23 ± 0.06, and 0.03 ± 0.06 for 13C3. These uncertainties are the source’s 95% intervals. The authors interpret the C3+ pattern as faster isotope scrambling than in aromatics, whose labels show slower sequential incorporation. Their fitted C3+ total 13C fraction also varies with pulse interval (about 7 at-% at 3.75 min and 33 at-% at 1.25 min). This already supports a substantial information-acquisition baseline; it does not prove the proposed higher-order ancestry identity or supply chemical-state-conditioned marking.

There is a public raw dataset for the **separate FASPA methods paper**, *Direct Discerning Reaction Pathways in Methanol-to-Hydrocarbons by Transient Operation – FASPA*, DOI [10.1016/j.cej.2022.139696](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cej.2022.139696). Its 4TU.ResearchData record is [DOI 10.4121/21316656.v1](https://doi.org/10.4121/21316656.v1), CC BY 4.0; the repository lists one 695,507,743-byte archive of Excel files and folders. This dataset accompanies the CEJ FASPA paper, not the separate isotope-count ACS Catalysis paper. The latter’s author portal lists no associated dataset; its SI has summary figures/tables and the C3 fitting procedure, but no raw time series or fitting source code. The SI identifies Athena Visual Studio for the C3 estimate; no public code was located.

The separate FASPA method paper by Liutkova et al., *Catalysis Today* 417 (2023), DOI [10.1016/j.cattod.2022.05.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cattod.2022.05.005), gives the repeated-pulse reconstruction protocol explicitly: methanol pulses every 303 s, GC analysis every 432 s, arrays of 100–400 chromatograms assigned to pulse times, and the first 10 pulses excluded because of unstable catalyst response. Their validation requires that later pulses give identical product distributions; temporal resolution is reconstructed from this assumption and check. The archived data improve replayability for that FASPA study, but do not repair the molecule-mixture ambiguity in the separate ACS isotope-count analysis.

## Selective poisoning: real in another reaction, not a high-temperature MTO result

Ezenwa et al. 2026 report co-feeding 2,6-di-tert-butylpyridine (DTBP) to selectively titrate **extracrystalline acid sites** in MFI during toluene methylation. The primary paper, *Polymethylated Aromatics Deactivate Intracrystalline Acid Sites in MFI Zeolites during Low-Temperature Toluene Methylation*, DOI [10.1016/j.jcat.2025.116653](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcat.2025.116653), uses 403 K, 4 kPa toluene, 65–68 kPa DME, <1% conversion, and DTBP partial pressures of roughly 0.005–0.040 kPa. It validates DTBP against MCM-41 and reports external-site poisoning without a significant initial-rate change under those conditions. The intervention separates site-location contributions during deactivation; it does not tag a methylbenzene chemical state.

The paper explicitly contrasts this low-temperature reaction with high-temperature MTH. Its own mechanism is that bulky polymethylbenzene species accumulate and block intracrystalline transport at 403 K, whereas the same types of species may dealkylate under high-temperature MTH. Therefore, this result cannot establish DTBP stability, access selectivity, neutrality, competitive adsorption, or an interpretable response at 673 K in methanol-to-hydrocarbons over H-ZSM-5. Nor does MFI poisoning establish anything in HSAPO-34’s CHA cages. I found no same-regime demonstration of selective internal HCP poisoning or post-formation chemical-state marking.

## Acquisition fields and next gate

For the isotope ancestry proposal, chemical neutrality, internal eligible inventory, marker scrambling, intact-product detector fraction, cross-talk, catalyst covariance and total apparatus cost remain unacquired. FASPA/ACS supplies some concrete measurements—input pulses, mass channels, broad product responses, fragmentation models, fitted C3 averages and confidence intervals—but not a product-specific intact-ion detection fraction or an acquired interference/covariance model for the proposed nonlinear statistic. In HSAPO-34, the trapped-pool composition and 13C position fractions were acquired after destructive workup; they were not acquired as a calibrated time-local tag with measured disturbance. The prior-art operation intentionally changes the pool, so “neutrality” is not the right success criterion for that perturbation; instead its collateral effects and selectivity/yield/lifetime consequences need matched controls.

The 403 K DTBP paper has a measured site-location access boundary and specified co-feed dose in its own regime. Those measurements do not transfer. High-temperature poisoning or optical pumping remains UNKNOWN rather than impossible; no target-specific experiment demonstrated selective engagement with an internal hydrocarbon-pool species while retaining a calibrated, reproducible MTO response.

The decisive remaining gate for the selected catalyst decision is not another isotope waveform or an unqualified selective poison. It is a matched HSAPO-34 comparison of the known 423/448 K pool-homologation protocol against baseline over a useful turnover/lifetime window, reporting absolute conversion, carbon yield, selectivity, deactivation, measured pool composition, full feed/time/catalyst dose and operating cost. A new chemical-state-conditioned measurement should proceed only if that decision leaves a specific ambiguity that the existing intervention and site-specific isotope analysis do not resolve.

**Disposition:** preserve the mathematical temporal-ancestry identity as a scoped feed-entry observable. Stop primary development of a generic chemical-state tag for the already-addressed HSAPO-34 methylbenzene selectivity question. This is a competitive/prior-art failure, not evidence that every chemical-state-conditioned operation is physically impossible or that the existing HSAPO-34 treatment is an industrially superior process.
