# Phase nucleation: kinetic barrier reconstruction

## Result and scope

This note reconstructs one narrow, quantitative model result: two single-spin-flip dynamics can have the same Ising Gibbs equilibrium and the same critical droplet while producing different low-temperature nucleation exponents. This is a model-level warning against inferring a kinetic rate from a static free-energy barrier alone. It does not establish that either rate law describes a particular material.

The main sources are Neves and Schonmann, “Critical droplets and metastability for a Glauber dynamics at very low temperatures,” *Communications in Mathematical Physics* 137 (1991), 209–230, [DOI 10.1007/BF02431878](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02431878), and Park, Rikvold, Buendía, and Novotny, “Low-temperature nucleation in a kinetic Ising model with soft stochastic dynamics,” *Physical Review Letters* 92 (2004), 015701, [DOI 10.1103/PhysRevLett.92.015701](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.92.015701), [arXiv:cond-mat/0307595v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0307595).

Neves–Schonmann and Park et al. use different normalizations for interaction/field energies. Formulas below use the Park convention \(J=1\), \(E=-\sum\sigma_x\sigma_y-H\sum\sigma_x\); compare critical length directly and convert energy and inverse-temperature units before comparing raw barriers.

## Hamiltonian and critical droplet

Take the square-lattice Ising Hamiltonian

\[
E(\sigma)=-J\sum_{\langle x,y\rangle}\sigma_x\sigma_y-H\sum_x\sigma_x,
\qquad J>0,\ H>0,
\]

on a finite periodic square. Start in the all-minus state; plus spins are the stable phase. Assume low temperature (\(\beta\to\infty\)), a fixed finite box large enough to fit the critical droplet without wrapping, and \(0<H<4J\). Exclude the discrete degeneracy values \(2J/H\in\mathbb N\) for the simple unique-critical-size description.

For a plus-spin cluster \(C\) in an all-minus background, the energy difference is

\[
\Delta E(C)=2J|\partial C|-2H|C|,
\]

where \(|\partial C|\) counts nearest-neighbor boundary bonds. The critical side length is

\[
\ell=\left\lfloor\frac{2J}{H}\right\rfloor+1.
\]

The critical saddle is an \(\ell\times(\ell-1)\) rectangle with one added boundary spin (“knob” or protuberance). It has perimeter \(4\ell\) and area \(\ell(\ell-1)+1\), so its energy above the all-minus state is

\[
\Gamma_{\rm hard}=8J\ell-2H(\ell^2-\ell+1).
\]

The droplet-size threshold is sharp in the zero-temperature limit: rectangular droplets with smaller side \(r<2J/H\) shrink with probability tending to one, while those with \(r>2J/H\) grow with probability tending to one, under the stated Ising dynamics. At equality, critical shapes can be degenerate; this note does not use that case.

In the Neves–Schonmann parameterization, the primary theorem passage gives the sufficient torus-side condition \(N>(2/h)^2+2\), which prevents the competing wrap-around ring route in the cited construction. Because their Hamiltonian is rescaled relative to Park et al., apply this numerical size condition only after converting the field/energy units.

For the standard (“hard”) Glauber flip rate,

\[
W_{\rm hard}(\sigma\to\sigma^x)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\Delta E_x)},
\]

the mean metastable lifetime has low-temperature exponential scale \(\exp(\beta\Gamma_{\rm hard})\). The source literature also derives prefactors for the finite-volume setting; those depend on the exact dynamics and time normalization.

## Same equilibrium, different activation exponent

Write the flip energy as \(\Delta E=\Delta E_J+\Delta E_H\), separating bond and field contributions. Park et al. compare the hard rate above with the “soft” rate

\[
W_{\rm soft}=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\Delta E_J)}\,
             \frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\Delta E_H)}.
\]

Both satisfy detailed balance for the same total Hamiltonian because

\[
\frac{W(\Delta E)}{W(-\Delta E)}=\exp(-\beta\Delta E).
\]

Thus both have the same finite-volume Gibbs stationary distribution. Yet the reported low-temperature result for \(0<H<4J\) is

\[
\Gamma_{\rm soft}=\Gamma_{\rm hard}+2H.
\]

The source explains the increment through the extra field-dependent suppression of the final critical-droplet growth flip under the factorized rate. This shows that detailed balance fixes equilibrium weights but leaves kinetic conductances/rates undetermined.

### Worked parameter point

Set \(J=1\), \(H=3/2\). Then \(\ell=2\), and the critical droplet is a 2-by-1 rectangle plus one protuberance. Its static saddle energy is

\[
\Gamma_{\rm hard}=8(2)-2(3/2)(4-2+1)=7.
\]

The soft rate gives \(\Gamma_{\rm soft}=10\). In the paper’s stated prefactor convention, \(A_{\rm hard}=3/8\) and \(A_{\rm soft}=1/8\) for \(1<H<2\), hence

\[
\frac{\mathbb E\tau_{\rm soft}}{\mathbb E\tau_{\rm hard}}
\sim \frac13\exp(3\beta).
\]

The prefactor is tied to the source’s clock convention; the exponent difference is the more robust discriminator. This numerical example is a mathematical-model prediction, not a prediction for an uncalibrated real material.

## Reconstruction and evidence status

The critical geometry and the hard energy barrier follow directly from the Ising Hamiltonian and the critical-side theorem. The formula for the hard barrier is the maximum energy along the minimal nucleation path. The Park et al. kinetic comparison is supported by an analytical one-step path calculation, computer-assisted absorbing-Markov-chain calculations with branching configurations, and kinetic Monte Carlo simulations. Their paper reports that the two dynamics’ fitted parameters agree with those calculations within two standard errors for the simulated cases (square of side 24; 2,000 escape runs, or 6,000 for fields at or above 4 in their units). This is finite numerical corroboration, not an independent proof audit or validation in a material.

The simple analytic calculation is exact for its reduced one-step chain. The full-lattice soft-dynamics relation is treated here as a source-reported model result corroborated by the paper’s absorbing-chain and simulation checks; no independent full-lattice proof or code replay was completed in this task. The hard-dynamics exponential barrier is the established metastability result. No claim of novelty is made.

## Conditional measurement-design lemma

Suppose a controlled simulation or experiment has independent exponential waiting times at inverse temperatures \(\beta_1,\beta_2\), with \(\mathbb E[\tau(\beta)]=A\exp(\beta\Gamma)\) over the selected low-temperature window and a temperature-independent prefactor \(A\). From \(M\) independent runs at each setting, define

\[
\widehat\Gamma=\frac{\log\bar\tau(\beta_2)-\log\bar\tau(\beta_1)}{\Delta\beta},
\qquad \Delta\beta=\beta_2-\beta_1.
\]

Because \(M\bar\tau/\mathbb E\tau\) has a Gamma\((M,1)\) law under this sampling model, the exact variance is

\[
\operatorname{Var}(\widehat\Gamma)=\frac{2\psi_1(M)}{(\Delta\beta)^2},
\]

where \(\psi_1\) is the trigamma function. For large \(M\), this is approximately \(2/[M(\Delta\beta)^2]\). To resolve the hard-versus-soft slope gap \(2H\) at a nominal \(z\)-standard-error separation, the approximate per-temperature replication requirement is

\[
M\ \gtrsim\ \frac{z^2}{2H^2(\Delta\beta)^2}.
\]

This is a conditional sampling calculation, not a new nucleation theorem. It assumes independent exponential waiting times, a valid single-droplet regime, and a prefactor that is effectively constant over the fit window. A real analysis should use several temperatures, test for curvature and non-exponential waiting, and estimate finite-temperature corrections rather than rely on two points.

## Discriminating protocol and falsifiers

For a direct kinetic Monte Carlo check, set \(J=1,H=1.5\), use a fixed periodic size large enough to contain the droplet (the published simulations used \(L=24\)), initialize every run in the metastable all-minus phase, and compare the two rate formulas above. Record first-passage time, transition path, and nucleus morphology for multiple inverse temperatures. Verify independently that both chains sample the same Gibbs distribution at equilibrium. Fit \(\log\mathbb E\tau\) against \(\beta\) in the low-temperature single-droplet window.

The hard-rate prediction has slope 7 and the soft-rate prediction slope 10 in the units above. Failure of either slope after finite-size, clock, and single-droplet checks falsifies that kinetic-rule model at this parameter point. If neither prediction fits, reject both rate laws or the assumed reaction coordinate; do not change the equilibrium Hamiltonian to absorb a kinetic discrepancy.

For a material test, the analogue must first be justified: identify a controllable field conjugate to the order parameter, calibrate effective \(J,H\), measure temperature-dependent nucleation events and nucleus morphologies with time-resolved imaging, and establish that the event is single-droplet rather than multi-droplet or diffusion-limited. A mismatch does not falsify Ising mathematics; it falsifies the mapping of that material and rate law to the model.

## Costs and limits

- **Data:** \(M\) independent metastable preparations per temperature and field setting; total observed exposure is approximately \(M\sum_i\mathbb E\tau(\beta_i)\). The laboratory reset, imaging, and temperature-control costs are not reported by the sources and remain unknown.
- **Brute-force simulation:** with one attempted spin update per site per sweep, cost is proportional to \(L^2\) times the total simulated sweep count. The mean waiting time grows exponentially in \(\beta\Gamma\); rare-event acceleration changes the estimator and must charge interface placement, event counts, and bias/reweighting checks separately. The source reports finite run counts but not wall-clock or joule costs.
- **Energy:** \(J,H\), and \(\Gamma\) are model energy units. These are not electrical energy consumed by a simulation or physical work delivered by a device. No measured energy-per-sample figure is available.
- **Physical transfer:** 2D nearest-neighbor Ising spins, nonconserved single-spin flips, uniform field, low-temperature finite-volume asymptotics, and the specified kinetic rates are all substantive assumptions. Disorder, elastic strain, anisotropic interfaces, transport limitation, or additional internal coordinates can change the critical path and rate.
