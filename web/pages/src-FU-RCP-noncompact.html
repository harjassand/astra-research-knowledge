# Compact support is not fundamental: a criterion for exponentially weighted tempered distributions

Status: internally proved theorem candidate, 2026-10-15. This document is an independently reconstructed extension of the supplied compact-support argument. It is not a claim of historical priority, independent review, formal verification, efficient computation, or practical capability.

Source pin: `origin/pr-94` at commit `df8e6dac844f1832e79cea6cd40d0915307b12bb`; source proof blob `9ee801590e5eff0cf246d6ebc45157f0622a5d16`, path `updates/FT-foundational-capability-v2-20261015/package/workers/mathematical_invention/PROOF.md`.

## 1. Result and the changed hypothesis

Compact support can be removed entirely. The replacement is a distributional condition that says every positive exponential weight of the input is tempered. In particular, the result applies to **every finite real signed measure whose real bilateral Laplace transform is finite everywhere**, with no restriction on the location or number of negative regions in its tails.

Let \(E\) be a real Banach space, let \(C\subsetneq E\) be a closed convex cone with nonempty interior, and let \(e\in\operatorname{int}C\). Pointedness and normality of the cone are not assumed. For \(k\ge1\) and an integer \(N\ge0\), write
\[
 \|h\|_{k,N}:=\max_{|\alpha|\le N}\sup_{y\in\mathbb R^m}
 e^{-k|y|}|\partial^\alpha h(y)|.
 \tag{1}
\]

Assume the distribution \(T:C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^m)\to E\) has the following property:

> **(W)** For every integer \(k\ge1\), there are finite \(A_k>0\) and an integer \(N_k\ge0\) such that
> \[
> \|T(h)\|\le A_k\|h\|_{k,N_k}
> \qquad(h\in C_c^\infty(\mathbb R^m)).
> \tag{2}
> \]

The orders \(N_k\) may depend on \(k\). Increasing the data if necessary, assume \(A_k,N_k\) are nondecreasing. Section 2 gives a canonical extension of \(T\) to constants and exponential functions. Assume \(T(1)=e\), and set
\[
 \mathcal L_T(g)=T_y(e^{g\cdot y}),\qquad g\in\mathbb R^m.
 \tag{3}
\]
Define
\[
 \mathscr E=\left\{p\in C^\infty(\mathbb R^m):
 \int e^{a|x|}|\partial^\alpha p(x)|\,dx<\infty
 \text{ for every }a\ge0\text{ and every }\alpha\right\}.
 \tag{4}
\]

**Theorem.** Under (W) and \(T(1)=e\), the following are equivalent.

1. \(\mathcal L_T(g)\in\operatorname{int}C\) for every \(g\in\mathbb R^m\).
2. There is a scalar probability measure \(\nu\) with \(\int e^{a|z|}\nu(dz)<\infty\) for every \(a\ge0\), such that \(T*\nu\) is a \(C\)-positive distribution.
3. There is a strictly positive probability density \(p\in\mathscr E\) such that \(T*p\) takes values in \(C\).

Moreover, for each \(0<\varepsilon<1\), condition 1 permits a **smooth, radial, strictly log-concave** choice \(p=Z^{-1}e^{-V}\) satisfying
\[
 (1-\varepsilon)p(x)\mathcal L_T(\nabla V(x))
 \preceq_C (T*p)(x)
 \preceq_C(1+\varepsilon)p(x)\mathcal L_T(\nabla V(x))
 \quad(x\in\mathbb R^m).
 \tag{5}
\]
Thus the output is interior at every point, has Bochner integral \(e\), and all its derivatives are norm-integrable against every positive exponential weight.

There is no prescribed growth bound on \(A_k,N_k\), no prescribed lower bound on the positivity margin as \(|g|\to\infty\), and no compact-support or eventual-tail-sign condition. The data may be arbitrarily costly to obtain and the regularizer may be arbitrarily expensive to prepare.

## 2. Meaning of the hypothesis and canonical extension

Put \(\rho_0(y)=\sqrt{1+|y|^2}\). Condition (W) is equivalent to
\[
 e^{a\rho_0}T\in\mathcal S'(\mathbb R^m;E)
 \qquad\text{for every }a>0.
 \tag{6}
\]
Here the multiplication and temperedness initially have their usual distributional meaning.

To prove (W) implies (6), choose an integer \(k>a\). Every derivative of \(e^{a\rho_0}\) is bounded by a constant times \(e^{a\rho_0}\), with the constant depending on the derivative order and \(a\). Applying (2) to \(e^{a\rho_0}h\) bounds \((e^{a\rho_0}T)(h)\) by finitely many unweighted supremum norms of derivatives of \(h\). This extends continuously to Schwartz functions.

Conversely, fix \(k\) and choose \(b>k\). Temperedness of \(U=e^{b\rho_0}T\) supplies finite \(M,N,D\) with
\[
 \|U(\phi)\|\le D\max_{|\alpha|\le N}
 \sup_y(1+|y|)^M|\partial^\alpha\phi(y)|.
\]
Use \(\phi=e^{-b\rho_0}h\). Derivatives of the exponential factor are bounded by constants times \(e^{-b\rho_0}\), and
\((1+|y|)^M e^{-b\rho_0(y)+k|y|}\) is bounded. This yields (2).

Let \(\mathcal X\) consist of smooth functions \(h\) for which some finite \(b\ge0\) satisfies
\[
 \sup_y e^{-b|y|}|\partial^\alpha h(y)|<\infty
 \quad\text{for every multi-index }\alpha.
 \tag{7}
\]
One exponent \(b\) must work for all derivative orders, but the constants may differ. Polynomial factors in derivatives can be absorbed by increasing \(b\).

Choose a smooth cutoff \(\chi\), equal to one on \(B_1\), zero outside \(B_2\), and set \(\chi_R(y)=\chi(y/R)\). If \(h\in\mathcal X\), choose an integer \(k>b\). The product rule shows
\[
 \|\chi_Rh-h\|_{k,N_k}\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{8}
\]
Indeed, the zero-order tail and each cutoff-derivative term are bounded by a constant times a polynomial in \(R^{-1}\) times \(e^{-(k-b)R}\). Therefore
\[
 T(h):=\lim_{R\to\infty}T(\chi_Rh)
 \tag{9}
\]
exists in \(E\), is independent of the cutoff and choice of larger \(k\), and retains any bound (2) for which the cutoff approximation converges in that seminorm. The independence follows by comparing the same compactly supported cutoff approximants using a common larger weight.

In particular, (3) is well-defined. It is continuous, and in fact smooth: for \(g\) in a compact ball, choose \(k>|g|+1\), then differentiate \(e^{g\cdot y}\) in \(g\) in the norm (1). The resulting polynomial factors in \(y\) are absorbed by the exponential gap.

Examples covered by (W) include:

- Every compactly supported distribution: multiply its compact seminorm bound by \(e^{kR}\).
- Every finite signed or bounded-variation \(E\)-valued measure \(\sigma\) with \(\int e^{k|y|}\,d|\sigma|(y)<\infty\) for every \(k\): take \(N_k=0\) and \(A_k=\int e^{k|y|}\,d|\sigma|\).
- Any finite sum \(\sum_{|\alpha|\le N}\partial^\alpha\sigma_\alpha\) of derivatives of such measures.

For a **real signed measure**, saying that \(\int e^{g\cdot y}\,d\sigma(y)\) exists as a finite Lebesgue integral means its positive and negative parts are both integrable. Thus finiteness for all real \(g\) already gives all exponential variation moments: use
\[
 e^{a|y|}\le e^{a\sum_j|y_j|}
 \le\sum_{\eta\in\{-1,1\}^m}e^{a\eta\cdot y}.
 \tag{10}
\]
This assertion does not silently replace conditionally convergent transforms or analytic continuations by genuine finite integrals.

## 3. Convolution, integrability, and the moment identity

For a scalar finite measure \(\nu\), define
\[
 (T*\nu)(\phi)=T_y\!\left(\int\phi(y+z)\,\nu(dz)\right).
 \tag{11}
\]
For \(\phi\in C_c^\infty\), the function in parentheses is smooth with every derivative bounded, so Section 2 applies. Equation (2) with \(k=1\) proves continuity in a finite distribution seminorm. Thus (11) is a well-defined distribution, although neither factor need have compact support.

If \(p\in\mathscr E\), then all exponentially weighted supremum norms of all its derivatives are finite:
\[
 P_{a,n}(p):=\max_{|\alpha|\le n}\sup_z
 e^{a|z|}|\partial^\alpha p(z)|<\infty.
 \tag{12}
\]
One proof applies the local \(W^{m,1}\)-to-supremum estimate on a unit cube to each derivative; the cube weights differ from \(e^{a|z|}\) by a bounded factor. The required derivatives and weighted integrals are all available in (4).

Set \(M(x)=T_y(p(x-y))\). Differentiation in \(x\) is permitted by convergence in (1), using (12) for one further derivative. The triangle inequality gives
\[
 \|\partial^\beta M(x)\|
 \le A_k e^{-k|x|}P_{k,N_k+|\beta|}(p)
 \qquad(k\ge1).
 \tag{13}
\]
Consequently every derivative of \(M\) has all exponentially weighted norm integrals finite.

Fix \(g\) and choose an integer \(k>|g|\). In the completion of \(C_c^\infty\) under \(\|\cdot\|_{k,N_k}\), the function
\(x\mapsto e^{g\cdot x}p(x-\cdot)\) is Bochner integrable because its norm is at most
\(P_{k,N_k}(p)e^{-(k-|g|)|x|}\). Its integral is
\[
 \int e^{g\cdot x}p(x-y)\,dx
 =e^{g\cdot y}\int e^{g\cdot z}p(z)\,dz.
\]
The bounded linear extension of \(T\) can be interchanged with this integral. Hence
\[
 \int e^{g\cdot x}M(x)\,dx
 =\left(\int e^{g\cdot z}p(z)\,dz\right)\mathcal L_T(g),
 \qquad \int M(x)\,dx=e.
 \tag{14}
\]

## 4. Necessity for arbitrary all-exponential scalar noise

Assume condition 2, with \(S=T*\nu\). Choose the cutoffs additionally nonnegative, at most one, radial, and nonincreasing in their radial profile. Let
\[
 h_{R,g}(y)=\int e^{g\cdot(y+z)}\chi_R(y+z)\,\nu(dz),
 \qquad m_\nu(g)=\int e^{g\cdot z}\,\nu(dz).
\]
For an integer \(k>|g|\),
\[
 \|h_{R,g}-m_\nu(g)e^{g\cdot y}\|_{k,N_k}\longrightarrow0.
 \tag{15}
\]
For completeness, for each fixed \(z\), the weighted seminorm of
\(e^{g\cdot y}(\chi_R(y+z)-1)\) tends to zero: outside the growing translated cutoff ball, \(|y|\to\infty\), and the factor \(e^{-(k-|g|)|y|}\) controls every cutoff derivative. Uniformly for \(R\ge1\) this seminorm is bounded by a finite constant depending only on \(g,N_k\) and the cutoff. Multiplication by \(e^{g\cdot z}\) and integration against \(\nu\), followed by dominated convergence, proves (15).

It follows that
\[
 S(e^{g\cdot x}\chi_R(x))\longrightarrow m_\nu(g)\mathcal L_T(g)
 \quad\text{in }E.
 \tag{16}
\]
At \(g=0\), \(Q_R:=S(\chi_R)\to e\). Choose finite \(A\) for which \(Q_A\in\operatorname{int}C\). For \(R\ge2A\),
\[
 e^{g\cdot x}\chi_R(x)-e^{-2A|g|}\chi_A(x)\ge0.
\]
Positivity of \(S\), closedness of \(C\), and (16) give
\[
 m_\nu(g)\mathcal L_T(g)-e^{-2A|g|}Q_A\in C.
 \tag{17}
\]
Since \(m_\nu(g)>0\) and the second term is interior, \(\mathcal L_T(g)\) is interior. This proves \(2\Rightarrow1\). Condition 3 implies condition 2 by (11).

## 5. The slow radial potential

Let \(G:[1,\infty)\to[1,\infty)\) be any finite nondecreasing function. Fix a smooth nondecreasing step \(\theta\), equal to zero on \(( -\infty,0]\) and one on \([1,\infty)\). Define
\[
 S_1=\max\{2,2\log G(2)\},
 \qquad
 S_{n+1}=\max\{S_n+2,2(n+1),2\log G(n+2)\},
 \tag{18}
\]
\[
 q(s)=1+\sum_{n\ge1}\theta(s-S_n),\qquad s\ge0.
 \tag{19}
\]
The sum is locally finite and its transition intervals are disjoint. Consequently
\[
 q\ge1,\quad q'\ge0,\quad q(s)\to\infty,\quad
 q(s)\le1+s/2,
 \quad \sup_s|q^{(j)}(s)|\le\|\theta^{(j)}\|_\infty\ (j\ge1).
 \tag{20}
\]
In addition, with \(C_G=G(1)\),
\[
 G(q(s))\le C_Ge^{s/2}.
 \tag{21}
\]
Before the first threshold, this is immediate. If \(S_n\) is the latest activated threshold, then \(q(s)\le n+1\) and \(G(n+1)\le e^{S_n/2}\le e^{s/2}\), proving (21).

Define
\[
 \rho(z)=\sqrt{1+|z|^2},\quad s(z)=\log\rho(z),\quad
 H(u)=\int_1^u q(\log v)\,dv,\quad f(z)=H(\rho(z)).
 \tag{22}
\]
Then
\[
 \nabla f(z)=q(s(z))z/\rho(z),
\]
\[
 D^2f=q(s)D^2\rho+(q'(s)/\rho)\nabla\rho\otimes\nabla\rho\succ0.
 \tag{23}
\]
The strict inequality follows from \(q>0\) and positive definiteness of
\(D^2\rho=\rho^{-1}I-\rho^{-3}zz^\top\). For every integer \(j\ge2\),
\[
 \|D^j f(z)\|\le C_jq(s(z))\rho(z)^{1-j}.
 \tag{24}
\]
The constants depend only on \(m,j,\theta\), not on the threshold locations. To check (24), use \(D^r\rho=O_r(\rho^{1-r})\); for \(\ell\ge2\), \(H^{(\ell)}(u)\) is \(u^{1-\ell}\) times a fixed linear combination of derivatives \(q',\ldots,q^{(\ell-1)}\). Apply the chain rule and (20).

Also \(f(z)/|z|\to\infty\). The last half of the integral defining \(H(\rho)\) is at least
\((\rho/2)q(\log(\rho/2))\), and its ratio to \(|z|\) tends to infinity.

For \(L\ge2\), put
\[
 V_L(x)=Lf(x/L),\qquad p_L(x)=Z_L^{-1}e^{-V_L(x)}.
 \tag{25}
\]
These are smooth and radial; \(V_L\) is strictly convex and superlinear. Thus \(0<Z_L<\infty\), and \(p_L\) is strictly positive and strictly log-concave. Equations (20), (24) imply that every derivative of \(p_L\) is bounded by \(p_L\) times a fixed power of \(1+\log\rho(x/L)\), up to a finite order-dependent constant. Superlinearity therefore proves \(p_L\in\mathscr E\).

## 6. The noncompact step: a global weighted error estimate

This is the step that replaces the fixed support ball. A local Taylor estimate alone does not control a distribution with unbounded support; the following estimate holds for **every translation \(y\)**.

Fix \(x\), and abbreviate
\[
 z=x/L,\quad \rho=\rho(z),\quad s=\log\rho,\quad
 t=q(s),\quad g=\nabla V_L(x),\quad
 \delta=\frac{q(s)+1}{L\rho}.
 \tag{26}
\]
Then \(|g|\le t\) and \(0<\delta\le2/L\le1\). Let
\[
 F_x(y)=V_L(x-y)-V_L(x)+g\cdot y.
 \tag{27}
\]
Convexity gives \(F_x(y)\ge0\) globally.

The function \(\rho\) is 1-Lipschitz and at least one. For \(0\le u\le1\) and \(r=|y|\),
\[
 (1+r/L)^{-1}\le\frac{\rho(z-uy/L)}{\rho(z)}\le1+r/L,
\quad
 |\log\rho(z-uy/L)-s|\le\log(1+r/L).
 \tag{28}
\]
For example, \(\rho(z)\le\rho(z-uy/L)+r/L\le\rho(z-uy/L)(1+r/L)\); the other direction is identical. Since \(q\) has a fixed Lipschitz constant, (28) implies
\[
 q(\log\rho(z-uy/L))
 \le C(q(s)+1)(1+r/L).
\]
Scaling (24), for \(j\ge2\), yields
\[
 \|D^jV_L(x-uy)\|
 \le C_j\delta(L\rho)^{2-j}(1+r/L)^j
 \le C_j\delta(1+r)^j.
 \tag{29}
\]

Taylor's integral formula and the gradient difference formula now give
\[
 |F_x(y)|\le C\delta(1+r)^4,\qquad
 |\nabla_yF_x(y)|\le C\delta(1+r)^3,
 \tag{30}
\]
while each derivative of order \(j\ge2\) is bounded by \(C_j\delta(1+r)^j\).

For every \(N\ge0\), let
\[
 K_N=\max\{1,N\}\max\{4,N\}.
\]
The preceding estimates, \(F_x\ge0\), \(\delta\le1\), and the chain rule imply
\[
 \max_{|\alpha|\le N}
 |\partial_y^\alpha(e^{-F_x(y)}-1)|
 \le B_N\delta(1+|y|)^{K_N}.
 \tag{31}
\]
For order zero, use \(0\le1-e^{-F_x}\le F_x\). For positive orders, each chain-rule term is \(e^{-F_x}\) times a product of at most \(N\) derivatives of \(F_x\). Its factor \(\delta^j\) is at most \(\delta\), and \(e^{-F_x}\le1\). This proves (31) even when \(y\) is arbitrarily large or crosses the origin.

Consequently there are finite constants \(D_N\), depending only on \(m,N,\theta\), for which
\[
 \left\|e^{g\cdot y}(e^{-F_x(y)}-1)\right\|_{k,N}
 \le D_N\delta(1+t)^N
 \quad\text{whenever }k\ge t+1.
 \tag{32}
\]
Indeed, after the product rule the remaining supremum is bounded by
\(\sup_{r\ge0}(1+r)^{K_N}e^{-r}<\infty\). We can and do choose \(D_N\ge1\) nondecreasing in \(N\).

There is no uncharged tail truncation in (32). The only tail input is the explicitly stated weighted seminorm (2).

## 7. Sufficiency and the choice of thresholds

Assume condition 1. For \(w\in\operatorname{int}C\), put
\[
 d_C(w)=\operatorname{dist}(w,E\setminus C),\qquad
 \mu(t)=\min_{|g|\le t}d_C(\mathcal L_T(g)).
 \tag{33}
\]
The norm continuity of the transform, compactness of the finite-dimensional \(g\)-ball, and openness of the interior give \(\mu(t)>0\). It is nonincreasing.

For \(t\ge1\), set \(k(t)=\lceil t\rceil+1\) and define
\[
 G(t)=\max\left\{1,
 \frac{A_{k(t)}D_{N_{k(t)}}(1+t)^{N_{k(t)}+1}}{\mu(t)}
 \right\}.
 \tag{34}
\]
This is finite and nondecreasing, even if the distribution orders and all constants grow without any prescribed rate. Construct \(q,V_L,p_L\) from Sections 5–6 using this \(G\).

The identity
\[
 p_L(x-y)=p_L(x)e^{g\cdot y}e^{-F_x(y)}
 \tag{35}
\]
is exact. All functions here are in the canonical extension domain from Section 2; their derivatives are bounded by \(e^{t|y|}\) times polynomials. Thus
\[
 \frac{(T*p_L)(x)}{p_L(x)}
 =\mathcal L_T(g)+E_x,
\quad
 E_x=T_y\!\left(e^{g\cdot y}(e^{-F_x(y)}-1)\right).
 \tag{36}
\]
Apply (2) with \(k=k(t)\), then (32), where \(t=q(s)\):
\[
 \|E_x\|
 \le \frac{A_{k(t)}D_{N_{k(t)}}(1+t)^{N_{k(t)}+1}}{L\rho}.
\]
Equations (21), (34) give the global estimate
\[
 \frac{\|E_x\|}{\mu(q(s))}
 \le \frac{G(q(s))}{L e^s}
 \le \frac{C_G}{L}e^{-s/2}.
 \tag{37}
\]
Choose
\[
 L\ge\max\{2,C_G/\varepsilon\}.
 \tag{38}
\]
Then
\[
 \|E_x\|\le\varepsilon\mu(q(s))
 \le\varepsilon d_C(\mathcal L_T(g)).
 \tag{39}
\]
The closed ball of radius \(\varepsilon d_C(w)\) around \(\varepsilon w\) lies in \(C\), by closure and positive homogeneity of the cone. Hence
\(-\varepsilon w\preceq_C E_x\preceq_C\varepsilon w\) for \(w=\mathcal L_T(g)\). This is (5). Its lower bound is interior because \(1-\varepsilon>0\). Section 3 provides normalization and all output integrability assertions. Thus \(1\Rightarrow3\), completing the equivalence.

## 8. A genuinely noncompact signed input with negative mass arbitrarily far out

On the real line let
\[
 \sigma=\delta_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-n^2}
 \bigl(\delta_{n-1/4}+\delta_{n+1/4}-2\delta_n\bigr).
 \tag{40}
\]
This is a finite signed measure with total mass one and all exponential variation moments. It has a negative atom at every positive integer, so no terminal half-line is nonnegative. Its genuine bilateral Laplace transform is
\[
 \mathcal L_\sigma(g)
 =1+2\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-n^2+gn}\bigl(\cosh(g/4)-1\bigr)
 \ge1
 \qquad(g\in\mathbb R).
 \tag{41}
\]
The theorem therefore supplies an even, smooth, strictly log-concave \(p\in\mathscr E\) with \(\sigma*p>0\) everywhere.

The compact truncation \(\sigma|_{(-\infty,n]}\) has rightmost atom \(-2e^{-n^2}\delta_n\), so its Laplace transform is negative for all sufficiently large positive \(g\). Thus straightforward truncation at arbitrarily large integer radii does not preserve the global compact theorem's hypothesis. The global weighted estimate is doing work that a fixed-support argument does not provide.

## 9. Sharp boundary: positivity on the whole finite Laplace domain is insufficient

The full-real-domain condition cannot be replaced by positivity wherever a merely exponentially controlled input has a finite transform.

Fix \(a>0\), choose \(0<c<6/\pi^2\), and define
\[
 \eta=c\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-an}}{n^2}\delta_n,
 \qquad B=\eta(\mathbb R),\qquad
 \tau=(1+B)\delta_0-\eta.
 \tag{42}
\]
Then \(\tau\) is a finite signed measure of total mass one, and
\(\int e^{a|y|}\,d|\tau|(y)<\infty\). Its maximal domain of finite real Laplace integrals is exactly \(( -\infty,a]\). On that entire domain,
\[
 \mathcal L_\tau(g)
 =1+B-c\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{e^{(g-a)n}}{n^2}
 \ge1+B-c\pi^2/6>0.
 \tag{43}
\]
Nevertheless, no scalar probability measure \(\nu\) with all exponential moments can make \(\tau*\nu\) nonnegative. If it did, then, as finite measures,
\[
 (1+B)\nu\ge\eta*\nu.
 \tag{44}
\]
For any \(g>a\), integrate the nonnegative function \(e^{gx}\). The left side has finite integral. Tonelli's theorem makes the right integral
\[
 \left(\int e^{gy}\eta(dy)\right)
 \left(\int e^{gz}\nu(dz)\right)=+\infty,
 \tag{45}
\]
since the first factor diverges and the second is strictly positive and finite. This is a contradiction.

This is **not** a counterexample to the full-real-domain theorem: \(\mathcal L_\tau\) is not finite for \(g>a\). It precisely identifies the boundary that exponential control at only one rate fails to cross. The obstruction already excludes arbitrary probability noise, without imposing smoothness, radiality, or log-concavity.

## 10. Costs, comparison with literature, and claim limits

The existence proof charges two countable families of global input data:

1. Certified weighted seminorm bounds \((A_k,N_k)\) for all integer \(k\ge1\). For a signed measure, certified upper bounds on all \(\int e^{k|y|}\,d|\sigma|\) suffice.
2. Certified strictly positive lower bounds on \(d_C(\mathcal L_T(g))\) over every finite real \(g\)-ball. Nonincreasing bounds at integer radii can replace the exact \(\mu\) in (34).

These data are neither produced by the theorem nor recoverable in general from finitely many samples. All thresholds in (18) depend on them. Nothing bounds their growth, the resulting noise's variance, its preparation or sampling cost, conditioning, deconvolution cost, or sensitivity to unmodelled tails. The theorem does not turn supplied moment/margin access into acquired access.

The normalized output has total mass \(e\) because of a justified Bochner identity, not a formal exchange of noncompact distributions and integrals. The strict interior conclusion uses norm convergence of cutoffs, not weak normalization. The proof uses arbitrary real dimension \(m<\infty\), while the ordered Banach space \(E\) may be infinite-dimensional.

Historical priority remains unresolved. Noncompact support alone is not a new direction: Katkova and Vishnyakova's Theorem 1 already treats scalar continuous inputs on a half-line with all exponential absolute moments, nonnegative initial and eventual regions, and positive real Laplace transform. Their multiplier is Poisson-lattice noise. The present theorem removes the eventual-sign restriction and treats arbitrary cone-valued distributions with the radial strict-log-concavity conclusion. Their paper also credits the earlier positive-convolution work of Diamond and Essén. Direct theorem-level comparison with the latter paper was not completed; its publisher endpoint returned HTTP 403.
