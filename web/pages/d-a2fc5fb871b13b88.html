L206: Lemma 6. (Joint diagonalization by similarity)Let $$$ \left({A}_1,\dots, {A}_d\right) $$$ be a commuting family such that $$$ {A}_k $$$ is diagonalizable for every $$$ k=1,\dots, d $$$. For almost every $$$ \mu \in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$, the following statement holds: If $$$ X $$$ is an invertible matrix such that $$$ {X}^{-1}A\left(\mu \right)X $$$ is diagonal, then $$$ {X}^{-1}{A}_kX $$$ is also diagonal for $$$ k=1,\dots, d $$$.
L207: Proof.The proof of this result is along the lines of the proof of [cite21†9 , Theorem 2.2]; we mainly include it for the sake of completeness. We proceed by induction on $$$ n $$$, with the case $$$ n=1 $$$ being trivial. After reordering the columns of $$$ X $$$, we may assume that its first $$$ {n}_1\ge 1 $$$ columns span the eigenspace ${𝒳}_{1}$ associated with an eigenvalue $$$ {\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right) $$$ of $$$ A\left(\mu \right) $$$. Then
L208: $$$$ {X}^{-1}A\left(\mu \right)X=\left[\begin{array}{ll}{\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right){I}_{n_1}& 0\\ {}0& {A}_{22}\left(\mu \right)\end{array}\right],\kern1em {X}^{-1}{A}_kX=\left[\begin{array}{ll}{A}_{11}^{(k)}& {A}_{12}^{(k)}\\ {}{A}_{21}^{(k)}& {A}_{22}^{(k)}\end{array}\right], $$$$
L209: 
L210: with $$$ {A}_{11}^{(k)}\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n_1\times {n}_1} $$$. As similarity transformations preserve commutativity, the matrix $$$ {X}^{-1}A\left(\mu \right)X $$$ commutes with each $$$ {X}^{-1}{A}_kX $$$. This implies
L211: $$$$ {A}_{12}^{(k)}\left({\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right)I-{A}_{22}\left(\mu \right)\right)=0,\kern1em \left({A}_{22}\left(\mu \right)-{\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right)I\right){A}_{21}^{(k)}=0. $$$$
L212: By construction, $$$ {\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right) $$$ is not an eigenvalue of $$$ {A}_{22}\left(\mu \right) $$$. Hence both $$$ {\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right)I-{A}_{22}\left(\mu \right) $$$ and $$$ {A}_{22}\left(\mu \right)-{\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right)I $$$ are invertible, and the preceding relations imply that $$$ {A}_{12}^{(k)}=0 $$$ and $$$ {A}_{21}^{(k)}=0 $$$. Therefore,
L213: 
L214: $$$$ {X}^{-1}{A}_kX=\left[\begin{array}{ll}{A}_{11}^{(k)}& 0\\ {}0& {A}_{22}^{(k)}\end{array}\right]. $$$$
L215: The matrices $$$ {A}_{11}^{(1)},\dots, {A}_{11}^{(d)} $$$ are diagonalizable and form a commuting family. Since
L216: 
L217: $$$$ {\mu}_1{A}_{11}^{(1)}+\cdots +{\mu}_d{A}_{11}^{(d)}={\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right){I}_{n_1}, $$$$
L218: 
L219: it follows from [cite21†9 , Lemma 2.1] that, for almost every $$$ \mu \in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$, every $$$ {A}_{11}^{(k)} $$$ is a multiple of the identity matrix. In particular, each $$$ {A}_{11}^{(k)} $$$ is diagonal.
L220: The family $$$ {A}_{22}^{(1)},\dots, {A}_{22}^{(d)} $$$ is also commuting and consists of diagonalizable matrices, while $$$ {A}_{22}\left(\mu \right) $$$ is diagonal. The induction hypothesis therefore shows that every $$$ {A}_{22}^{(k)} $$$ is diagonal, which completes the proof.

L223: Theorem 7. (Exact recovery)Let $$$ \left({A}_1,\dots, {A}_d\right) $$$ be SDC. Then the following holds for almost every $$$ \left(\mu, \theta \right)\in {\mathbb{R}}^d\times {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$: If $$$ X $$$ is an invertible matrix such that $$$ {X}^TA\left(\mu \right)X $$$ and $$$ {X}^TA\left(\theta \right)X $$$ are diagonal, then the matrices $$$ {X}^T{A}_kX $$$ are diagonal for $$$ k=1,\dots, d $$$.
L224: Proof.By Lemma cite62†4 , there exists an invertible matrix $$$ P\in {\mathbb{R}}^{n\times n} $$$ such that $$$ \left({B}_1,\dots, {B}_d\right) $$$ with $$$ {B}_k:= {P}^T{A}_kP $$$ is a commuting family. Note that $$$ \ker \left({A}_k\right)=P\ker \left({B}_k\right) $$$ and therefore $$$ \ker \left(A\left(\mu \right)\right)=P\ker \left(B\left(\mu \right)\right) $$$. This relation together with Lemma cite57†2 implies that
L225: $$$$ \ker \left(B\left(\mu \right)\right)=\ker \left({B}_1\right)\cap \cdots \cap \ker \left({B}_d\right) $$$$ (3.1)
L226: 
L227: holds for almost every $$$ \mu \in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$. For the rest of the proof, we fix a $$$ \mu $$$ for which (cite63†3.1 ) holds. For each such $$$ \mu $$$, the argument below establishes the result for almost every $$$ \theta \in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$. The claim for almost every pair $$$ \left(\mu, \theta \right) $$$ then follows from Fubini's theorem.
L228: By the assumptions, $$$ {D}_{\mu}:= {X}^TA\left(\mu \right)X $$$ and $$$ {D}_{\theta}:= {X}^TA\left(\theta \right)X $$$ are diagonal and it thus follows that
L229: 
L230: $$$$ {Y}^TB\left(\mu \right)Y={D}_{\mu },\kern1em {Y}^TB\left(\theta \right)Y={D}_{\theta }, $$$$ (3.2)
L231: 
L232: where $$$ Y:= {P}^{-1}X $$$ is invertible. It remains to show that $$$ {Y}^T{B}_kY $$$ is diagonal for $$$ k=1,\dots, d $$$.
L233: First consider $$$ \ker \left({B}_1\right)\cap \cdots \cap \ker \left({B}_d\right)=\left\{0\right\} $$$. By (cite63†3.1 ), $$$ B\left(\mu \right) $$$ is invertible and therefore $$$ {D}_{\mu } $$$ is invertible, and
L234: 
L235: $$$$ {Y}^{-1}B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}B\left(\theta \right)Y={D}_{\mu}^{-1}{D}_{\theta }. $$$$
L236: 
L237: The family
L238: 
L239: $$$$ \left(B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}{B}_1,\dots, B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}{B}_d\right) $$$$
L240: is commuting because $$$ B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1} $$$ commutes with every $$$ {B}_k $$$. Moreover, $$$ B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1} $$$ and $$$ {B}_k $$$ are symmetric and commute, and hence their product $$$ B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}{B}_k $$$ is symmetric and therefore diagonalizable. Note that $$$ {Y}^{-1}B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}B\left(\theta \right)Y $$$ is diagonal. This allows us to apply Lemma cite64†6 to this family and conclude that
L241:

L243: is diagonal for almost every $$$ \theta $$$. Finally, using (cite65†3.2 ), we obtain
L244: 
L245: $$$$ {Y}^T{B}_kY=\left({Y}^TB\left(\mu \right)Y\right)\left({Y}^{-1}B{\left(\mu \right)}^{-1}{B}_kY\right)={D}_{\mu }{C}_k, $$$$
L246: 
L247: which is diagonal.

L252: by deflation. By (cite63†3.1 ), $𝒦=\mathrm{ker}(B(\mu ))$, and hence $$$ {D}_{\mu } $$$ is singular. After reordering the diagonal entries of $$$ {D}_{\mu } $$$ and the corresponding columns of $$$ Y $$$, we may write
L253:

L256:

L259:

L279: ## 4 Robust Recovery
L280: We now consider the situation when Algorithm cite60†1 is applied to a family $$$ \left({\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_1,\dots, {\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_d\right) $$$ that is not SDC itself but close to an SDC family $$$ \left({A}_1,\dots, {A}_d\right) $$$.
L281: Algorithm cite60†1 then proceeds by forming a pair $$$ \left(\overset{\widetilde }{A}\left(\theta \right),\overset{\widetilde }{A}\left(\mu \right)\right) $$$ of two random linear combinations of $$$ {\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_1,\dots, {\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_d $$$.
L282: We aim at showing a robustness result for Algorithm cite60†1 of the following form: Any invertible $$$ \tilde{X} $$$ such that $$$ {\tilde{X}}^T\overset{\widetilde }{A}\left(\mu \right)\tilde{X} $$$ and $$$ {\tilde{X}}^T\overset{\widetilde }{A}\left(\theta \right)\tilde{X} $$$ are diagonal nearly diagonalizes the whole family $$$ \left({\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_1,\dots, {\overset{\widetilde }{A}}_d\right) $$$.
L283: While the exact recovery result of Theorem cite66†7 applies to general SDC families, our robustness analysis will only consider regular (Definition cite67†1 ) and, in particular, positive definite (Definition cite58†3 ) SDC families. This restriction is due to the fact that nonregular families are not well-behaved under perturbations.
L284: ### 4.1 A Structural Bound
L285: Let $𝒜=({A}_{1},\dots ,{A}_{d})$ be a regular SDC family and let $$$ X $$$ be an invertible matrix such that $$$ {D}_k:= {X}^T{A}_kX $$$ is diagonal. Let $$$ \theta \in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$ be an arbitrary fixed vector such that $$$ A\left(\theta \right) $$$ and, hence, $$$ D\left(\theta \right)={\theta}_1{D}_1+\cdots +{\theta}_d{D}_d $$$ is invertible.
L286: Then the diagonal matrix $$$ {\Lambda}_k={D}_kD{\left(\theta \right)}^{-1} $$$ contains the eigenvalues of the pair $$$ \left({A}_k,A\left(\theta \right)\right) $$$. Collecting all diagonal entries at position $$$ i $$$ into a vector $$$ \left[{\Lambda}_1\left(i,i\right),\dots, {\Lambda}_d\left(i,i\right)\right]\in {\mathbb{R}}^d $$$, we may reorder the columns of $$$ X $$$ so that identical vectors are grouped together. In other words, we may assume that
L287: $$$$ {\Lambda}_k=\left[\begin{array}{lll}{\lambda}_1^{(k)}{I}_{n_1}& & \\ {}& \ddots & \\ {}& & {\lambda}_m^{(k)}{I}_{n_m}\end{array}\right],\kern1em {\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_i\ne {\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_j,\kern1em \forall i\ne j. $$$$ (4.1)
L288: 
L289: with the joint eigenvalue vectors
L290: 
L291: $$$$ {\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_i:= \left[{\lambda}_i^{(1)}\kern0.3em \cdots \kern0.3em {\lambda}_i^{(d)}\right],\kern2em i=1,\dots, m. $$$$
L292: It is simple to see that this grouping does not depend on the particular choice of $$$ \theta $$$.
L293: 
L294: By (cite68†4.1 ), the matrix $$$ \Lambda \left(\mu \right)=D\left(\mu \right)D{\left(\theta \right)}^{-1}={\mu}_1{\Lambda}_1+\cdots +{\mu}_d{\Lambda}_d $$$ takes the following form for almost every $$$ \mu $$$:
L295: $$$$ \Lambda \left(\mu \right)=\left[\begin{array}{lll}{\lambda}_1\left(\mu \right){I}_{n_1}& & \\ {}& \ddots & \\ {}& & {\lambda}_m\left(\mu \right){I}_{n_m}\end{array}\right],\kern0.3em {\lambda}_i\left(\mu \right)\ne {\lambda}_j\left(\mu \right),\kern0.3em \forall i\ne j. $$$$ (4.2)
