# Rare-signal detection under false alarms, censoring, heralding, and calibration

**Verdict.** The strongest defensible result here is a finite-error information-per-resource converse for adaptive detectors, paired with exact finite-sample Poisson policies and a nuisance-overlap impossibility. The converse is a direct stopped-experiment combination of data processing and the KL chain rule; likelihood-ratio testing, sequential probability-ratio testing, on/off conditioning, and the component lemmas are classical. I found no frontier-level novelty. The useful contribution is a single accounting contract that makes it harder to call postselection, censoring, or a free background calibration a detection advantage.

## 1. Stopped-experiment resource bound

Let H ∈ {0,1} be fixed. At step t, a detector chooses an action a_t from the observed history, receives an outcome Y_t, and may stop or censor the record. The action-selection and censoring rules are the same under both hypotheses. Let P_h^a be the law of the recorded outcome under H=h, and let c(a)>0 be the *charged* cost of the action. The cost may bundle exposure, probe energy, reset/preparation, reference calibration, control, readout, and computation; each term must be declared. For an unbundled resource vector c_j(a), the weighted version below can be used.

Define

\[
\kappa_1=\sup_a\frac{D(P_1^a\Vert P_0^a)}{c(a)},\qquad
\kappa_0=\sup_a\frac{D(P_0^a\Vert P_1^a)}{c(a)}.
\]

For any possibly randomized stopping/censoring policy with alarm event (A), if
\(P_0(A)\le\alpha\) and \(P_1(A)\ge1-\beta\), then

\[
\boxed{\;\kappa_1\,\mathbb E_1[C]\;\ge d(1-\beta\Vert\alpha),\qquad
\kappa_0\,\mathbb E_0[C]\;\ge d(1-\alpha\Vert\beta)\;}
\]

where C is the sum of charged action costs through stopping, d(p∥q)=p ln(p/q)+(1−p) ln((1−p)/(1−q)), and the inequalities are meaningful for α+β<1. These are two distinct directional bounds: under a fixed horizon the second one yields the usual ln(1/β) cost as miss probability tends to zero, while under H₁ the first bounds the cost needed to suppress false alarms. Do not exchange the KL directions.

**Proof.** The stopped transcript includes the chosen actions, recorded/censored outcomes, stopping time, and final decision. The action and censoring kernels add no hypothesis information beyond the history; conditional KL chain rule gives

\[
D(\mathsf P_1^{\rm transcript}\Vert\mathsf P_0^{\rm transcript})
=\mathbb E_1\sum_{t\le T}
D(P_1^{a_t}\Vert P_0^{a_t})
\le\kappa_1\mathbb E_1 C.
\]

Data processing from the transcript to the binary event A gives the first bound. Reverse the hypotheses and use the no-alarm event to get the second. A randomized censoring channel can only reduce each conditional KL; if it consumes the same exposure, its cost remains in C. This is an expected-cost result. A deterministic per-run cap C≤B also implies the same lower bounds with B, but a mean-energy promise does not establish a hard spectral-support promise.

For resource vector C_j, if a set of nonnegative shadow prices λ_j satisfies
\(D(P_1^a\Vert P_0^a)\le\sum_j\lambda_j c_j(a)\) for every action, then
\(d(1-\beta\Vert\alpha)\le\sum_j\lambda_j\mathbb E_1 C_j\). This exposes which claimed resource is doing the work and prevents omitting a costly component by changing units.

The bound is a converse, not a generic claim that one action or one SPRT is exactly optimal for every adaptive composite problem. For a single simple i.i.d. pair with equal per-observation cost, Wald and Wolfowitz proved the SPRT's optimality for the two expected sample counts at fixed error constraints. For controlled actions, the relevant design question is information per *total* action cost; Chernoff's sequential-design framework is the classical precedent. The primary sources are [Wald and Wolfowitz (1948)](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177730197) and [Chernoff (1959)](https://doi.org/10.1214/aoms/1177706205).

## 2. Rare prevalence sets the likelihood threshold

Suppose the target is present with prior probability π, and false alarms and misses have losses L_FP,L_FN>0. After data x, the Bayes alarm rule is exactly

\[
\frac{p_1(x)}{p_0(x)}\ge
\frac{(1-\pi)L_{FP}}{\pi L_{FN}}.
\]

Thus prior rarity raises the required log-evidence by approximately ln(1/π). Equivalently, if sensitivity is q=P₁(A) and desired positive predictive value is r, then

\[
P_0(A)=\alpha\le
\frac{\pi q(1-r)}{(1-\pi)r}.
\]

This is a base-rate identity, not an asymptotic approximation. It makes an explicit false-alarm rate essential when “accuracy” or “detection rate” is quoted for a very rare target.

For one fixed experiment and a hard type-I limit (α), the [Neyman–Pearson rule](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsta.1933.0009) alarms above a likelihood-ratio threshold, randomizing at the boundary when observations are discrete. An implementable sequential version accumulates the log-likelihood ratio and stops at calibrated upper/lower boundaries; a finite exposure deadline uses the exact fixed-horizon test below. The likelihood must include the calibration sample and every observed herald/failure flag.

## 3. Exact Poisson detector and charged exposure

For a calibrated, constant background rate b>0, signal increment s>0, and exposure T, the observed count is

\[
N\sim\operatorname{Pois}(bT)\quad(H_0),\qquad
N\sim\operatorname{Pois}((b+s)T)\quad(H_1).
\]

The log-likelihood ratio is N ln(1+s/b)−sT. Hence the exact size-α most-powerful rule is a count threshold: choose k so that the Pois(bT) upper tail above k is at most α and its tail at or above k is at least α. Alarm for N>k, randomizing at N=k with probability

\[
\gamma=\frac{\alpha-P_{bT}(N>k)}{P_{bT}(N=k)}.
\]

Its power is the Pois((b+s)T) upper tail above k plus γ times the probability mass at k. This is the strongest equally informed fixed-T control for the specified simple model; a Gaussian score threshold or unweighted count rule is not a stronger baseline. The code [poisson_np.py](poisson_np.py) computes the randomized threshold and known-background power with the Python standard library; the on/off power sum reports an explicit upper bound on its omitted Poisson tail.

The per-exposure divergences are

\[
I_1=(b+s)\ln(1+s/b)-s,\qquad
I_0=s-b\ln(1+s/b).
\]

Any size-α, miss-β test using this fixed exposure obeys

\[
T I_1\ge d(1-\beta\Vert\alpha),\qquad
T I_0\ge d(1-\alpha\Vert\beta).
\]

For weak signals s/b≪1, both information rates are s²/(2b)+O(s³/b²). At fixed false-alarm tolerance and vanishing miss probability, required exposure therefore grows as approximately 2b ln(1/β)/s², subject to the exact finite-T threshold. The constants above are exact for this model; the weak-signal expansion is not a general detector law.

If event marks x are retained and the two inhomogeneous Poisson intensities are b(x) and b(x)+s(x), the exact log-likelihood is

\[
\log\Lambda=\sum_{j\,\mathrm{observed}}\ln\!\left(1+\frac{s(x_j)}{b(x_j)}\right)-T\int s(x)\,dx.
\]

This likelihood-weighted event rule is the equally informed control. Reducing the data to total count loses information unless s(x)/b(x) is constant. If the detector clips/censors marks, replace P_h by the induced law on the clipped record; its KL cannot exceed that of the uncensored record.

## 4. Unknown background: exact conditional control and an overlap obstruction

If b is unknown but constant and a background-only reference region is available,

\[
X_{on}\sim\operatorname{Pois}(bT_{on}+sT_{on}),\qquad
X_{off}\sim\operatorname{Pois}(bT_{off})\quad(H_1),
\]

with the signal term removed under H₀. Conditional on M=X_on+X_off=m, under the null,

\[
X_{on}\mid M=m\sim\operatorname{Bin}\!\left(m,\frac{T_{on}}{T_{on}+T_{off}}\right),
\]

independently of the unknown b. Under H₁, the conditional success probability is

\[
p_1(b)=\frac{(b+s)T_{on}}{(b+s)T_{on}+bT_{off}}
>p_0=\frac{T_{on}}{T_{on}+T_{off}}.
\]

For each total m, the upper binomial tail, randomized to conditional size α, is Neyman–Pearson optimal for every b within the class of tests whose conditional size is α at each m. It has exact size α uniformly over b. The power is the Poisson mixture of those conditional powers under the alternative. The code includes this policy. Its exposure cost is T_on+T_off; it cannot be compared to a known-b rule with a free calibration input as if both received the same information for free. This conditional method goes back at least to [Przyborowski and Wilenski (1940)](https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/31.3-4.313); later on/off signal work includes [Li and Ma (1983)](https://doi.org/10.1086/161295). Low-count coverage issues are the subject of [Feldman and Cousins (1998)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevD.57.3873).

There is an exact impossibility without calibration. If only the on-count is observed and the background nuisance set is an interval B=[b_−,b_+] of width at least s, then some null rate b₀+s∈B and alternative rate b₀∈B give identical Poisson laws. Any uniformly size-α test then has power at most α at that alternative, so no uniform guarantee with α+β<1 exists. An off-region transfer factor that drifts outside its calibration set creates the same failure; the common-background assumption is part of the theorem, not a cosmetic convenience. With unbounded b, fixed s, and fixed on/off exposure ratio, p₁(b)→p₀, so no nontrivial uniform power above size is available at finite exposure.

## 5. What censoring and heralding do

For any hypothesis-independent censoring map K, D(KP₁∥KP₀)≤D(P₁∥P₀). A detector can stop early and save future exposure, but the stopped-transcript bound charges the exposure already consumed and limits how much error probability its retained history can buy. Deleting failed/censored records from the analysis does not delete their physical exposure.

For a one-attempt herald with success probabilities q_h, success-conditional output law R_h, and a recorded success/failure bit, its full one-attempt information is exactly

\[
D(\mathrm{Ber}(q_1)\Vert\mathrm{Ber}(q_0))
 +q_1D(R_1\Vert R_0).
\]

The first term is lost if the analyst conditions on success and discards the herald rate. If a physical apparatus repeats until a herald succeeds, the expected attempt count under H_h is 1/q_h; at per-attempt cost c, the heralded sample costs c/q_h in expectation. A computational algorithm that samples an already-defined conditional distribution without waiting for physical successes has a different cost contract and must charge its own oracle, representation, bit, and runtime costs.

## 6. Interface with the Astra detection/herald cards

The local Astra cards report useful but different contracts:

- **N18** gives a source-derived, unreviewed expected-input-cost exponent for a known finite-dimensional memoryless channel with a pure null output and exact zero-false-alarm rejection. Its α (energy exponent) and β (separable exponent) are not the type-I/type-II error symbols above. It does not include background calibration or apparatus/drive work. With a pure null, an orthogonal-output click already has zero false alarms; for that binary measurement D(P₁∥P₀)=∞, while D(P₀∥P₁)=−ln p_miss. The finite-KL stopped bound is singular in one direction, and the N18 no-click exponent is the relevant sharper statement. With background leakage/full-support null, exact zero-false-alarm rejection disappears and the ordinary finite-divergence analysis applies.
- **N32** reports a two-use separation under a hard spectral input-energy support ceiling, which is a different promise from expected total cost. Its card marks the explicit matrix witness as reconstructed while the full linked proof packet is absent. Its calibrated perturbation margin retains the pure-null condition; it is not a general dark-count model.
- **N42** is a conditional *computational sampler* for a specified positive rational Gaussian Bargmann kernel. Its polynomial cost in numeric h,E, with no inverse herald probability, is conditional on the perfect-matching FPRAS and exact-zero detection interface; it is not a laboratory herald preparation or later-measurement guarantee.
- **N43/N64** provide source-derived probability-free conditional photon-energy/tail bounds for feasible Gaussian heralds. Such energy bounds can control a conditional simulation cutoff, but they do not improve physical herald success probability or remove N42's positive-kernel/relative-counting premises.

These are card statuses and boundaries, not externally certified theorems. The cards live at [N18](../astra/cards/N18-cost-detection.txt), [N32](../astra/cards/N32-hard-energy-detection.txt), [N42](../astra/cards/N42-rare-gaussian-heralding.txt), [N43](../astra/cards/N43-herald-energy.txt), and [N64](../astra/cards/N64-all-gaussian-herald-tails.txt). ## 7. Falsifiable detector comparison and scope

For an actual sensor, preregister the signal template and nuisance family, estimate/charge calibration using off-region exposure, then compare at matched total cost and type-I level: (i) the exact joint likelihood-ratio rule using all retained marks and calibration counts; (ii) the exact conditional on/off rule; and (iii) the proposed physical/readout detector. Report empirical type-I and miss rates with confidence intervals, exposure, probe/control energy, censored-event count, herald attempts/failures, calibration drift, and computational cost. The model is falsified for deployment if null calibration drifts outside the stated nuisance set or if the signal/background event laws fail the Poisson assumptions. The on/off procedure must retain exact counts and a valid on/off transfer ratio; Gaussianized significance is only a secondary control in the low-count regime.

The reference script's bundled example uses b=3, s=0.5, α=10⁻³; at T=40, the known-background exact test has size 10⁻³ and power 0.100225. With on/off exposures 20+20, the uniform-size conditional rule has power 0.0132566 at b=3, with an omitted-tail bound below 5×10⁻⁶⁶. This illustrates the price of spending half the exposure to eliminate the nuisance in this particular model; it is not a head-to-head result because the known-b run treats calibration as a supplied free input. The script's arithmetic is a finite demonstration, not a proof check or external experiment.

**Novelty/status:** internally derived elementary converse and identifiability construction; policies and ingredients are classic; no external validity or priority claim. The live research gate is a concrete physical detector/channel where the charged resource frontier differs from the classical information-per-cost baseline after all calibration, herald, and preparation costs are included. Until such a model and data exist, this branch is a rigorous accounting framework, not a new physical sensing advantage.
