Abstract

Stochastic models of diffusion are routinely used to study dispersal of populations, including populations of animals, plants, seeds and cells. Advances in imaging and field measurement technologies mean that data are often collected across a range of scales, including count data collected across a series of fixed sampling regions to characterize population-level dispersal, as well as individual trajectory data to examine at the motion of individuals within a diffusive population. In this work we consider a lattice-based random walk model and examine the extent to which model parameters can be determined by collecting count data and/or trajectory data. Our analysis combines agent-based stochastic simulations, mean-field partial differential equation approximations, likelihood-based estimation, identifiability analysis, and model-based prediction. These combined tools reveal that working with count data alone can sometimes lead to challenges involving structural non-identifiability that can be alleviated by collecting trajectory data. Furthermore, these tools allow us to explore how different experimental designs impact inferential precision by comparing how different trajectory data collection protocols affects practical identifiability. Open source implementations of all algorithms used in this work are available on GitHub.

1 Introduction

Random walk models are often used to understand and interpret a range of physical, chemical and biological phenomena. Random walks provide a natural microscopic description of stochastic transport, uncertainty accumulation, and emergence of continuum-scale behaviour. In mathematical finance, random walk models underpin the classical idea that asset prices evolve through unpredictable increments [35], and first-passage formulations of random walk models are especially useful for understanding barrier crossing phenomena in a financial context, such as bankruptcy [50]. In mathematical biology, random walk models are used to describe cell migration, chemotaxis, and dispersal, providing a bridge between individual-level cellular movement mechanisms and population-level outcomes [39, 4, 14, 46]. A similar framework is used in mathematical ecology to model animal movement and foraging behaviour [40, 24], as well as animal-mediated seed dispersal [74, 37]. Random walk models are also used to understand chemical reaction phenomena, providing a clear link between well-mixed stochastic reactions and additional stochasticity introduced when diffusive transport is incorporated along with modelling stochastic reactions [22, 27].

As measurement technologies advance, especially in ecology and cell biology, it is natural for us to seek to understand the extent to which different types of data can be used to characterize random walk processes. This characterization could involve determining model parameters so that solutions of the relevant mathematical model give a plausible explanation of observational data. This process of parameter estimation can be interpreted as providing a means of turning empirical measurements into mechanistic understanding. One of the most fundamental measurements that can be made is to simply count numbers of individuals within a population undergoing some form of stochastic transport process. For example, Figure 1 depicts a schematic snapshot showing the spatial distribution of individuals within a population. In this snapshot it is visually obvious that the number density decreases with  MATH:x xx. One way to quantify this trend is to place three equally-sized, non-overlapping rectangular regions on the population. Count data can track how numbers of individuals per region changes over time which provides a simple way to characterize the stochastic transport phenomena driving the motion of these individuals [55, 68]. The same concept is often used in ecological settings, where field surveys are performed by placing a coarse grid across an area of interest and counting numbers of individuals per grid cell. These grid cells are sometimes called quadrats in the ecology literature [52, 66]. This procedure provides a quantitative picture about the relative numbers of individuals in different spatial regions. In addition to counting individuals, there is also great interest in tagging some subset of individuals within a population undergoing some form of stochastic transport process and studying properties of the trajectories of those tagged individuals. This notion is illustrated schematically in Figure 1 where we have tagged a small number of individuals by colouring them red. Collecting trajectory data would involve tracking the locations of these labelled individuals. In animal ecology it is possible to physically tag individuals within a population [45], while in cell biology it is possible to use fluorescent labels to track locations of individuals within a population [18, 19]. Despite the growing interest in collecting trajectory data, relatively little is known about data quality and data quantity requirements to infer mechanisms from that trajectory data. One potential reason for this is that trajectory data are known to be highly variable [6].

Trajectory data are often reported in the cell biology literature where experimentalists make observations about the motion of individually-tagged cells within a bulk population. Such measurements can be made in classical two-dimensional (2D) culture systems where cells migrate on a plastic dish [9, 69, 64, 65, 76], or similar measurements can be made in a three-dimensional (3D) setting, for example by tagging individual cells within 3D tissues and characterizing the resulting trajectories [18, 19]. Cell migration in the context of biofilm formation can also involve the interpretation of individually-labelled trajectories [5, 75]. In movement ecology, individual animals can be tracked using GPS, telemetry and radar, while the resulting data are interpreted using random walk models to understand dispersal and spatial spread [14, 25, 43]. This is especially clear in insect dispersal studies, where radar has been used to reconstruct butterfly flight paths in the field [41], and in collective animal systems such as bird flocks, where 3D trajectory data reveal diffusion-like motion of individuals within the group [12]. Together, these systems provide a broad range of biological applications in which one can compare the information content of individual trajectories with population-level observations for estimating transport parameters and testing diffusion-based descriptions.

In this work we are concerned with parameter estimation for trajectory data, and in particular we are interested in the concept of identifiability [29, 72, 26, 54]. Identifiability plays a central role in parameter estimation, since it characterizes whether unknown parameters can be determined uniquely from measured outputs. This issue is critical because non-identifiability undermines estimation, interpretation and prediction, yet sometimes this important point can be overlooked. A key standard distinction that we will make use of is made between structural identifiability, which is a property of the mathematical model under idealised infinite, noise free data, and practical identifiability, which is a joint property of a mathematical model combined with a set of data. Put simply, structural identifiability asks whether unique parameter estimates are possible in principle, whereas practical identifiability asks whether parameter estimates are possible in practice. This work deals with both structural and practical identifiability [29, 72, 26, 54].

Standard inference methods for random walk models often rely on simulation-based methods, such as approximate Bayesian computation, which introduce challenges relating to computational requirements and understanding the sufficiency of various summary statistics [7, 32, 60]. Although the use of surrogate deterministic models for inference using count data is relatively well developed [7, 56], comparable techniques for trajectory data are less developed. We address this point by considering a canonical lattice based random walk model describing biased motion. The random walk model involves a carrying capacity parameter that specifies the maximum occupancy of each lattice site. This feature gives us a flexible modeling framework that interpolates between different modeling frameworks without needing to treat those frameworks separately. Using well-controlled simulation data, mean-field partial differential equation (PDE) descriptions of the random walk model [14, 46], and likelihood-based inference and identifiability analysis [44, 60, 54], we explore the extent to which trajectory data complements more standard count data. Specifically we show that some fundamental problems, such as characterising transport parameters using count data associated with an unbiased motility mechanism leads to a challenge involving structural non-identifiability [54, 29]. In contrast, charaterizing transport parameters using trajectory data for biased motility can lead to challenges in practical non-identifiability [54, 29]. As we will demonstrate, one way of dealing with non-identifiability is to work with combined data involving pooling count and trajectory data. A key feature of our approach is that all steps in the parameter estimation, parameter identifiability and model-based prediction workflow are performed using coarse-grained PDE descriptions which means that our workflow is computationally tractable, and avoids relying on a working with repeated stochastic simulations.

A major theme of this work is that the transport characteristics of diffusive populations are often measured by counting individuals, giving rise to count data, as illustrated in Figure 1. A standard approach to model this data is to divide these counts by some unknown maximum carrying-capacity count,  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa. This intermediate step converts counts into a non-dimensional quantity,  MATH:\rho=N/\kappa ρ=N/κ\rho=N/\kappa, so that  MATH:\rho=1 ρ=1\rho=1 corresponds to the maximum packing density. Phenomenological continuum models, such as exponential growth, logistic growth, and ecological competition models, are typically formulated in terms of  MATH:\rho ρ\rho without necessarily asking whether  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa can be estimated from the data [39, 62, 20, 31]. Certainly, in the case of the snapshot shown in Figure 1, the value of  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa is not obvious from recording the count data. In the current work, we take a more fundamental and practical approach by working directly with count data. After formulating our discrete models in terms of counts, we explore the extent to which trajectory data can assist in estimating  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa and other relevant parameters. As we will illustrate, a surprising outcome is that count data can be insensitive to  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa, whereas trajectory data allows us to estimate  MATH:\kappa κ\kappa reasonably precisely.

5 Conclusions and Future Work

In this work we use a combination of stochastic simulations, mean-field PDE approximations and likelihood-based tools to explore the value of individual trajectory data for parameter inference and identifiability analysis. Our work on this problem is motivated by the observation that experimental cell biologists and field ecologists place a very high value on tagging individual cells and animals within larger populations, and making measurements of the trajectories of those tagged individuals. Instead of working with noisy experimental data, we focus our analysis on a reasonably intuitive, lattice-based random walk model of population dispersal. The simulation model involves just three parameters that describe the carrying capacity of lattice sites  MATH:\kappa>0 κ>0\kappa>0, the motility probability per time step for isolated agents  MATH:M>0 M>0M>0, and the drift parameter  MATH:\rho_{x}\in[-1,1] ρx∈[−1,1]\rho_{x}\in[-1,1]. To provide a more rigorous mathematical description of simulation data, we derive a PDE-based continuum description involving three related parameters  MATH:\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top} 𝜽=(K,D,vx)⊤\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top}.

The derivation of the continuum PDEs for the expected number of agents per site  MATH:N NN and the probability density function of tagged agent location  MATH:P PP reveal some interesting features. For example, the PDE for  MATH:N NN is independent of  MATH:K KK when motility is unbiased with  MATH:\rho_{x}=0 ρx=0\rho_{x}=0. This means that  MATH:K KK is structurally non-identifiable from count data, whereas the PDE for  MATH:P PP involves both  MATH:K KK and  MATH:D DD meaning that collecting trajectory data can resolve this non-identifiability [29]. This mathematical observation provides a remarkable illustration of the value in collecting trajectory data since more standard count data is insensitive to  MATH:K KK, whereas the motion of tagged trajectories is sensitive to  MATH:K KK. For biased motility where  MATH:\rho_{x}\neq 0 ρx≠0\rho_{x}\neq 0, the PDE models for both  MATH:N NN and  MATH:P PP depend upon all three parameters  MATH:\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top} 𝜽=(K,D,vx)⊤\boldsymbol{\theta}=(K,D,v_{x})^{\top}. This indicates that if we have access to sufficient data for biased motility, all three parameters can be estimated using either count data or studying the motion of tagged individuals.

The question of practical identifiability can not be determined by examining the structure of the mean-field PDE models alone. Here we use simulation data to explore the extent to which parameter estimation is enhanced by collecting both count and trajectory data. For count data we develop a novel binomial loglikelihood function. This approach differs from previous problems where  MATH:K KK has been pre-estimated and the binomial coefficient in the loglikelihood function is not explicitly evaluated since it acts like a normalisation constant [59, 60, 33]. Here we have a different situation where  MATH:K KK is unknown and so we must explicitly compute the binomial coefficient. For trajectory data we use the new PDE model for  MATH:P PP, which we use here for the first time in an inference and identifiability analysis context. For biased motility, collecting expensive count data leads to reasonable parameter estimates together with reasonably narrow parameter confidence set. In contrast, for unbiased motility, count data enables  MATH:D DD to be estimated quite well, whereas  MATH:K KK is not well-constrained by the curvature of the loglikelihood function. We also demonstrate how to combine count data and trajectory data to give a combined loglikelihood function that performs well in terms of inference, leading to accurate point estimates of the parameters with a reasonably well-constrained parameter confidence set.

One of the motivations for this work is that field ecologists and experimental cell biologists often collect trajectory data alongside or instead of count data. The amount of time and effort that goes into creating trajectory data means that there is scope for building mathematical models and developing a quantitative and objective workflow to examine how different types of tagging impacts how well parameters can be identified. Through a series of simple computations we compare inferential accuracy and precision by tagging different arrangements of agents in the same simulation and comparing the inferential precision by comparing the size and shape of the resulting parameter confidence set. For example, comparing the inferential accuracy and prevision between tagging individuals at the edge of the population compared to tarring individuals well-behind the edge of the population shows that the uncertainty in our parameter estimates is highly sensitive to this kind of experimental design question. This relationship between experimental design and parameter identifiability is important because spending time and money to perform an experiment that leads to poor identifiability may not be a good use of resources. Therefore, developing tools that operate at the interface of stochastic simulations, surrogate continuum PDE models, likelihood based estimation and identifiability analysis are very insightful to explore the implications of different experimental design choices.

There are many ways that the tools presented in this work can be extend. For instance, here we focus on a particular initial condition and boundary conditions in the 2D random walk model which means that the surrogate PDE models simplify to one-dimensional PDEs. This simplification is often relevant to experimental studies [9, 69, 19, 17], however the tools we develop here can also be deployed for different choices of initial conditions where the macroscopic quantities  MATH:N NN and  MATH:P PP depend upon both the horizontal and vertical location in the Cartesian plane [56]. In this case the same likelihood-based estimation, identifiability analysis, and likelihood-based prediction can be undertaken with count data collected by placing a coarser mesh across the simulation domain and solving the full two-dimensional PDEs, Equations (2) and (6) [56]. The rest of the inference-identifiability-prediction workflow remains unchanged.

More substantial generalizations of this work could involve applying different methods to coarse-grain the stochastic simulations to avoid the mean-field assumption [3, 21] or working with lattice-free simulation frameworks [47, 46]. In this first instance we have taken the most fundamental approach of working with a lattice-based simulation framework along with standard mean-field based PDE descriptions to keep the exposition as clear as possible. Our continuum-discrete comparisons in Figure 3 provide confidence that the mean-field PDE descriptions provide reasonably accurate approximations of the random walk model. Our open-access software on GitHub can be used to explore how the continuum-discrete comparison performs under a range of parameter values and our computational explorations (not shown) indicate that the accuracy of the continuum PDEs persists under a wide range of parameter values.

Another avenue for further consideration is to note that here we have taken a very simple view of tagging an individual: we simply record both the first and last position of that agent and use the PDE description of  MATH:P PP to evaluate the associated loglikelihood. This approach can be refined by recording several intermediate locations along the trajectory path. For example, if we take a single trajectory and divide it into two equal durations we can use the PDE description of  MATH:P PP to evaluate a loglikelihood for both durations. While this approach is conceptually straightforward, it requires multiple PDE solves per trajectory whereas the current approach does not.
