Introduction</title><p>The impact on the planet and on society caused by the increasing emission of greenhouse gases to the atmosphere has raised the urgent need for development of technologies for mitigating gas emissions and their capture. Anthropogenic CO<sub>2</sub> emissions can be controlled by implementing efficient processes of gas capture, which may include physical, chemical, and/or biochemical approaches [<xref rid="B1-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">1</xref>,<xref rid="B2-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">2</xref>,<xref rid="B3-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>]. Among them, the use of the metallo hydro-lyase enzyme named carbonic anhydrase (CA, EC 4.2.1.1) has been reported as one of the most promising paths [<xref rid="B4-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">4</xref>]. </p><p>CA catalyzes the reversible reaction that involves the hydration of CO<sub>2</sub> to bicarbonate with concomitant release of protons (Reaction (R1)) and with an unprecedently high reaction rate (kcat ≈ 10<sup>6</sup> s<sup>−1</sup>) [<xref rid="B5-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">5</xref>]. This kinetic potential, as well as the ubiquitous occurrence of CAs in nature [<xref rid="B6-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">6</xref>], entitle this enzyme as a good biocatalyst for industrial applications, such as CO<sub>2</sub> capture from air, post-combustion, biogas, and natural gas streams [<xref rid="B3-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">3</xref>,<xref rid="B7-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">7</xref>]. From this group, the CA from bovine erythrocytes (BCA) has been one of the most studied due to its activity under a wide pH range and commercial availability [<xref rid="B8-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>], among other interesting properties.
<disp-formula id="FD1-ijms-21-02918"><label>(R1)</label><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="mm1"><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">C</mml:mi><mml:msub><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">O</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mo>+</mml:mo><mml:msub><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">H</mml:mi><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">O</mml:mi><mml:mo> </mml:mo><mml:mover><mml:mo>⇔</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">C</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">A</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mover><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">H</mml:mi><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">C</mml:mi><mml:msubsup><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">O</mml:mi><mml:mn>3</mml:mn><mml:mo>−</mml:mo></mml:msubsup><mml:mo>+</mml:mo><mml:msup><mml:mi mathvariant="normal">H</mml:mi><mml:mo>+</mml:mo></mml:msup></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></disp-formula></p><p>The biocatalytic CO<sub>2</sub> capture mediated by CAs has been studied in a wide range of aqueous solvents, most likely in the presence of promoters, as amines (e.g., monoethanolamine-MEA, N-methyldiethanolamine-MDEA) [<xref rid="B10-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>] and salts, such as inorganic carbonates (K<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>, Na<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>) [<xref rid="B11-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>], and ionic liquids under high water activity (a<sub>w</sub>) conditions (e.g., 1-butyl-3-methyl-imidazolium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide-[C<sub>4</sub>mim][Tf<sub>2</sub>N], at a<sub>w</sub>= 0.836) [<xref rid="B13-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">13</xref>]. Therefore, the comprehension of the enzyme performance under different environmental conditions is important for the development of industrially-relevant processes of CO<sub>2</sub> capture.</p><p>Due to its key role in living organisms, the previous structural investigations on BCA have focused on the elucidation of its properties under physiological conditions (pH below 7.5) to address queries related to life sciences [<xref rid="B8-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">8</xref>,<xref rid="B9-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>,<xref rid="B14-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">14</xref>,<xref rid="B15-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>]. Thus, to the best of our knowledge, there is a lack of studies and available data that relate BCA structural properties and its performance under industrially-relevant CO<sub>2</sub> capture conditions (mostly alkaline pH conditions) [<xref rid="B10-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">10</xref>,<xref rid="B11-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B12-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">12</xref>]. </p><p>In this study, the effect of relevant variables, such as pH and enzyme concentration, were assessed during CO<sub>2</sub> capture in aqueous media. Changes on the BCA enzyme conformation, under environmental conditions typical of industrial CO<sub>2</sub> capture, were observed using well established techniques such as circular dichroism (CD) and fluorescence emission. </p></sec><sec sec-type="results" id="sec2-ijms-21-02918"><title>2. Results and Discussion</title><sec id="sec2dot1-ijms-21-02918"><title>2.1. Characterization of Aqueous Media and BCA</title><p>First, the mineral water (MW) used in the work was characterized according to its ionic composition (<xref rid="ijms-21-02918-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>). The electric conductivity and pH (before pH adjustment to specific test conditions) were determined to be 0.472 mS cm<sup>−1</sup> and 8.77 mS cm<sup>−1</sup>, respectively. MW was used instead of deionized water due to the easier controllability of its pH and proximity of the original pH to the desired conditions of the tests. Under an alkaline environment, besides Reaction (R1), Reaction (R2) is also likely to occur, which favors the overall absorption performance [<xref rid="B16-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">16</xref>].
<disp-formula id="FD2-ijms-21-02918"><label>(R2)</label><mml:math xmlns:mml="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" id="mm2"><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:msub><mml:mrow><mml:mi>CO</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mn>2</mml:mn></mml:msub><mml:msup><mml:mrow><mml:mrow><mml:mo>+</mml:mo><mml:mi>OH</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mrow><mml:mo>−</mml:mo></mml:msup><mml:mtext> </mml:mtext><mml:mover><mml:mo>⇔</mml:mo><mml:mrow><mml:mi>CA</mml:mi></mml:mrow></mml:mover><mml:msubsup><mml:mrow><mml:mi>HCO</mml:mi></mml:mrow><mml:mn>3</mml:mn><mml:mo>−</mml:mo></mml:msubsup></mml:mrow></mml:mrow></mml:math></disp-formula></p><p>BCA samples were observed under different conditions and techniques. CD analyses of the enzyme in aqueous solution were carried out to investigate the effect of pH in its thermal stability and overall stability over time. For each pH condition, 38 spectra containing 71 data points each, were acquired from 20 to 94 °C (with a 2 °C interval), and the signals at 208 nm were plotted as a function of temperature (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f001">Figure 1</xref>). It is clear to see that the enzyme melting temperature lowers as the pH becomes more alkaline. The melting temperature (T<sub>m</sub>) of BCA decreased 11 °C in total, from pH 9 to pH 11 (<xref rid="ijms-21-02918-t002" ref-type="table">Table 2</xref>). The increase of the absolute signal at 208 nm suggests that BCA only partially unfolds and may have considerable residual alpha helical content in a “molten globule state”, which is a state found in other forms of carbonic anhydrase [<xref rid="B17-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">17</xref>,<xref rid="B18-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">18</xref>,<xref rid="B19-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">19</xref>].</p><p>In all cases, the T<sub>m</sub> values were found to be below typical temperatures adopted in large-scale stripping columns used in CO<sub>2</sub> absorption plants (&gt;75 °C) [<xref rid="B15-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">15</xref>], which indicates that, in the event of designing an upscaled process, this enzyme must be kept in the absorption column, to operate at temperatures below its T<sub>m</sub>. This can be achieved through protein immobilization in supports, which can then be used in different absorption process operations, including bubble columns [<xref rid="B20-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">20</xref>] and stirred reactors [<xref rid="B21-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">21</xref>,<xref rid="B22-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">22</xref>].</p><p>After heating until 94 °C, the protein was analyzed again at 20 °C, and it is possible to observe that, at pH 9 and pH 11, the changes in the CD spectra caused by heating are maintained and, at pH 10, BCA seems to revert to a CD profile similar to the initial one (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-21-02918">Figure S1</xref>). This correlates to the changes in the secondary structure (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f002">Figure 2</xref>) where it is possible to observe that, under pH 9 and pH 11, the BCA structure changes more significantly due to the decrease in the antiparallel β-sheet portion (in orange), and due to the increase in the α-helix (green) at pH 9. On the other hand, at pH 10, the structure seemed to be less damaged by the temperature ramp, with a slightly lower content of parallel β-sheet than the original protein (PDB reference). Altogether, these observations indicate that the changes in secondary structure are more permanent at pH 9 and pH 11. At pH 10, the denaturation of the enzyme is more reversible.</p><p>Dramatic changes in the BCA secondary structure were not expected in any condition tested in this study. As reported by Gudiksen [<xref rid="B23-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">23</xref>], even at known denaturing conditions, such as in the presence of saturated guanidinium chloride solutions, BCA is not completely unfolded, and tends to form aggregates when in the partially unfolded state.</p><p>The protein stability was also assessed during incubation at 30 °C, under the three pH conditions (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f003">Figure 3</xref>). In this set of data, only slight changes in the proportion of the fractions as well as in CD spectra (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-21-02918">Figure S2</xref>) were observed in all cases.</p><p>The influence of pH on the structure of BCA, in the acidic range, was studied using the fluorescence-anisotropy technique by Bushmarina et al. [<xref rid="B9-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">9</xref>]. The authors observed more prominent structural changes at pH 3.5–4.5, which was correlated with the mobility of tryptophan residues. These authors also concluded that the pH-induced unfolding of this enzyme leads to the formation of two partially-folded intermediates, which is a different behavior as compared to guanidinium chloride-induced unfolding.</p><p>A fluorescence analysis was also performed in the present work. In this case, the technique supported the observation of the protein in the presence and absence of a buffered environment, which revealed no significant changes, from the point of view of exposure of the aromatic residue of tryptophan (<xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f004">Figure 4</xref>). Only a subtle band shift (&lt;1 nm) to lower emission wavelengths (blue shift) was observed in the presence of the salt. Although the spectral changes are not significant to support the existence of protein structural changes caused by the presence of the buffer, the blue shift would be compatible with a decrease of the overall exposure of tryptophans to the external media and, thus, to a higher protein folding.</p></sec><sec id="sec2dot2-ijms-21-02918"><title>2.2. CO<sub>2</sub> Absorption Tests</title><p>The influence of pH on the BCA performance for CO<sub>2</sub> absorption was kinetically investigated through online monitoring of pressure decay as a result of the gas (CO<sub>2</sub>) capture in the aqueous phase (<xref ref-type="app" rid="app1-ijms-21-02918">Figure S3</xref>). As shown in <xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f005">Figure 5</xref>, the addition of BCA to each test condition had a low influence on the overall sorption coefficient (S<sub>CO2</sub>), determined at equilibrium. However, under unbuffered conditions, the presence of the enzyme speeds up the initial capture rate (r<sub>CO2</sub>) by 76% (at pH 10). No positive effect was observed at pH 9 as well as at pH 11. The best pH condition observed in the present study was within the range reported by Wojtasik et al. [<xref rid="B24-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>] (pH 10–10.3) during CO<sub>2</sub> capture in a 30 wt% methyldiethanolamine (MDEA) solution. When considering a buffered environment, the observation was that S<sub>CO2</sub> was increased by 35% in the presence of the enzyme, as compared to an unbuffered condition at the same initial pH. The enzyme, however, did not exert a positive influence for the buffered condition, as compared to the use of buffer in the absence of BCA (blank assay). This may be related to the possibility of CA action toward CO<sub>2</sub> hydration products (reversibility of the reaction, as shown in Reactions (R1) and (R2)), so that the overall effect might have been neutralized in this condition.</p><p>The S<sub>CO2</sub> and r<sub>CO2</sub> values reported in this case can be increased in future tests by the adoption of the following conditions: (1) addition of an absorption promoter (e.g., amine, inorganic carbonate), (2) higher partial pressure in the absorption cell since it is well known that pressure exerts a strong effect in gas solubility in liquid phases [<xref rid="B25-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">25</xref>]. Additionally, r<sub>CO2</sub> may be improved by utilization of a mixing device in the absorption cell, as previously stated during the comparison of fixed and rotating packed beds for biocatalytic CO<sub>2</sub> capture [<xref rid="B24-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">24</xref>].</p><p>After the absorption tests at pH 9, 10, and 11, the pH and electric conductivity of solution were measured. The results shown in <xref rid="ijms-21-02918-t003" ref-type="table">Table 3</xref> indicate that, at pH 9, there was an increase in the conductivity, characterized by the incorporation of the CO<sub>2</sub> as HCO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> in the liquid phase, whereas, at a more alkaline pH (pH 11), the conductivity decreased, which suggests precipitation of salts, most likely in the form of carbonates (<xref rid="ijms-21-02918-t001" ref-type="table">Table 1</xref>) [<xref rid="B26-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">26</xref>]. The different behaviors observed by the addition of BCA at pH 10 and pH 11 are supported by the conclusions given by Li et al. [<xref rid="B27-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">27</xref>] regarding the effects of CA on the calcium carbonate salts morphology (e.g., formation of calcite or vaterite crystals).</p><p>When assessing the influence of a buffered environment (at pH 10), there was an increase in the S<sub>CO2</sub> values, but no advantage of using BCA under the view of kinetic performance, as compared to the biocatalytic, unbuffered condition. According to our preliminary analyses, the carbonate-bicarbonate buffer was shown to be a good choice for this reaction, as compared to other buffers, such as glycine-NaOH (50 mM, pH 10), which yielded a S<sub>CO2</sub> of 2.3 g<sub>CO2</sub> kg<sup>−1</sup><sub>solution</sub> and an r<sub>CO2</sub> of 40.1 nmol<sub>CO2</sub> s<sup>−1</sup>.</p><p>Regarding the effect of enzyme concentration in the absorption performance, it is possible to observe from <xref ref-type="fig" rid="ijms-21-02918-f006">Figure 6</xref> that low biocatalyst concentrations are sufficient to favour the absorption, and higher concentrations do not seem to improve the reaction yield. A similar pattern was observed during biocatalytic CO<sub>2</sub> absorption in other alkaline conditions with a linear performance increase at low enzyme concentrations (&lt;0.5 g L<sup>−1</sup>) and subsequent asymptotic deviation at higher concentrations [<xref rid="B11-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">11</xref>,<xref rid="B28-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">28</xref>,<xref rid="B29-ijms-21-02918" ref-type="bibr">29</xref>].
