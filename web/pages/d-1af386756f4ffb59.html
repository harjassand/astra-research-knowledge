 Abstract 
 
 Many problems in modern scientific computing
are challenging because of a curse of dimension ,
where their mathematical formulation involves objects whose
dimension is exponential in the nominal
“size” of the problem.
Tensor networks can provide a compact representation
for exponentially large vectors and matrices
that arise in applications, but these representations
do not always lead to reliable algorithms.
This paper develops and analyzes techniques
for randomized dimension reduction of tensor
network data. These techniques support a suite of efficient
algorithms for provably solving exponential-scale
linear algebra problems, including trace estimation
and eigenvalue approximation.
The paper includes several stylized illustrations
from quantum many-body physics with ambient dimension
up to . 
 
 
 
 1 Introduction 

 
 At the frontier of scientific computing, we encounter problems
that suffer from a curse of dimension , where direct methods would require us to instantiate objects that scale exponentially with the nominal “size” of the problem [ 28 ] .
For instance, 
 
 1. 
 
 In high-dimensional function approximation, we may need to discretize a function .
Using points per spatial dimension, the discretization
requires pieces of data. 
 
 2. 
 
 In probability theory, the joint probability distribution of random experiments, each with possible outcomes, is described by parameters. 
 
 3. 
 
 In quantum many-body physics, we represent the joint quantum state of interacting subsystems, each with local dimension , by means of a vector in a Hilbert space with dimension . 
 
 
 When is large, any algorithm that manipulates
 parameters is doomed.
To address this challenge, we must design algorithms
that work with compact representations
of the problem data, the problem solution, and the intermediate objects
that arise during computations. 
 
 
 For each of the three examples above,
we can sometimes circumvent the exponential scaling
by employing tensor networks 
 [ 82 , 11 , 98 , 1 ] to represent information. A tensor network models an exponentially large vector or matrix as the contraction of several tensors of small order; see Section   1.2 for background. Tensor networks have become essential tools for processing high-dimensional data in
applications spanning quantum physics [ 103 , 120 , 34 , 105 , 86 ] , quantum chemistry [ 81 , 20 , 130 , 131 ] , high-dimensional function approximation [ 60 , 38 , 112 , 99 ] ,
machine learning
 [ 80 , 107 , 57 , 127 ] ,
and fluid dynamics [ 61 , 87 , 51 , 46 ] . 
 
 
 Although tensor networks provide a compact way of representing exponential-scale data, the cost of performing computations on a tensor network may still be prohibitive. To avoid this dilemma, researchers have proposed methods
for randomized dimension reduction 
of tensor networks  [ 3 , 63 , 14 , 101 , 18 , 88 , 74 , 110 , 17 , 4 , 19 ] .
This approach applies a randomized embedding
to the high-dimensional tensor network data to produce a low-dimensional summary.
After the embedding, we can process the summary data with
traditional algorithms
to solve a range of computational problems.
This strategy has been very fruitful. Nevertheless, the existing randomized algorithms for tensor networks
often lack end-to-end guarantees, and several popular methods can
fail dramatically  [ 75 , 77 , 18 ] . 
 
 
 This paper advances the theory and practice of
randomized dimension reduction for tensor networks.
Our approach involves a type of random tensor network,
called a random matrix product state (),
that is easily implemented with standard libraries for
tensor network computations.
Rakhshan & Rabusseau [ 96 , 97 ] 
introduced dimension reduction maps
and showed that they preserve the geometry of a finite point set.
We prove through a stronger argument that dimension reduction preserves the geometry of an entire subspace of points, thereby unlocking the application to linear algebra. 
 
 
 Using dimension reduction, we develop a suite of efficient algorithms that provably
solve exponential-scale linear algebra problems. For matrix data with tensor network structure,
we obtain powerful new techniques for eigenvalue approximation,
trace estimation, and low-rank matrix approximation.
As applications, we demonstrate that these algorithms
can help us solve two problems from quantum many-body physics:
estimating the partition function for a system at finite temperature
and estimating the von Neumann entropy for an open quantum system.

 2 Related work 

 
 Dimension reduction for tensor networks is a young but expanding topic of interest.
This section compares several approaches that have been proposed in the literature. 
 
 
 2.1 Random Kronecker vectors and Khatri–Rao dimension reduction 

 
 The simplest construction for a structured high-dimensional random vector is the random Kronecker vector , obtained by taking the tensor product of independent random vectors , each drawn from an isotropic distribution, such as the standard Gaussian distribution.
To obtain a test matrix , we can stack random Kronecker vectors columnwise.
The resulting object is called a Khatri–Rao test matrix.
Random Kronecker vectors and Khatri–Rao test matrices are widely studied and deployed in algorithms [ 18 , 17 , 4 , 36 , 101 , 15 , 16 , 109 , 21 , 2 , 59 , 75 ] .
They are closely connected to the present work because an with bond dimension is a Gaussian–Kronecker vector,
and an test matrix with is a Khatri–Rao test matrix. 
 
 
 Unfortunately, while random Kronecker vectors are easy to generate and manipulate, they suffer from a severe computational limitation known as overwhelming orthogonality [ 77 , 18 ] .
For each tensor-structured vector , the squared inner product is exponentially small 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 except with exponentially small probability.
As a consequence, randomized algorithms based on Kronecker vectors sometimes require resources that are exponential in the tensor order . This issue afflicts algorithms for trace estimation [ 75 , 77 ] , dimension reduction [ 2 ] , and other problems [ 77 ] . 
 
 
 Random Kronecker vectors sometimes perform well in practice despite their exponentially bad worst-case behavior [ 18 , 17 , 101 , 4 ] .
However, as the examples in this paper demonstrate, the deficiencies of random Kronecker vectors manifest in many applications; see the numerical experiments documented in Figures   1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , and  5 .
This paper advocates for random with bond dimension as a more powerful alternative to random Kronecker vectors. 
 
 
 
 2.2 : History and existing analysis 

 
 Random matrix products have been used for decades in computational quantum many-body physics to initialize iterative algorithms like DMRG.
The statistical properties of random matrix product states were first systematically studied by researchers in theoretical quantum information and quantum many-body physics [ 39 , 40 , 47 , 66 , 64 , 65 ] .
These researchers study different random constructions that have unit norm and, similar to us, provide evidence that
a random matrix product state “behaves randomly”

Early works [ 39 , 40 ] showed that is sufficient for random matrix product states to achieve an equidistribution property known as quantum typicality ; the recent paper [ 64 ] shows that is sufficient for the inverse participation ratio to be close to the value for a uniformly random unit vector.

 3.1 Design methodology: Black-box access by matrix– products 

 
 Many standard linear algebra algorithms [ 45 , chs. 10–11] interact with a matrix only through the matrix–vector product operation .
The matrix–vector paradigm is powerful because it allows us to design algorithms that operate efficiently whenever we have a mechanism for computing quickly. 
 
 
 Throughout this section, we consider an exponentially large matrix that is compactly represented and supports the efficient computation of matrix– products : 
 
 
 
 Matrix– access model. 
We interact with the matrix only through a subroutine (“oracle”) whose input is an of bond dimension at most and whose output is , represented as an of bond dimension at most .
