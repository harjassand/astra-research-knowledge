# Sufficient predictive compression under finite samples

**Status:** Literature-grounded reconstruction plus two elementary proofs. This is a scoped result, not a claim of novelty or a general method for learning representations under shift.

## Interface and answer

The learner observes a finite sample from a source process, stores a summary, and later predicts future observations. The useful guarantee depends on fixing the source family and the prediction target. For an i.i.d. categorical source, exact sufficient compression and a finite-sample prediction bound are immediate. For arbitrary processes, no representation-independent compression guarantee follows from sufficiency language alone. Under an unknown target representation alignment, even raw source retention and unlimited unlabeled target data cannot guarantee better than chance.

The important distinction is between (i) a statistic that is sufficient for a specified source model, (ii) a predictive state that is sufficient for future tests of a specified dynamical system, and (iii) a learned representation intended to transfer across domains. Only the first two are exact statements about one fixed model. Neither implies the third.

## Theorem: finite-sample predictive compression for a categorical source

Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be i.i.d. on \([K]=\{1,\ldots,K\}\) with unknown probability vector \(p\), and let future observations be drawn from that same \(p\). Define the counts \(N_i=\sum_{t=1}^n\mathbf 1\{X_t=i\}\) and \(T(X^n)=(N_1,\ldots,N_K)\).

1. **Exact predictive sufficiency.** For every prior on \(p\), the posterior distribution of \(p\), and therefore the posterior predictive distribution of every future block \(X_{n+1:n+H}\), depends on the observed sample only through \(T\). Indeed,
   \[
   p(x^n\mid p)=\prod_{i=1}^K p_i^{N_i(x^n)},
   \]
   so the likelihood factorization contains no dependence on the order of the observations. Under a Dirichlet prior \(\operatorname{Dir}(\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_K)\), the one-step posterior predictive is exactly
   \[
   q_i=\Pr(X_{n+1}=i\mid X^n)=\frac{N_i+\alpha_i}{n+\alpha_0},\qquad \alpha_0=\sum_i\alpha_i.
   \]
   The maximum-likelihood one-step predictor \(\widehat p_i=N_i/n\) also depends only on \(T\).

2. **Storage.** With \(n,K\) known to the decoder, there are exactly \(\binom{n+K-1}{K-1}\) possible count vectors. An exact enumerative code therefore needs \(\lceil\log_2\binom{n+K-1}{K-1}\rceil\) bits in the worst case. A simpler fixed-width representation uses \(K\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil\) bits. The raw ordered sample uses \(n\lceil\log_2 K\rceil\) bits. For fixed \(K\), the count representation is \((K-1)\log_2 n+O(K)\) bits, versus \(n\log_2 K\) raw bits. For example, with \(K=2\), the exact count has \(n+1\) states and takes \(\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil\) bits.

3. **Finite-sample prediction quality.** For \(n\ge1\), the empirical predictor obeys
   \[
   \mathbb E_p\big[\operatorname{TV}(\widehat p,p)\big]
   \le \frac12\sqrt{\frac{K-1}{n}}.
   \]
   Proof: \(\mathbb E|\widehat p_i-p_i|\le\sqrt{p_i(1-p_i)/n}\). Summing and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives
   \(\mathbb E\operatorname{TV}\le\tfrac12\sqrt{K(1-\sum_i p_i^2)/n}\le\tfrac12\sqrt{(K-1)/n}\), since \(\sum_i p_i^2\ge1/K\). Thus \(n\ge(K-1)/(4\varepsilon^2)\) suffices for expected one-step total-variation error at most \(\varepsilon\). For an \(H\)-symbol block predicted as \(\widehat p^{\otimes H}\), the coupling inequality gives expected TV at most \(H/2\sqrt{(K-1)/n}\). These are expectation bounds, not high-probability claims.

4. **Charged acquisition and runtime.** The guarantee still uses all \(n\) acquired observations: acquisition/sample cost is \(n\), not \(K\log n\). A streaming implementation uses \(O(K\log n)\) counter bits and \(O(1)\) work per observation; initial processing is \(O(n)\). Emitting the full next-symbol distribution takes \(O(K)\) arithmetic operations. Counts and the rational MLE probabilities are exact; no floating-point precision is hidden. If the prior parameters \(\alpha_i\) are not fixed and supplied, their representation must also be charged. If \(K\) is large relative to \(n\), a sparse or enumerative code should be compared against raw storage; the fixed-width count vector is not always smaller.

This theorem is a storage and prediction result for the fixed i.i.d. family. It does not reduce sample acquisition, and it does not identify a source-to-target representation map.

## General sufficiency and its limits

For a dominated parametric family, a statistic \(T(D)\) is sufficient for parameter \(\theta\) when the sample likelihood factors as
\[
  p_\theta(D)=g_\theta(T(D))h(D).
\]
For any prior, this makes the posterior for \(\theta\), and hence predictions whose conditional law is determined by \(\theta\), a function of \(T(D)\). This is model-relative sufficiency. It gives no universal bit bound: \(T\) can have as many values as the data, and continuous-valued sufficient statistics need a specified precision or a quantization-error budget. A claim of “\(d\) sufficient statistics” does not by itself mean “\(d\) machine words.”

There is also a sharp family-wide limit. Suppose a summary of length-\(n\) histories over a \(K\)-symbol alphabet must preserve the exact next-symbol prediction for **every** possible process on that alphabet. If it merged distinct histories \(h\ne h'\), a process with memory \(n\) could assign different next-symbol laws after \(h\) and \(h'\), contradicting sufficiency. Thus such a process-independent exact summary must be injective on the \(K^n\) histories and needs at least \(n\log_2 K\) bits. Compression is possible when a fixed process/model makes histories predictively equivalent; there is no nontrivial exact universal compression over all processes.

## Predictive state: exact representation versus acquired model

For a fixed process, the full conditional law of the future given a history is itself a predictive sufficient statistic. Histories with the same conditional future law may be identified; histories with different future laws cannot be identified if exact predictions are required. Causal states (the \(\epsilon\)-machine construction) formalize a minimal representation of this kind, but the number of states need not be finite.

For controlled systems, a predictive state representation (PSR) uses probabilities of a selected set of action-observation tests as state. Littman, Sutton, and Singh define a PSR as a vector of test predictions sufficient to calculate predictions for every test. Their Theorem 1 states that any environment representable by a finite POMDP has a linear PSR with no more tests than states in the minimal POMDP. The theorem is about representation of a supplied system. The same paper says that learning the PSR and update functions in the controlled case remained an open question; its construction is from a known POMDP and has polynomial time in that supplied model.

Resource accounting for a dense linear PSR with \(r\) core tests, \(|A|\) actions, and \(|O|\) observations:

- The current state has \(r\) probabilities. At a chosen \(b\)-bit precision it costs \(rb\) bits, with a further error analysis needed to turn quantization into a prediction guarantee.
- A dense update model can contain \(O(r^2|A||O|)\) matrix entries; storing the update rules and test identities is additional to the current state. A dense matrix update costs \(O(r^2)\) arithmetic operations per action-observation pair. Structure can lower these costs.
- Exact real probabilities do not have a finite bit cost without a rational/algebraic encoding assumption. Even rational Bayesian state updates can require precision growing with history length.
- Sample complexity for acquiring the tests and update operators is **not** supplied by the finite-POMDP representation theorem. It needs an explicit observation/behavior policy, coverage, identifiability, conditioning, and a finite-precision error target.

So predictive state is the right object when the future process is the task. A compact predictive state is still not a finite-sample acquisition theorem unless the test set, identifiability, coverage, precision, and update computation are charged and bounded.

## Strict no-go under unknown representation alignment

Consider a binary latent variable \(U\sim\operatorname{Unif}\{0,1\}\). In the source, the observed representation and label are \(Z_s=U\), \(Y=U\). In the target, the label remains \(Y=U\), but the observed representation is \(Z_t=U\oplus B\), where the target alignment bit \(B\in\{0,1\}\) is unknown. The learner receives any number of labeled source samples and any number of unlabeled target samples.

The complete observable training-data distribution is identical for \(B=0\) and \(B=1\): source pairs always satisfy \(Y=Z_s\), and target \(Z_t\) is uniform in both worlds. Yet the target rules are opposite: \(Y=Z_t\) for \(B=0\), and \(Y=1-Z_t\) for \(B=1\). For any classifier \(g\) based on the observations, its target risks satisfy \(R_0(g)+R_1(g)=1\). Because the data law is the same, the same inequality holds after averaging over any randomized learner and its training data. Therefore at least one of the two worlds has target error at least \(1/2\). This lower bound is sharp for binary classification. It holds with raw source retention and even infinitely many unlabeled target samples.

The missing quantity is the one-bit target alignment \(B\). More unlabeled target observations do not reveal it because the target marginal is invariant under the flip. A target label paired with its representation, or an aligned cross-domain observation, supplies information that breaks the ambiguity. This result is a direct construction; it is distinct from, and consistent with, domain-adaptation impossibility results in the literature.

The obstruction also explains why exact sufficiency does not transfer automatically: a source statistic can preserve every prediction made under its source model while carrying no information about which target coordinate system is in use. Raw retention is a strong control here and still fails, so compression is not the cause of the impossibility.

## Comparison

| Representation | What is exactly preserved | Storage/precision | Samples and runtime | Shift guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw history | Everything in the observed source history | \(n\log_2K\) bits for finite symbols; continuous values need their own precision | Reads/retains all \(n\); any model-building work is extra | No transfer guarantee if alignment or target labels are unconstrained |
| General sufficient statistic | Posterior/predictions for a named likelihood family and named target | Depends on range and precision of the statistic; no universal bit bound | Usually computed from the same \(n\) samples; cost depends on the statistic | Source-model sufficiency only |
| Categorical histogram | Entire posterior predictive law for every future block under one fixed i.i.d. categorical family | \(\lceil\log_2\binom{n+K-1}{K-1}\rceil\) bits optimally; simple counters use \(K\lceil\log_2(n+1)\rceil\) | \(n\) samples; \(O(n)\) build, \(O(1)\) per update, \(O(K)\) for a full forecast | None beyond the fixed source family |
| Predictive state / PSR | Future-test predictions of one fixed process/system | \(r\) approximate reals plus tests and update operators; finite precision must be charged | Finite-sample acquisition needs separate coverage/identifiability results | None for an unknown encoder alignment |

## Primary literature and exact scope

1. M. L. Littman, R. S. Sutton, and S. Singh, [“Predictive Representations of State” (NeurIPS 2001)](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2001/file/1e4d36177d71bbb3558e43af9577d70e-Paper.pdf). The definition of a PSR as predictions sufficient for all tests is on PDF pp. 4–6; Theorem 1 and its supplied-POMDP construction are pp. 6–10; the authors' learnability caveat is pp. 4 and 11. In particular, the paper explicitly says learning a PSR for the action-conditioned case remains open in its conclusion. This is a representation theorem, not a finite-sample guarantee.
2. C. R. Shalizi and J. P. Crutchfield, [“Computational Mechanics: Pattern and Prediction, Structure and Simplicity,” *Journal of Statistical Physics* 104 (2001), 817–879](https://csc.ucdavis.edu/~cmg/compmech/pubs/cmppss.htm). The authors describe causal states as the minimal representation consistent with accurate prediction. This is a fixed-process characterization; it does not bound the number of states or finite-sample acquisition cost for arbitrary processes.
3. S. Ben-David, T. Lu, T. Luu, and D. Pal, [“Impossibility Theorems for Domain Adaptation,” AISTATS / PMLR 9 (2010), 129–136](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v9/david10a.html). Their setting allows labeled source and unlabeled target samples. They show that covariate shift alone is insufficient without additional similarity or a shared low-error classifier assumption; their lower bounds apply to any adaptation algorithm without target labels. This backs the need to state transfer premises; it is not the same construction as the unknown-flip lemma above.
4. Y. Ruan, Y. Dubois, and C. J. Maddison, [“Optimal Representations for Covariate Shift,” ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.00057). Theorem 1 characterizes their idealized domain-generalization representation by simultaneous task discriminability and support matching (paper §3.2). Proposition 1 / Appendix Proposition 4 show that, in their stated 0–1-loss idealized setting, source-only representation learning has bad target domains unless target information/assumptions are supplied (Appendix §B.3). Their assumptions and population-risk interface matter; this is not a blanket impossibility theorem for all structured shifts.

## Decision and unresolved obligations

- **Proved here:** exact count sufficiency, the count-code size, the stated expected-TV bound, the family-wide injectivity lower bound, and the binary unknown-alignment error lower bound.
- **Reported in primary source:** PSR finite-POMDP representation theorem; causal-state minimality; domain-adaptation and idealized domain-generalization impossibilities.
- **Not established:** a finite-sample learner for general PSRs with explicit coverage, conditioning, quantization, and computational costs; a useful transfer guarantee under an unspecified representation shift; novelty or priority of the elementary two-world construction.
- **Next decisive extension:** if the acquisition setting has a specified target interface, add exactly the side information available there (known encoder map, paired observations, target labels, or invariant conditional law) and test whether the lower-bound pair remains observationally indistinguishable. Then state a finite-sample theorem for that interface and charge every side channel.
