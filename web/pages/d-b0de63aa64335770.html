# Three concrete obstructions to scalarizing the cylinder mechanism

7 October2026. Outcome: no valid Euclidean scalar reduction was obtained. Three promising direct reductions are ruled out below, with complete proofs in their exact interfaces. This is not a proof that a different scalar Euclidean finite-index construction is impossible.

## Exact target and primary interface

The target is a nonzero real smooth radially localized state on Euclidean R^n satisfying -Delta u+Vu=0 with real smooth bounded V tending to0. The motivating cylinder result additionally has finite negative spectral count; retaining that stronger property is part of the research challenge, rather than an automatic property of a transformed operator.

The source's Lemma4.3 phase-change block, Step4, has the exact matrix

    A_4=diag(1,d(x),1),
    d=[(psi'(x))^2-psi''(x)/k]/4.

It is smooth positive and is identically1/4 on a left neighborhood and identically1 on a right neighborhood of its transition interval. The following geometric obstruction holds for **every** positive smooth d with those endpoint conditions. It therefore does not rely on numerical curvature or a particular cutoff.

## 1. No coordinate/gauge/conformal flattening of the prescribed operator

Allowed operation: a smooth local diffeomorphism, conjugation by a smooth nowhere-zero scalar function (including density conjugation), and multiplication of the equation by a smooth positive scalar. Such transformations are often proposed to turn a divergence-form equation into a Euclidean scalar Schrödinger equation.

Scalar conjugation changes only first- and zeroth-order terms, not the principal matrix. Under new coordinates y=F(x), the principal matrix becomes DF*A*DF^T. Equation multiplication rescales that matrix by a scalar. Thus an identity principal matrix after these operations requires the inverse principal metric g=A^(-1) to be locally conformal to the Euclidean metric. This is a necessary condition; eliminating the first-order term would impose further conditions, which are unnecessary for the obstruction.

Put f=d^(-1/2). On the Step4 block,

    g=dtheta^2+dx^2+f(x)^2 dphi^2.

This is the product of a flat theta line with a two-dimensional warped surface. Its surface Gaussian curvature is K_s=-f''/f. Direct Christoffel calculation gives

    Ric_theta,theta=0,
    Ric_x,x=K_s,
    Ric_phi,phi=K_s f^2,
    scalar curvature=2K_s.

In dimension3 define the Schouten tensor P=Ric-(scalar/4)g and Cotton tensor C_ijk=grad_k P_ij-grad_j P_ik. Because every connection coefficient involving theta vanishes,

    C_theta,theta,x=-K_s'/2
       =(f*f'''-f'*f'')/(2f^2).                         (1)

Here is the needed conformal-flat necessity without importing a sufficiency theorem. For a metric exp(2omega) times the Euclidean metric, its Schouten components in those coordinates are

    P_ij=-omega_ij+omega_i*omega_j
                              -(1/2)|grad omega|^2 delta_ij,

and Gamma^l_ij=delta_li omega_j+delta_lj omega_i-delta_ij omega_l. Substitute them into C. The third derivatives cancel by commuting derivatives; the Hessian-gradient terms in partial_k P_ij-partial_j P_ik are exactly canceled by the two remaining Christoffel terms. Thus C=0 for every such metric. Cotton is a tensor under coordinate changes, so any metric locally conformal to Euclidean has zero Cotton.

Our f is identically2 near the left endpoint and identically1 near the right endpoint. Consequently K_s=0 on both endpoint neighborhoods. If(1) vanished throughout the connected interval, K_s would be constant, hence zero everywhere. Then f''=0, so f would be affine. A function affine throughout the interval and constant on a left neighborhood must be constant throughout, contradicting its right value1. Therefore(1) is nonzero somewhere. The metric is not locally conformal to Euclidean at that point.

This rules out flattening the actual prescribed Step4 operator by the entire allowed class of operations, including conformal equation factors and arbitrary coordinate mixing. The failure is local, so periodic unwrapping does not remove it. It is stronger than merely noting that a generic anisotropic matrix might be curved.

Ordinary flat product lifts do not cure it either. In dimension N>=4 the inverse metric of blockdiag(A_4,I_(N-3)) is a flat(N-2)-dimensional factor times that same surface. At a point where K_s is nonzero, its Weyl tensor on a plane spanned by two flat directions is

    W_ijij=2K_s/[(N-1)(N-2)] !=0.

The formula follows from Ric_i,i=Ric_j,j=0 and scalar=2K_s in the trace-free Riemann formula. Conformal Euclidean metrics have zero Weyl tensor, as follows by inserting their Schouten curvature expression. Since f cannot remain affine, K_s is nonzero somewhere. Thus a flat product lift also fails the necessary conformal-flat condition. This statement does not cover an engineered higher-dimensional embedding with nontrivial normal profiles.

## 2. A critical nodal point defeats any scalar gauge of the supplied state

In the flat eta=1 subinterval at the end of source Step2, its state is

    u=e^(-kx)[cos(k theta)+cos(2k phi)].

At theta=pi/k, phi=0,

    u=0, grad u=0, Delta u=-3k^2 e^(-kx) !=0.            (2)

Every smooth solution of Delta u=V u with bounded scalar V must have Delta u=0 wherever u=0. More robustly, for any smooth nonvanishing scalar multiplier rho,

    Delta(rho*u)=rho*Delta u+2grad rho dot grad u+u Delta rho.

At the point(2) this reduces to rho*Delta u, which is nonzero. Hence no scalar gauge of this exact state solves a Euclidean scalar-potential equation, even if the potential is allowed to depend on all coordinates. This is a nodal compatibility obstruction; quotient division would become singular there.

An anisotropic linear coordinate change can make this one flat block harmonic by rescaling phi. The curvature argument above shows why those local rescalings cannot flatten the full changing-principal construction. The two obstructions must not be conflated: (2) covers gauge-only preservation of the state, whereas(1) covers operator conjugacy under coordinates as well.

For the pure-mode phase-change state u=cos(2k phi)e^(-k psi), direct Euclidean scalarization is possible on that block alone, but the potential is

    V=Delta u/u=k^2(psi'^2-4)-k psi''=4k^2(d-1).

On its left flat neighborhood this equals-3k^2. Successive source frequencies double indefinitely, so this naive block-by-block scalarization would not have a globally bounded, much less vanishing, potential. It also leaves the nodal mismatch in the two-mode blocks unresolved.

## 3. Any exact two independent real cosine modes cannot be switched by scalar V

This rules out a wider mode-engineering route, even after one redesigns the axial phases. Let I be a connected interval and k,m positive integers. Suppose real A,B in C^2(I) and a continuous scalar V on T^2 times I satisfy

    u=A(x)cos(k theta)+B(x)cos(m phi),
    Delta u=V(theta,phi,x)u.                             (3)

No assumption that V is independent of the angles is made. At an x with A and B both nonzero, choose any sufficiently small nonzero t, set cos(m phi)=t and cos(k theta)=-(B/A)t. Such angles exist and give u=0. Equation(3) forces

    0=[B''-m^2 B-(B/A)(A''-k^2 A)]t,
    A''/A-k^2=B''/B-m^2=:v(x).                          (4)

The residual is then v(x)u at every angle. Hence V=v(x) wherever u is nonzero. The nonzero angular set is dense because this nonzero trigonometric polynomial has no open zero set; continuity of V makes it constant in angles over the entire slice.

If B=0 and A is nonzero at x, set cos(k theta)=0 and cos(m phi)=1. This is a zero of u, so(3) forces B''=0 at that x. The remaining residual is (A''/A-k^2)u, giving the same axial V by angular density. The case A=0,B nonzero is symmetric. This order matters: one cannot initially compare Fourier coefficients of V u while V might still depend on angles. If both amplitudes vanish at x, the right side of(3) is identically zero on the whole slice, so independent cosine coefficients give A''=B''=0 there.

Define v(x) to be the torus average of V at every x, including slices on which both amplitudes vanish. It is continuous. The preceding cases prove everywhere on I that

    A''=(k^2+v)A, B''=(m^2+v)B.                        (5)

Thus the scalar equation has forced a common axial potential and decoupled the two amplitudes. If B vanishes on an incoming interval, its value and first derivative vanish at an interior point. Uniqueness for the linear second-order ODE in(5) gives B=0 on all of I. The same holds for A. Consequently there is no exact two-mode block starting with only cos(k theta) and ending with a nonzero cos(m phi), for any smooth scalar potential, even angle dependent.

This applies to the source's cutoff interpolation and to the natural redesigned interpolation chi_1(x)e^(-kx)cos(k theta)+chi_2(x)e^(-mx)cos(m phi). It identifies the specific missing mechanism: a scalar reduction must generate additional modes or change the nodal geometry, rather than directly transfer the supplied exact two-real-mode interpolation. It does not prohibit richer mode families, harmonic repair, noninvertible modifications, or a new geometric construction.
