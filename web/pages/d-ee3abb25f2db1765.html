# Metastable escape under a slowly switching environment

## Verdict

The strongest defensible result from this branch is an exact, two-mode killed-chain calculation that exposes a three-regime competition between a physical escape barrier and the cost of reaching a different environment. It yields an explicit mean escape time, a sharp exponential-scale prediction, and a counterexample to replacing a temporally switching hazard by its stationary average.

The calculation is internally reconstructed and proved below. It is not novelty-cleared. Resonant activation over switching barriers, environmental modulation of extinction, and multiscale metastable Markov chains are established subjects; Freidlin–Koralov's general hierarchy for parameter-dependent chains is especially close in scope. Treat the result as a compact mechanism and a possible experimental discriminator, not as a new theorem claim until a source-level comparison establishes a genuinely missing case.

## Model and exact result

Let the environment \(E_t\in\{1,2\}\) be a continuous-time Markov chain, with rates \(1\to2\) equal to \(\alpha>0\) and \(2\to1\) equal to \(\beta>0\). Conditional on the current environment, escape from a metastable basin occurs with constant hazard \(\lambda_i>0\) in mode \(i\). Escape is absorbing. This is exactly a Markov-modulated Poisson killing model; the theorem does not derive the mode-specific hazards from an underlying reaction network or diffusion.

For \(m_i=\mathbb E_i[\tau]\), first-step analysis gives

\[
\begin{aligned}
(\alpha+\lambda_1)m_1-\alpha m_2&=1,\\
-\beta m_1+(\beta+\lambda_2)m_2&=1.
\end{aligned}
\]

The determinant is \(D=\alpha\lambda_2+\beta\lambda_1+\lambda_1\lambda_2\), so

\[
m_1=\frac{\alpha+\beta+\lambda_2}{D},\qquad
m_2=\frac{\alpha+\beta+\lambda_1}{D}.
\]

These equalities hold for every positive rate tuple. Survival is also exact:
\[
\Pr_i(\tau>t)=e_i^{\mathsf T}\exp\!\left[t\begin{pmatrix}-(\alpha+\lambda_1)&\alpha\\\beta&-(\beta+\lambda_2)\end{pmatrix}\right]\mathbf 1.
\]

Now take a system-size/barrier parameter \(N\to\infty\), with
\[
\alpha_N=\beta_N=c e^{-sN},\quad
\lambda_{1,N}=a_1e^{-b_1N},\quad
\lambda_{2,N}=a_2e^{-b_2N},
\]
where \(0<b_1<b_2\) and \(a_1,a_2,c>0\) are fixed. Mode 1 is the lower escape barrier; mode 2 is the more protected one. Starting from mode 2,
\[
\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\log \mathbb E_2[\tau]
=\operatorname{median}(b_1,s,b_2).
\]
Away from the crossover values, the sharper predictions are
\[
\mathbb E_2[\tau]\sim
\begin{cases}
2a_1^{-1}e^{b_1N},&s<b_1,\\
c^{-1}e^{sN},&b_1<s<b_2,\\
a_2^{-1}e^{b_2N},&s>b_2.
\end{cases}
\]
The three regimes have distinct mechanisms:

* If \(s<b_1\), the environment mixes before escape. Its stationary mass is one half in each mode, and the dominant average hazard is \((a_1/2)e^{-b_1N}\).
* If \(b_1<s<b_2\), mode 2 almost never escapes before a switch, while mode 1 almost always kills before switching back. The waiting time to the useful environmental switch, \(c^{-1}e^{sN}\), is the bottleneck.
* If \(s>b_2\), the initial mode-2 escape occurs before an environmental switch and its own barrier controls the mean.

The asymptotic statements are for fixed exponents and fixed positive prefactors. They are uniform over prefactors in compact subsets of \((0,\infty)^3\), but not over a growing number of modes, changing exponents, or prefactors with unbounded exponential dependence on \(N\). The exact formula remains valid at \(s=b_1,b_2\); the displayed equivalences exclude these boundaries because their leading constants change. At \(s=b_1\), the scaled absorption law is generally phase-type rather than exponential, since switching and mode-1 killing remain on the same scale. At \(s=b_2\), the mode-2 hazard and useful switch compete, changing the prefactor to \((a_2+c)^{-1}\) for start mode 2.

Starting from mode 1 instead gives exponential-scale exponent \(b_1\) for every fixed \(s\); the formula for \(m_1\) gives the corresponding prefactors and crossover constants. Thus an initial-condition-blind "barrier" can fail even within this minimal model.

### Proof of the asymptotic law

The exact expression for \(m_2\) is
\[
m_{2,N}=\frac{2c e^{-sN}+a_1e^{-b_1N}}{ca_2e^{-(s+b_2)N}+ca_1e^{-(s+b_1)N}+a_1a_2e^{-(b_1+b_2)N}}.
\]
The numerator's leading exponent is \(\min(s,b_1)\). Since \(b_2>b_1\), the denominator's leading exponent is \(b_1+\min(s,b_2)\). Their difference, in denominator-minus-numerator order, is
\[
b_1+\min(s,b_2)-\min(s,b_1)=\operatorname{median}(b_1,s,b_2).
\]
When the minima are unique, the leading coefficients give the three equivalences above. This is an algebraic proof, with no approximation to the stochastic dynamics.

In the same strict regimes, the rescaled escape time is asymptotically exponential: after fast environmental mixing for \(s<b_1\); after an asymptotically negligible mode-1 killing delay for \(b_1<s<b_2\); and by direct mode-2 killing for \(s>b_2\). These follow from competing exponential clocks and the exact two-mode construction. No claim is made for a general metastable process whose conditional hazard is not memoryless.

## Counterexample to stationary hazard averaging

Set \(b_1=1,b_2=4,s=2,a_1=a_2=c=1\), start in mode 2. The exact theorem gives
\[
\mathbb E_2[\tau]\sim e^{2N}.
\]
The stationary environment is still exactly balanced at every \(N\). Replacing the modulated hazard by its stationary average gives \(\bar\lambda_N=(e^{-N}+e^{-4N})/2\sim e^{-N}/2\), hence the false prediction \(\bar\lambda_N^{-1}\sim2e^N\). Averaging the two barrier exponents gives \(2.5N\), which is false as well. The error is not a prefactor: the predicted exponential slope changes. The missing state variable is the environment's transition clock, which can be slower than escape in mode 1 yet faster than escape in mode 2.

## Discriminating experiment

An appropriate test is a two-condition, single-cell extinction or phenotype-loss assay in a microfluidic chamber. Use a fluorescent environmental-state reporter and a population/state reporter. Hold the two environments' measured long-run occupancy at 1/2 and hold each mode's conditional exit hazard fixed while changing only the switching rate. Estimate \(a_i,b_i\) from mode-labelled survival data and \(c,s\) from reporter transition times, across a controlled size/barrier series \(N\). Compare the exact killed-chain prediction with (i) a stationary-average-hazard model and (ii) an equally informed model that retains the environment state.

The discriminating prediction is a slope plateau at \(b_1\) for fast switching, a slope \(s\) in the intermediate band, and a plateau at \(b_2\) for sufficiently slow switching, when experiments can vary the barrier scale enough to approximate the stated exponential family. A single fixed-size frequency scan can test non-monotonicity or residence-time effects, but cannot identify the asymptotic median law by itself. The falsifier is a reproducible deviation from the exact two-state survival curve after mode-specific hazard and transition rates are independently estimated, or failure of the predicted slope changes under a barrier series while the memoryless-within-mode assumption continues to fit.

The test requires measuring rare exits, not only environmental occupancy. A direct estimate of an exponential mean from uncensored independent trajectories has relative standard error about \(1/\sqrt n\), so roughly 100 completed exits per condition are needed for 10% relative precision before accounting for censoring or uncertainty in rates. Since the expected wait is exponential in \(N\), this can dominate the experiment. Parallel chambers and validated rare-event sampling can reduce wall time, but sampling weights and the complete acquisition/compute cost must be reported. A fluorescence label, controlled medium switch, or selected trajectories cannot be treated as free if that is the data source.

## Literature and novelty boundary

The fluctuating-barrier question is established. Doering and Gadoua (1992) report resonant activation for a barrier switching between two heights, with slow, fast, and intermediate switching regimes. Agudov, Dubkov, and Spagnolo (2003) give exact mean-escape-time results for a Brownian particle in a dichotomously switching piecewise-linear barrier over arbitrary switching rates. Freidlin and Koralov (2017) develop a general hierarchy for parameter-dependent finite Markov chains and exponentially separated transition rates, with metastable behavior depending on the initial state and time scale. These results make the broad mechanism and likely exponential-scale novelty non-credible without a narrower literature comparison. Bianchi and Gaudillière (2016) prove exponential exit laws under conditions tied to relaxation and quasi-stationarity; our elementary two-mode kill model does not replace those results. Thakur and Meyer-Ortmanns (2023) study MTE control for two bacterial phenotypes under environmental switching using Gillespie simulation and WKB in limiting cases, reinforcing the biological relevance while making the application theme itself non-novel.

## Cost, assumptions, and next gate

The mathematical calculation takes constant-size linear algebra after the four rates are supplied. It does not acquire a hidden-state model, estimate hazards, or derive an Arrhenius/Kramers law. In an application, rate estimation, environmental-state measurement, rare-event data, reporter calibration, finite-size corrections, and censoring analysis must be charged. The approximation requires (a) mode transitions independent of the event conditional on mode, (b) constant hazards within each mode, and (c) a metastable basin that is well represented by a single hazard per mode. Correlated escape attempts, within-mode relaxation on the rare-event timescale, switching that changes the basin geometry, and non-exponential dwell times invalidate the reduction.
