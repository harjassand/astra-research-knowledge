Commutative PIT versus noncommutative matrix queries: the acquisition gap
Date: 2026-10-08
Status: scoped derivation; source claims below are not independent proof or priority validation.

Finding

The transfer barrier is the abelianization quotient, together with a stronger query interface. A scalar commutative oracle exposes f(a) for a point a in the base field. N51/N68-style results ask a hidden free-algebra object to evaluate on noncommuting matrices and receive a full matrix. Matrix evaluation does not descend to the commutative quotient unless the queried matrices commute. The two sides therefore do not share an oracle merely because their formulas have similar syntax.

A commutative PIT algorithm needs either (i) a small, explicitly generated set of scalar points that hits every nonzero member of the promised circuit class, or (ii) an acquired and verified structural witness that yields such a set or a direct identity test. Sparsity, bounded ABP width/order, low partial-derivative dimension, and degree are examples of structural promises that support specialized PIT. For general commutative arithmetic circuits, size alone does not currently supply a polynomial-time deterministic black-box hitting set. Kabanets–Impagliazzo show why a general deterministic polynomial-time PIT is a major barrier: it would imply either NEXP is not contained in P/poly or the permanent lacks polynomial-size arithmetic circuits. This is an implication, not an impossibility theorem.

Interfaces and supplied structure

1. Commutative scalar PIT. Hidden object: f in K[x_1,...,x_n], often given only through O_f(a)=f(a). A query is one scalar point a in K^n and returns one field element. A class promise such as circuit size s and degree D is supplied; the polynomial's monomial support is not. For degree D, random scalar evaluations have the Schwartz–Zippel guarantee, while deterministic PIT requires an explicit hitting set. General circuits can have exponential degree and support in their circuit size. For example, the size-O(n) product ∏_{i=1}^n(1+x_i) has 2^n monomials. Expanding it into the sparse representation required by N68 costs exponential space even though the identity question itself is trivial.

2. Noncommutative rational formulas (N51 as stored in the repository). Hidden object: a size-s formula in the free skew field, with ordered multiplication and inverse gates. Each query is an n-tuple of square matrices; the response is a full matrix. A query is admissible only if every original inverse gate is evaluated at an invertible matrix. The repository's source-derived, unreviewed claim is that n,s alone generate a polynomial-total-bit list of integer/rational matrix tuples, uniformly across fields and extension coefficients, such that each nonzero admissible size-s formula has a defined invertible hit. The list-generation input has unary size parameters; the source card sets B=2s+1 and preserves all original inverse domains. The card explicitly says the full promised-size generator was not executed, its constants are extreme (already >4e62 tuples in a base case), and external correctness/priority is unresolved. Even granting the claimed theorem, it is an NC rational-formula hitting list, not a scalar commutative hitting set or a commutative circuit compiler. Total query evaluations, exact matrix-response bits, and formula-domain checks remain part of any end-to-end cost.

3. Sparse noncommutative polynomials (N68's primary-source boundary). Lakhani–Mukhopadhyay, ECCC TR26-120 rev. 2, Theorem 2.1: for every n,s≥1 there is a deterministic poly(n,s)-time construction of one tuple (A_1,...,A_n) in M_s(Z)^n that hits every f in C⟨x_1,...,x_n⟩ with at most s nonzero words; each input-matrix entry has O(s log n) bits. The oracle must accept matrix-algebra inputs and return f(A). The tuple encodes each word w by an injective integer code α(w) in base n+1; at most s distinct words give independent Vandermonde vectors. This is a one-query identity test under that matrix-oracle model. The N68 card's stronger 2s-dimensional one-response reconstruction of all terms is an internal source-derived candidate; its decoder explicitly depends polynomially on the actual rational response bits. It is not the ECCC theorem and its external proof/priority remains open.

Exact obstruction: matrix substitution does not factor through commutativization

Let ab: K⟨x_1,...,x_n⟩ → K[x_1,...,x_n] be the abelianization map, which imposes x_i x_j=x_j x_i. Its kernel contains the commutators x_i x_j−x_j x_i. An evaluation map ev_A into M_d(K) factors through ab only when the matrices A_i commute pairwise.

For n=2, take X=E_12 and Y=E_21. Then

  XY−YX = diag(1,−1),

which is invertible (also in characteristic two), although the commutative polynomial xy−yx is identically zero. Moreover, the equivalent commutative circuits xy and yx evaluate to E_11 and E_22, respectively. Thus a scalar polynomial oracle does not determine a matrix response at a noncommuting tuple, and evaluating a supplied commutative circuit's syntax at noncommuting matrices can turn a true commutative identity into a nonzero, even invertible, matrix.

Equivalently, given a syntactic noncommutative lift F of a commutative circuit, ab(F)=f. Free-algebra PIT asks whether F=0; commutative PIT asks whether F lies in ker(ab). N51/N68 hit or reconstruct in the free algebra, not modulo the commutator ideal. To transfer them one would need a quotient-aware hitting construction or a size-controlled canonical lift that preserves zero-ness. Restricting all queries to commuting tuples avoids the false positive but removes the word-order information used by the NC matrix constructions and leaves the scalar hitting-set problem.

Concrete scalar-query lower bound (exact and scoped)

Claim (INTERNALLY-PROVED-CANDIDATE). Fix Q and n≥1. Consider deterministic adaptive PIT using only scalar evaluations at rational points for the class of nonzero polynomials in Q[x_1,...,x_n] with at most s monomials, arbitrary degree, and unrestricted rational coefficients. If the algorithm makes q<s scalar queries on the all-zero transcript, it cannot decide correctly. Let a^(1),...,a^(q) be the queried points on that transcript and set

  P(x_1,...,x_n) = ∏_{j=1}^q (x_1 − a_1^(j)).

P is nonzero over Q, vanishes at every queried point, and has at most q+1≤s monomials. It is consistent with the all-zero transcript just as the zero polynomial is. Therefore worst-case scalar query count is at least s. If queried coordinates have at most L bits, the rational coefficients of P have O(q(L+log(q+1))) bits after clearing denominators, so the adversary remains polynomial-bit whenever q and L are polynomially bounded; if a coefficient-height cap is part of the class, require it to include that bound. The same lower bound holds for n variables because the adversary uses only x_1.

This shows one scalar call cannot be equated with N68's one matrix call. The ECCC tuple has s^2 entries per variable, hence O(n s^3 log n) bits to write from the stated entry bound, and its response is an s-by-s matrix. The matrix call packages many correlated word tests. That is a call-count separation for the stated interfaces, not a complexity separation: deterministic scalar PIT for sparse commutative polynomials of unbounded degree is already known. Bläser–Hardt–Lipton–Vishnoi give a black-box algorithm over the integers with time polynomial in circuit size and monomial count, logarithmic in degree and coefficient height.

Bit-cost caveat for “one query”

The matrix input entries are short, but an exact full response need not be. In the ECCC construction with n=1 and the promised sparsity s=2, A_1=[[1,1],[0,2]]. For f=x_1^L,

  f(A_1)=A_1^L=[[1,2^L−1],[0,2^L]],

so the response contains Θ(L) bits. In particular, a succinct arithmetic circuit using repeated squaring can specify x_1^(2^r) with O(r) gates while the exact matrix response has Θ(2^r) bits. The one-query theorem counts a matrix-algebra oracle call and gives polynomial-time construction of the tuple; in an exact bit-cost model, reading that response can be exponential in a succinct circuit description. This is a model/cost distinction, not a refutation of the paper's query-complexity theorem. For N68's full acquisition candidate, the decoder itself charges actual response bits, as it must.

Why N51/N68 do not settle commutative PIT

• Algebra: their noncommuting evaluations detect word order and fail to respect ker(ab). The 2×2 commutator example gives a false positive for a commutative zero under the naive syntactic lift.
• Oracle: scalar evaluations cannot be promoted to matrix evaluations from the transcript alone. Equivalent circuits for xy have different matrix outputs. A matrix oracle is a stronger interface, not an encoding of one scalar query.
• Structure: N68 supplies a known sparsity ceiling s in the free monoid. General size-s commutative circuits can have 2^n terms, and DAG circuits can have degree 2^s. N51 supplies a size-s formula promise in a different algebra, plus original inverse-domain semantics. Neither acquires a commutative normal form or a polynomial-size representation modulo commutators.
• Costs: N68's call returns s^2 exact values and can have degree-dependent output length. N51's polynomial-bit list-generation claim is separate from actually evaluating every list point, processing the responses, and enforcing all inverse domains. These costs do not transfer to the scalar-query model.
• Complexity barrier: if a polynomial-size quotient-aware compiler/hitting procedure converted every size-s commutative circuit into objects tested by N51 or N68, while preserving f=0 iff the compiled object is zero, it would yield deterministic polynomial-time commutative PIT (assuming the compiler and oracle costs are polynomial). Kabanets–Impagliazzo then gives the stated circuit-lower-bound consequence. No such compiler is supplied by either result.

This argument does not show that commutative PIT is information-theoretically impossible. It identifies the missing acquisition step: learn or construct, within polynomial description and query/bit cost, a representation that has already quotiented out all commutators while retaining a small test set for the hidden circuit class. A direct next target is to formalize the minimal cost of quotient-aware matrix access for arithmetic circuits, separating scalar-only oracle access, syntax-evaluated matrix access, and explicit commuting-matrix access.
