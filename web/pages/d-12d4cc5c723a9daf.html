Abstract
    

We compare statistical experiments when observations are inexpensive and can be acquired sequentially until the decision maker chooses to stop. We introduce two orders. Small-cost decision dominance asks which of two equally priced experiments is eventually preferred in every decision problem as the per-observation cost vanishes; large-budget stopping dominance asks which experiment can reproduce every terminal experiment attainable from the other under all sufficiently large expected-sample budgets. Our main result shows that, for generic pairs, the two orders coincide and are both characterized by strict dominance of every pairwise Kullback–Leibler divergence. The key step is a uniform exact-conversion theorem: any finite-output stopping
policy based on one experiment can be reproduced exactly using another,
with first-order expected-sample requirements determined by pairwise KL rates and
a square-root remainder that is uniform over policies.

1 Introduction




Digitization has made many forms of data cheaper to acquire, store, and process (Einav and Levin, 2014; Varian, 2014; Goldfarb and Tucker, 2019). A firm can query a database, run a diagnostic, or collect an additional measurement at low marginal cost, and can condition whether to continue on the evidence already observed.11
            1
            
            
            
            
            
            
            
          A prominent example is online experimentation. Digital platforms make experimentation inexpensive and allow firms to condition how much data they collect on outcomes observed along the way (Kohavi et al., 2020; Johari et al., 2022).
 As a result, decision makers
may demand large amounts of data, and may choose when to stop acquiring. This raises a natural question for comparing data technologies. Suppose observations from two technologies are available at the same low cost, and a decision maker may acquire as many as she wishes before stopping. Which technology would she prefer across all decision problems she might ultimately face?




We call this comparison small-cost decision dominance. An experiment dominates another if, in every finite decision problem, it delivers a weakly higher optimal value for all sufficiently small common per-observation costs. This is the natural analogue of Blackwell’s decision-theoretic comparison when information is acquired sequentially at a cost rather than provided once and for free (Blackwell, 1951; Blackwell, 1953).




A similar question can be posed on the supply side. Fix an expected-sample budget. A designer may repeatedly sample from a source experiment,
choose when to stop, and then produce a finite output, thereby inducing a new
experiment from the entire observed history. Which source experiment should be
viewed as more productive when data is abundant? We compare source experiments by the sets of experiments they can generate in
this way. We say that one source large-budget stopping dominates another
if, once the budget is sufficiently large, it can exactly reproduce every
experiment attainable from the other under the same expected-sample budget in every state.




Our main result shows that the two comparisons have the same generic answer.
Let F=(F_{i})_{i\in\Theta} and G=(G_{i})_{i\in\Theta} be finite,
full-support, pairwise identified experiments on a finite state space. For
distinct states i and j, define




I_{ij}(E):=\mathrm{D}\!\left(E_{i}\middle\|E_{j}\right),



where \mathrm{D} denotes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence. We call the pair (F,G)
KL-generic if none of their I_{ij}’s coincide. Our main
theorem establishes that




\begin{split}&I_{ij}(F)>I_{ij}(G)\quad\text{for every }i\neq j\\
&\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\text{ large-budget stopping dominates }G\\
&\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\quad F\text{ small-cost decision dominates }G.\end{split}






Under state i, a policy must accumulate evidence against every alternative j from which its terminal output distinguishes i, and each observation from experiment E supplies such evidence at mean rate I_{ij}(E). A source can therefore be uniformly more productive only if it generates evidence faster in every direction. The theorem shows that, generically, these necessary pairwise conditions are also sufficient.




The proof of our main theorem rests on a uniform exact-conversion theorem. Given any finite-output stopping policy based on G, we construct a stopping policy based on F that induces exactly the same terminal experiment. Under state i, the reproducing F-policy’s expected sample size satisfies




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T},\qquad\rho_{i}(F\to G):=\max_{j\neq i}\frac{I_{ij}(G)}{I_{ij}(F)}.



A single reproducing policy satisfies these bounds simultaneously in every state, with a constant C_{F,G} uniform over policies and finite output spaces. If every pairwise KL inequality is strict, then \rho_{i}(F\to G)<1 for every i; the resulting linear saving eventually dominates the square-root remainder, yielding conversion within the same budget for all sufficiently large budgets.




1.1 Relation to the literature




This paper lies at the intersection of four literatures: the comparison of
sequential experiments, large-sample orders for repeated experiments,
endogenous information acquisition, and sequential testing and experimental
design. We discuss these connections in turn.




Comparison of sequential experiments.




The closest statistical antecedents compare experiments through sufficiency,
deficiency, and sequential sampling. Repetition can generate Blackwell
comparisons that fail for a single observation (Torgersen, 1970; Torgersen, 1991),
while Le Cam’s deficiency measures how closely one experiment can simulate
another (Le Cam, 1964; Le Cam and Yang, 2000). Our comparison differs from the usual
asymptotic-deficiency approach: the terminal experiment is reproduced exactly,
and the asymptotic object is the expected number of source observations needed
for that exact reproduction.




More directly, Greenshtein (1996) extends zero-deficiency comparisons to
sequential experiments. For stopped records generated by a common i.i.d. experiment, Greenshtein and Torgersen (1997) show that sufficiency of one
stopped record for another requires weakly larger expected sample size in every
state; related results for exponential families impose further restrictions on
the stopping-time distributions (Greenshtein and Torgersen, 1998). We instead
compare two distinct i.i.d. source experiments and give a sufficient condition
for exact reproduction of any finite terminal output, together with a statewise
sample-size bound that is uniform over target policies. Pairwise KL
inequalities arise as necessary conditions by data processing, consistent with
the classical observation that Blackwell sufficiency implies KL-information
inequalities (Goel and DeGroot, 1979). These inequalities do not characterize
one-shot Blackwell dominance. Our result shows that, with repeated sampling and
endogenous stopping, their strict versions generically characterize both of
our operational orders.




A separate literature extends Blackwell’s question to settings in which the
object being ranked is an information process rather than a repeatable source.
de Oliveira (2018) obtains an adapted-garbling characterization for
information arriving over time, while Dillenberger et al. (2023),
Whitmeyer and Williams (2024), and Renou and Venel (2024) study related
comparisons of dynamic information for different classes of intertemporal
decision problems. These papers rank the timing or evolution of information.
In our model, the state and source are stationary, observations are costly, and
the object ultimately produced is a single terminal experiment.





Large-sample orders for repeated experiments.




A second closely related literature asks whether repeated experiments become
comparable as the sample size grows. Moscarini and Smith (2002) compare the
value of deterministic i.i.d. samples in finite-state, finite-action decision
problems. Under their genericity conditions, large-sample value is governed by
the hardest pair of states to distinguish, yielding a scalar index based on
pairwise Chernoff information. Our decision order asks a parallel
value-comparison question but allows the sample size to depend on the realized
history. Moving from a deterministic horizon to endogenous stopping replaces
the fixed-sample Chernoff criterion with the full array of directed pairwise KL
rates.




A stronger fixed-horizon comparison is Blackwell dominance in large samples
(the MPST order). Azrieli (2014) requires F^{\otimes n} to Blackwell dominate
G^{\otimes n} for every sufficiently large n and shows that this order lies
strictly between one-shot Blackwell dominance and the Moscarini–Smith order. For bounded binary-state experiments, Mu et al. (2021)
show, under an endpoint genericity condition, that Blackwell dominance in large samples is equivalent to strict dominance of the Rényi-divergence
spectrum in both directions.




The distinction from our stopping order lies in the resource constraint. A
deterministic horizon assigns the same number of observations to every sample
path, so likelihood-ratio tails, and hence the Rényi spectrum or multivariate
moment-generating function, remain relevant. Under an expected-sample budget, a
policy can stop early on easy histories and continue on difficult ones, so
expected log-likelihood evidence is instead governed by directed KL
divergence. We elaborate on this distinction in Section 4.





Endogenous information acquisition.




Our production interpretation also connects to work that derives information
costs or feasible information structures from an underlying acquisition
process. Morris and Strack (2019) study sequential sampling from a prescribed
Wald source. In the binary case, every Bayes-plausible distribution of
posteriors can be generated by a stopping rule, and its expected acquisition
cost admits a log-likelihood representation; with multiple states,
attainability is more restrictive. Our question is comparative: when can one
source reproduce every terminal experiment generated by another?




Bloedel and Zhong (2025) derive an indirect information cost by minimizing a
primitive direct cost over sequential acquisition procedures and characterize
the resulting costs through sequential learning-proofness. Our approach is
complementary. Rather than assigning a scalar cost to a target experiment, we
fix a source technology and compare the sets of terminal experiments it can
generate under an expected-sample budget. Our KL characterization is
also closely related to Pomatto et al. (2023), whose axioms for
constant-marginal information costs yield KL-divergence-based cost measures.
Weak coordinatewise dominance of directed KL divergences is therefore
equivalent to assigning weakly more informational content to F than to G
under every cost in their class. Their result evaluates an experiment as an
informational output; ours evaluates it as a repeatable input technology and
asks which outputs can be produced from it.




A broader literature studies optimal dynamic information acquisition in a
given decision environment, allowing agents to choose how information is
gathered over time
(Fudenberg et al., 2018; Che and Mierendorff, 2019; Zhong, 2022; Hébert and Woodford, 2023).
Particularly relevant is Liang et al. (2022), who study the dynamic
allocation of attention across multiple Gaussian sources with endogenous
stopping. The distinction is that these papers optimize an acquisition
strategy within a given environment, whereas our comparison ranks fixed source
technologies uniformly across downstream decision problems and target
policies.





Sequential decision theory, testing, and experimental design.




The mechanics behind our KL characterization are closely related to classical
sequential decision theory. The problem of paying for observations and choosing
when to stop goes back to Arrow et al. (1949) and
Wald (1945); Wald (1947). For two simple hypotheses,
Wald and Wolfowitz (1948) establish the expected-sample optimality of the
sequential probability-ratio test, while Hoeffding (1960) derives
information-theoretic lower bounds for sequential procedures. Extensions to
multiple hypotheses and small sampling costs include
Kiefer and Sacks (1963); Lorden (1977); Dragalin et al. (1999). Across
this literature, I_{ij}(E) is the mean rate at which evidence accumulates
under state i against alternative j, which explains why directed KL
divergences govern first-order sample requirements.




Our objective, however, is not to solve a particular testing problem. The exact-conversion theorem must reproduce every finite terminal output generated by every
integrable stopping policy, regardless of whether that output is an estimate or an optimal testing decision. This policy-uniform requirement turns the familiar
evidence-rate calculation into an order over source experiments.




Sequential experimental design introduces an additional margin by allowing the
experiment used at each date to depend on past observations
(Chernoff, 1959; DeGroot, 1962; Kiefer and Sacks, 1963); modern work on controlled
sensing develops this idea for multiple hypotheses
(Nitinawarat et al., 2013). In our model, the source experiment is
fixed within a policy: the agent chooses when to stop and what terminal output
to produce, but not which experiment to sample next. We therefore compare
information technologies themselves rather than adaptive designs constructed
from a menu of technologies.




The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 defines the two orders and the associated sequential experiments. Section 3 states the equivalence and exact-conversion theorem and proves the former in the main text.
Section 4 compares our endogenous-stopping orders with the fixed-sample orders of Moscarini–Smith and Mu–Pomatto–Strack–Tamuz. Section 5 concludes. Proofs of the exact-conversion theorem and the auxiliary lemmas appear in the appendices.







2 Model and concepts




2.1 Experiments and sequential policies




Let the state space be \Theta=\{0,1,\ldots,d\}, where d\geq 1. A finite
experiment E=(E_{i})_{i\in\Theta} consists of a finite signal space
\mathcal{X}_{E} and, for each state i, a probability distribution E_{i} on
\mathcal{X}_{E}. Throughout, experiments have full support,




E_{i}(x)>0\qquad(i\in\Theta,\ x\in\mathcal{X}_{E}),



and identify every pair of states: E_{i}\neq E_{j} whenever i\neq j.
These assumptions ensure that all log-likelihood ratios are bounded and all
pairwise KL divergences are finite and strictly positive.




Repeated observations from E are represented by a signal stream
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots. Under state i, the signals are i.i.d. with law E_{i}.
A policy may also use any countable collection of auxiliary random variables
that are independent of the state and of the entire signal stream. Formally,
each policy is defined on a common product extension of
\mathcal{X}_{E}^{\mathbb{N}}, with state-i law denoted by \mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}, and is
adapted to a filtration (\mathcal{F}_{n}^{E})_{n\geq 0} generated by
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n} together with the auxiliary draws revealed by time n.




Let \mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{E}:=\sigma(\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{n}^{E}). A map T from the
policy’s underlying product space to \mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\} is a stopping time
if




\{T\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{E}\qquad\text{for every }n\geq 0.



Its stopped sigma-field is




\mathcal{F}_{T}^{E}:=\left\{A\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}^{E}:A\cap\{T\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{E}\text{ for every }n\geq 0\right\}.






Definition 1 (Stopping policy).



Let \mathcal{Z} be finite. A finite-output stopping E-policy is a pair
(T,Z), where T is a stopping time with \mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T<\infty for every
i\in\Theta, and Z is a \mathcal{Z}-valued, \mathcal{F}_{T}^{E}-measurable random
variable. The induced terminal experiment is




H(T,Z):=\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{i}^{E}(Z)\bigr)_{i\in\Theta}.








2.2 Small-cost decision dominance




A finite decision problem is D=(\mu,A,u), where
\mu\in\Delta(\Theta) is a prior, A is finite, and
u:A\times\Theta\to\mathbb{R} is the payoff function. If each observation costs
c>0, define




U_{c}(E;D):=\sup_{(T,a_{T})}\sum_{i\in\Theta}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\bigl[u(a_{T},i)-cT\bigr],



where the supremum is over finite-output stopping E-policies whose terminal
output is an action in A.




This formulation captures a decision maker who repeatedly samples from an
experiment, pays a constant cost for each observation, and chooses
endogenously when to stop and take an action.




Definition 2 (Small-cost decision dominance).



Experiment F small-cost decision dominates G, written
F\succeq_{\mathrm{d}}G, if for every finite decision problem D there is
\bar{c}_{D}>0 such that




U_{c}(F;D)\geq U_{c}(G;D)\qquad\text{whenever }0<c<\bar{c}_{D}.







Definition 2 compares experiments by asking whether one
eventually yields at least as much value as the other in every finite decision
problem once observations are sufficiently cheap. The cutoff \bar{c}_{D} is allowed to depend on the decision problem. This is
natural because different priors and payoff structures make information more or less valuable, so what counts as a sufficiently small per-observation cost can vary across decision problems.





2.3 Large-budget stopping dominance




Definition 3 (Exact reproduction).



An F-policy (N,Y) exactly reproduces a G-policy (T,Z) if




\mathcal{L}_{i}^{F}(Y)=\mathcal{L}_{i}^{G}(Z)\qquad\text{for every }i\in\Theta.







Next, we shift the comparison from the demand side to the
supply side. Consider an information producer with an expected-sample budget of n observations in each state. The producer can repeatedly sample from either
source experiment F or G, choosing when to stop and what output to produce. The question is which source is more productive, in the sense of
generating a larger set of attainable terminal experiments once the expected-sample
budget is sufficiently large.




Definition 4 (Large-budget stopping dominance).



For an expected-sample budget n\in\mathbb{R}_{+}, let




\mathcal{P}_{n}(E):=\left\{H(T,Z):(T,Z)\text{ is a finite-output stopping }E\text{-policy and }\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T\leq n\text{ for all }i\right\}.



Experiment F large-budget stopping dominates G, written
F\succeq_{\mathrm{s}}G, if there exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+} such that




\mathcal{P}_{n}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}(F)\qquad\text{for every }n\geq n_{0}.







We refer to \succeq_{\mathrm{d}} and \succeq_{\mathrm{s}} simply as the
decision order and the stopping order.




The set \mathcal{P}_{n}(E) consists of the terminal experiments attainable by
finite-output stopping E-policies whose expected sample size is at most n
in every state. Because the budget constraint is statewise, feasibility does
not depend on the distribution of states and is therefore robust across
priors. Moreover, exact
attainability of a terminal experiment H is equivalent to attaining some
terminal experiment K that Blackwell dominates H, since a costless
state-independent garbling of K can reproduce H.




A key requirement in Definition 4 is uniformity: a single
threshold n_{0} must work for every terminal experiment and every budget
n\geq n_{0}. Thus, beyond n_{0}, anything producible from G can be reproduced
from F without increasing the expected-sample budget.





2.4 Pairwise KL divergences and genericity




Finally, we introduce a statistical comparison of experiments based on
Kullback–Leibler divergence. For each ordered pair of distinct states
i\neq j, define




I_{ij}(E):=\mathrm{D}\!\left(E_{i}\middle\|E_{j}\right)=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{E}}E_{i}(x)\log\frac{E_{i}(x)}{E_{j}(x)}.



Under state i, I_{ij}(E) is the expected increment in the cumulative
log-likelihood ratio in favor of i against j. In general, I_{ij}(E)\neq I_{ji}(E).




We focus primarily on the generic case in which the two experiments have
different KL divergences for every ordered pair of states.




Definition 5 (KL-generic pair).



The pair (F,G) is KL-generic if




I_{ij}(F)\neq I_{ij}(G)\qquad\text{for every ordered pair }i\neq j.









3 Main results




3.1 The generic equivalence




Our main result shows that the decision and stopping orders are generically
equivalent, and that both are characterized by strict dominance of directed
pairwise KL divergences.




Theorem 1 (Generic multistate equivalence).



Let F and G be finite, full-support, pairwise identified experiments,
and suppose (F,G) is KL-generic. The following statements are equivalent.


(i) 



F\succeq_{\mathrm{d}}G.


(ii) 



F\succeq_{\mathrm{s}}G.


(iii) 



I_{ij}(F)>I_{ij}(G) for every ordered pair i\neq j.







The intuition is that directed pairwise KL divergence measures the rate at which an experiment accumulates evidence. When state i is true, each observation from E contributes, on average, I_{ij}(E) units of log-likelihood evidence in favor of i against j.
With endogenous stopping, the decision maker can
continue sampling while the accumulated evidence remains inconclusive and stop
once it becomes sufficiently decisive. Thus, to first order, the relevant
object is the rate at which evidence accumulates in each ordered state
direction. Condition (iii) therefore requires F to accumulate
evidence strictly faster than G against every alternative in every true
state.




This intuition drives both orders. If F has strictly larger pairwise KL
divergence in every direction, then any finite-output stopping G-policy can,
to first order, be reproduced from F using fewer observations in every
state. The nontrivial step is to turn this first-order evidence advantage into
an exact reproduction result that is uniform over policies and incurs only a
lower-order overhead. This is the technical core of the argument, established
in Theorem 2. Once the expected-sample budget is sufficiently
large, the first-order saving absorbs this overhead, so every terminal
experiment attainable from G is also attainable from F under the same
budget. Similarly, when observations are sufficiently cheap, nearly optimal
policies in nontrivial decision problems sample long enough for the same
first-order advantage to make F weakly more valuable than G. Conversely,
the stopping order implies the pairwise KL inequalities by requiring F to
reproduce long fixed samples from G, while the decision order recovers them
from binary testing problems that isolate individual pairs of states. We make
these arguments precise below.





3.2 Proof of the equivalence theorem




The key input is a uniform exact-conversion theorem. Define




\rho_{i}(F\to G):=\max_{j\neq i}\frac{I_{ij}(G)}{I_{ij}(F)}.



Thus \rho_{i}(F\to G) is the first-order conversion factor for reproducing G from F under state i. The maximum over j\neq i identifies the alternative against which reproducing the evidence generated by G is most demanding for F.




Theorem 2 (Uniform exact-conversion of stopping policies).



There exists a constant C_{F,G}<\infty such that every finite-output stopping
G-policy (T,Z) has an exact F-reproduction (N,Y) satisfying,
simultaneously for every state i,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T}.



The constant depends only on F and G, not on the policy or its finite
output space.





The theorem decomposes the expected source sample requirement into a first-order conversion term and a square-root remainder. In particular, the remainder is uniform over target policies and finite output spaces.
If condition (iii) holds, then \rho_{i}(F\to G)<1 for every state.
The resulting linear saving eventually dominates the square-root remainder.
Under a sufficiently large expected-sample budget, this yields exact
reproduction within the same budget; when observations are sufficiently cheap,
it yields weakly lower expected sampling costs for nearly optimal decision
policies. These arguments establish the sufficiency directions for the
stopping and decision orders. The converse directions recover the pairwise KL
inequalities from long fixed samples and pair-isolating binary decision
problems, respectively.




3.2.1 Pairwise KL divergence and the stopping order




We first show that condition (iii) is equivalent to large-budget
stopping dominance, i.e., (ii)\iff(iii).




Pairwise KL dominance implies the stopping order.




Suppose condition (iii) holds, and write
\rho_{i}:=\rho_{i}(F\to G)<1 and C:=C_{F,G}. Define




n_{0}:=\max_{i\in\Theta}\max\left\{1,\left(\frac{C}{1-\rho_{i}}\right)^{2}\right\}.



Fix n\geq n_{0} and H\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(G). By definition, some finite-output
stopping G-policy (T,Z) produces H and satisfies




t_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T\leq n\qquad\text{for every }i\in\Theta.



By Theorem 2, (T,Z) has an exact F-reproduction (N,Y)
satisfying




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N\leq\rho_{i}t_{i}+C\sqrt{t_{i}}\leq\rho_{i}n+C\sqrt{n}\leq n\qquad\text{for every }i\in\Theta,



where the last inequality follows from the definition of n_{0}. Hence
H\in\mathcal{P}_{n}(F). Since H was arbitrary,




\mathcal{P}_{n}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{P}_{n}(F)\qquad\text{for every }n\geq n_{0},



and therefore F\succeq_{\mathrm{s}}G.





The stopping order implies pairwise KL dominance.




Suppose F\succeq_{\mathrm{s}}G, and let n_{0} be a threshold from
Definition 4. Fix an ordered pair i\neq j and an integer
m\geq n_{0}. Consider the deterministic G-policy that takes exactly m
observations and reports the entire sample. Its terminal experiment is
G^{\otimes m}, so




G^{\otimes m}\in\mathcal{P}_{m}(G).



The stopping order therefore yields a finite-output stopping F-policy
(N,Y) that exactly reproduces G^{\otimes m} and satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{k}^{F}N\leq m for every state k. For the fixed ordered pair i\neq j,




\displaystyle mI_{ij}(G)
\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\!\left(G_{i}^{\otimes m}\middle\|G_{j}^{\otimes m}\right)




\displaystyle=\mathrm{D}\!\left(\mathcal{L}_{i}^{F}(Y)\middle\|\mathcal{L}_{j}^{F}(Y)\right)




\displaystyle\leq\mathrm{D}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{F}}\middle\|\mathbb{P}_{j}^{F}|_{\mathcal{F}_{N}^{F}}\right)




\displaystyle=I_{ij}(F)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N




\displaystyle\leq mI_{ij}(F).



The inequality follows from data processing because Y is
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{F}-measurable, while the stopped likelihood identity in
Lemma 5, equivalently Wald’s identity, gives the
subsequent equality. Thus I_{ij}(F)\geq I_{ij}(G).22

                This necessity argument is closely related to classical results on
comparison of experiments. Goel and DeGroot (1979) show that Blackwell
sufficiency implies corresponding KL-information inequalities. More
specifically for sequential experiments, Greenshtein and Torgersen (1997)
consider two stopping rules applied to a common i.i.d. source and show that
sufficiency of one stopped experiment for the other requires a weakly larger
expected sample size in every state. Our argument applies the same
information-accounting principle to the present setting.
Since i\neq j was arbitrary, all pairwise KL inequalities hold weakly.
KL-genericity rules out equality for every ordered pair, so all inequalities
are strict. This proves (ii)\iff(iii).






3.2.2 Pairwise KL divergence and the decision order




Next, we turn to the demand side and prove
(i)\iff(iii).




Pairwise KL dominance implies the decision order.




Suppose condition (iii) holds. Write
\rho_{i}:=\rho_{i}(F\to G)<1 and C:=C_{F,G}. Fix a finite decision problem
D=(\mu,A,u), let S:=\operatorname{supp}\mu, and define




A_{i}^{*}:=\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{a\in A}u(a,i).






If \bigcap_{i\in S}A_{i}^{*}\neq\varnothing, the decision maker can choose an
action that is optimal in every state in the support without sampling. Hence
both experiments attain the full-information payoff for every c>0. Suppose
instead that \bigcap_{i\in S}A_{i}^{*}=\varnothing.
For each i\in S, define




M_{i}:=\max\left\{1,\left(\frac{C}{1-\rho_{i}}\right)^{2}\right\}.






To apply the exact-conversion theorem, we need nearly optimal policies to sample long
enough that the linear saving (1-\rho_{i})\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T dominates the square-root
remainder. The following lemma, proved in the appendix, establishes this
uniformly across all states in the support of the prior.




Lemma 1 (Uniform effort in nontrivial decision problems).



Let E be finite, full support, and pairwise identified. Suppose
\bigcap_{i\in S}A_{i}^{*}=\varnothing. For every finite vector
M\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{S}, there exists c_{M}>0 such that, whenever 0<c<c_{M}, every
stopping E-policy (T,a_{T}) whose value is within c^{2} of U_{c}(E;D)
satisfies




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T>M_{i}\qquad\text{for every }i\in S.







Apply Lemma 1 to E=G and the vector (M_{i})_{i\in S}.
There exists \bar{c}_{D}>0 such that, whenever 0<c<\bar{c}_{D}, every
c^{2}-optimal stopping G-policy satisfies




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T>M_{i}\qquad\text{for every }i\in S.



Fix such a c. For each m, let




\varepsilon_{m}:=\min\{c^{2},1/m\},



and choose a stopping G-policy (T_{m},a_{m}) with value at least
U_{c}(G;D)-\varepsilon_{m}. Writing




t_{i,m}:=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T_{m},



we have \varepsilon_{m}\leq c^{2}, so Lemma 1 implies
t_{i,m}>M_{i} for every i\in S.




By Theorem 2, (T_{m},a_{m}) has an exact F-reproduction
(N_{m},a^{\prime}_{m}) satisfying




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{m}\leq\rho_{i}t_{i,m}+C\sqrt{t_{i,m}}\leq t_{i,m}\qquad\text{for every }i\in S,



where the last inequality follows from the definition of M_{i}. Exact
reproduction preserves the distribution of the terminal action in every state,
while expected sample size is weakly lower in every state in the support of
the prior. Therefore




\displaystyle U_{c}(F;D)
\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in S}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}\bigl[u(a^{\prime}_{m},i)-cN_{m}\bigr]




\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i\in S}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\bigl[u(a_{m},i)-cT_{m}\bigr]




\displaystyle\geq U_{c}(G;D)-\varepsilon_{m}.



Letting m\to\infty yields




U_{c}(F;D)\geq U_{c}(G;D).



Since D was arbitrary, F\succeq_{\mathrm{d}}G.





The decision order implies pairwise KL dominance.




Suppose F\succeq_{\mathrm{d}}G. To recover the pairwise KL inequalities, fix
distinct states i,j and p\in(0,1), and consider the binary testing problem
D_{ij}^{p}. The prior assigns probability p to state i, probability
1-p to state j, and zero probability to all other states. There are two
actions, a_{i} and a_{j}, each yielding payoff one when it correctly identifies
the state and zero otherwise in states i and j; their payoffs in all other
states may be set equal, say to one. Define the corresponding Bayes risk by




R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p}):=1-U_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p}).






The relevant asymptotic is closely related to the classical theory of binary
sequential testing. Wald and Wolfowitz (1948) establish the expected-sample
optimality of the sequential probability-ratio test for two simple hypotheses,
while Kiefer and Sacks (1963) develop asymptotically Bayes-optimal sequential
procedures for small sampling costs. We state the asymptotic in the form needed
here and prove it in the appendix for completeness.




Lemma 2 (Pair-isolating low-cost tests).



For every finite, full-support experiment E that identifies i and j,




\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\frac{R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p})}{c\log(1/c)}=\frac{p}{I_{ij}(E)}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}(E)}.

(1)






Applying the decision order to D_{ij}^{p} gives, for all sufficiently small
c,




R_{c}(F;D_{ij}^{p})\leq R_{c}(G;D_{ij}^{p}).



Dividing by c\log(1/c), letting c\downarrow 0, and applying
Lemma 2 yields




\frac{p}{I_{ij}(F)}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}(F)}\leq\frac{p}{I_{ij}(G)}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}(G)}.



Since this inequality holds for every p\in(0,1), letting p\uparrow 1 gives




I_{ij}(F)\geq I_{ij}(G).



Because the ordered pair i\neq j was arbitrary, all directed pairwise KL
inequalities hold weakly. KL-genericity rules out equality for every ordered
pair, so all inequalities are strict and condition (iii) follows.
This proves (i)\iff(iii).








4 Discussion




We compare our two orders with the fixed-sample orders of
Moscarini and Smith (2002) and Mu et al. (2021). For finite
experiments F and G, write F\succeq_{B}G for Blackwell dominance.
Following Mu et al. (2021), write
F\succeq_{\mathrm{MPST}}G if




F^{\otimes n}\succeq_{B}G^{\otimes n}



for every sufficiently large n. Write F\succeq_{\mathrm{MS}}G for the
Moscarini–Smith order, which requires that, in every finite-action Bayesian
decision problem, F^{\otimes n} yield weakly higher value than
G^{\otimes n} for every sufficiently large n, with the threshold allowed
to depend on the decision problem.




For KL-generic pairs, the implication structure is




\begin{gathered}\text{Blackwell dominance}\\
\Downarrow\ \text{(strict)}\\
\text{Blackwell dominance in large samples (MPST)}\\[-2.84526pt]
\begin{array}[]{c@{\qquad\qquad}c}\Downarrow\ \text{(strict)}&\Downarrow\ \text{(strict)}\\[2.84526pt]
\text{stopping order}&\text{Moscarini--Smith order}.\end{array}\end{gathered}



Every displayed implication is strict, and neither of the two bottom orders
contains the other.




4.1 Moscarini–Smith: decision value with a fixed sample




The Moscarini–Smith order and our decision order both allow the asymptotic threshold
to depend on the decision problem. Their difference is therefore the sampling
technology. Moscarini and Smith impose a deterministic horizon n on every
history, whereas we charge a constant cost per observation and allow the
decision maker to stop endogenously.




This distinction changes the statistical quantity governing the comparison.
With a deterministic sample size, asymptotic decision loss is driven by
decision-error probabilities. In the binary case, away from ties, the
Moscarini–Smith order is therefore characterized by Chernoff information
(Moscarini and Smith, 2002; Chernoff, 1952). Under endogenous stopping, what matters instead is the
expected sample size needed to accumulate evidence. When state i is true, one
observation from experiment E contributes mean log evidence
I_{ij}(E) against state j. Since different priors and payoff functions can
make different ordered pairs relevant, our decision order depends on the full
array




\bigl(I_{ij}(E)\bigr)_{i\neq j}.



Moreover, Theorem 1 shows that the same array governs exact
information production under stopping. This requirement is absent from the
Moscarini–Smith order, which compares only downstream decision values.





4.2 MPST: Blackwell dominance with a fixed sample




The MPST order strengthens the fixed-sample comparison by requiring
F^{\otimes n} to Blackwell dominate G^{\otimes n} for every sufficiently
large deterministic n. Hence the same garbling must work for all downstream
decision problems at each sufficiently large sample size, which immediately
implies the Moscarini–Smith order.




For generic binary experiments, Mu et al. (2021) characterize
this order by dominance of the full Rényi spectrum in both state directions
(Rényi, 1961). A deterministic horizon constrains the entire
likelihood-ratio distribution, including rare realizations, and therefore
requires more than control of its mean log increment.




Our stopping order relaxes precisely this horizon constraint. If N is a
stopping time based on repeated observations from F, Wald’s identity gives,
for every ordered pair i\neq j,




\mathbb{E}_{i}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{N}\log\frac{F_{i}(X_{t})}{F_{j}(X_{t})}\right]=I_{ij}(F)\mathbb{E}_{i}N.



Thus expected evidence is linear in expected sample size. The reproducing
policy may spend more observations on histories requiring unusually large
likelihood ratios and fewer on histories that terminate early, subject only to
the common expected-sample budget in each state. The exact-conversion theorem shows
that, to first order, this flexibility reduces the exact-production
constraints to the directed pairwise KL divergences. More generally, for any KL-generic pair of finite, full-support, pairwise identified experiments, the MPST order implies our
stopping order. Indeed, data processing and product additivity imply I_{ij}(F)\geq I_{ij}(G) for every ordered pair i\neq j; KL-genericity makes every inequality strict, and Theorem 1 then gives F\succeq_{\mathrm{s}}G.






5 Conclusion




Blackwell’s comparison treats an experiment as an information object delivered
once. When an experiment is instead a repeatable source from which observations
can be acquired sequentially, the relevant comparison changes. Our results show
that, generically, such sources nevertheless admit a simple and robust ranking.
One experiment yields at least as much value in every decision problem once
observations are sufficiently cheap if and only if, under sufficiently large
expected-sample budgets, it can reproduce every terminal experiment attainable from the other source. Both comparisons are characterized by the same
condition: the experiment has a strictly larger directed pairwise KL divergence for every ordered pair
of states.




The equivalence gives directed pairwise KL divergences an operational interpretation that goes beyond their familiar role in sequential testing. The quantities I_{ij}(E) are the rates at which a source produces evidence for state i against state j, and \rho_{i}(F\to G) is the resulting first-order conversion factor under
state i. Our exact-conversion theorem shows that these rates are sufficient not only to reproduce a particular test or decision rule, but to reproduce exactly the terminal experiment generated by any finite-output stopping policy, with an overhead that is lower order and uniform over target policies.




This characterization also clarifies why the way information is acquired is part of the comparison. A deterministic horizon forces the same number of observations to be collected on every history, leaving the tails of the likelihood-ratio distribution relevant and leading to criteria based on Chernoff information or the Rényi spectrum. Endogenous stopping permits sampling effort to be shifted across histories: easy histories can terminate early while difficult histories receive more observations. Under an expected-sample budget, what survives to first order is therefore the mean rate of evidence accumulation. In a multistate problem this rate is inherently directional, so there is generally no single scalar measure of information quality; each ordered pair of states represents a potentially binding informational bottleneck.




The boundaries of our characterization point to questions where richer features of experiments should reappear. When I_{ij}(F)=I_{ij}(G) for some ordered pair i\neq j, the first-order KL comparison no longer determines the ranking, and lower-order features of the likelihood process may matter. A second-order theory of sequential experiment comparison would therefore describe what replaces strict KL dominance on these boundaries. Likewise, restrictions between deterministic sampling and unrestricted stopping—for example, hard horizon constraints, restrictions on stopping times, or costs that depend on the sampling history—may generate intermediate notions of informativeness. Allowing the decision maker to choose adaptively among multiple sources would add a further allocation problem: information could be directed toward whichever state distinctions are currently most valuable.

k\in\Theta,




q_{k,c}:=\min\{E_{k}(x_{+})^{m_{c}},E_{k}(x_{-})^{m_{c}}\}>0.



Hence, conditional on any history that has not yet exited,




\mathbb{P}_{k}^{E}(\tau_{c}\leq n+m_{c}\mid\mathcal{F}_{n},\tau_{c}>n)\geq q_{k,c}.



Iterating this bound over blocks of length m_{c} gives




\mathbb{P}_{k}^{E}(\tau_{c}>rm_{c})\leq(1-q_{k,c})^{r},\qquad r=0,1,2,\ldots.



Therefore




\mathbb{E}_{k}^{E}\tau_{c}\leq m_{c}\sum_{r\geq 0}(1-q_{k,c})^{r}=\frac{m_{c}}{q_{k,c}}<\infty\qquad\text{for every }k\in\Theta.

(7)


Thus the test is an integrable stopping policy.




We next bound its error probabilities. At deterministic time n, the
likelihood ratio of the observed history under state i relative to state
j is e^{S_{n}}. If state-independent auxiliary randomization is used,
it has the same law in both states and therefore does not alter this ratio.
Let




A_{c}^{-}:=\{S_{\tau_{c}}\leq-h_{c}\}.



Since A_{c}^{-}\cap\{\tau_{c}=n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n},




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A_{c}^{-})
\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\!\left[e^{S_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{A_{c}^{-}\cap\{\tau_{c}=n\}}\right]




\displaystyle\leq e^{-h_{c}}=c.



Similarly,




\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}(S_{\tau_{c}}\geq h_{c})\leq c.






Because |Y_{t}|\leq B, the overshoot at either boundary is at most B:




h_{c}\leq S_{\tau_{c}}\leq h_{c}+B\quad\text{on an upper exit},



and




-h_{c}-B\leq S_{\tau_{c}}\leq-h_{c}\quad\text{on a lower exit}.



Under state i, the lower-exit probability is at most c, so




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{\tau_{c}}=h_{c}+O(1).

(8)


Indeed, the contribution of the wrong-boundary event is
O(ch_{c})=o(1). Symmetrically,




\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}[-S_{\tau_{c}}]=h_{c}+O(1).






For completeness, the required form of Wald’s identity follows directly in
this setting. By (7) and |Y_{t}|\leq B,




\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[|Y_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{c}\geq t\}}\right]\leq B\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{c}<\infty.



Hence expectation and summation may be interchanged. Since
\{\tau_{c}\geq t\} is determined before X_{t} is observed,




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{\tau_{c}}
\displaystyle=\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[Y_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{c}\geq t\}}\right]




\displaystyle=I_{ij}\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\tau_{c}\geq t)




\displaystyle=I_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{c}.



Combining this identity with (8) gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{c}=\frac{h_{c}}{I_{ij}}+O(1).



The symmetric calculation gives




\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\tau_{c}=\frac{h_{c}}{I_{ji}}+O(1).






The risk of this policy in D_{ij}^{p} is therefore




\displaystyle R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p})
\displaystyle\leq p\,\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(S_{\tau_{c}}\leq-h_{c})+(1-p)\,\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}(S_{\tau_{c}}\geq h_{c})




\displaystyle\qquad+c\left[p\,\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{c}+(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\tau_{c}\right]




\displaystyle=ch_{c}\left[\frac{p}{I_{ij}}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}}\right]+o(ch_{c}).



Consequently,




\limsup_{c\downarrow 0}\frac{R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p})}{ch_{c}}\leq\frac{p}{I_{ij}}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}}.

(9)






Lower bound.




For each c, choose an integrable policy (T_{c},a_{c}) whose risk is
within c^{2} of R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p}). The upper bound just proved implies
that this policy has risk O(ch_{c}). Define its two error probabilities by




\alpha_{c}:=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(a_{c}=a_{j}),\qquad\beta_{c}:=\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}(a_{c}=a_{i}).



Since p\in(0,1) is fixed and the error probabilities enter the risk with
positive weights,




\alpha_{c}=O(ch_{c}),\qquad\beta_{c}=O(ch_{c}).

(10)





We next relate these error probabilities to expected sample size. Let \mathcal{F}_{T_{c}} denote the information available when the policy stops. At
deterministic time n, the likelihood ratio under states i and j is
e^{S_{n}}; as above, any state-independent auxiliary randomization contributes
no factor. If A\in\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}, then
A\cap\{T_{c}=n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}, and therefore




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A)
\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\!\left[e^{S_{n}}\mathbf{1}_{A\cap\{T_{c}=n\}}\right]




\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\!\left[e^{S_{T_{c}}}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right].



Thus




\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}}=e^{S_{T_{c}}}.



It follows that




\mathrm{D}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}\middle\|\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}\right)=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{T_{c}}.






Because the policy is integrable, \mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}<\infty. Since the increments
are bounded,




\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[|Y_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{c}\geq t\}}\right]\leq B\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}<\infty.



Hence




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{T_{c}}
\displaystyle=\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[Y_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{T_{c}\geq t\}}\right]




\displaystyle=I_{ij}\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(T_{c}\geq t)




\displaystyle=I_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}.



Consequently,




\mathrm{D}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}\middle\|\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T_{c}}}\right)=I_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}.






The data-processing inequality for KL divergence says that a measurable
transformation cannot increase KL divergence. Apply it to the map from the
stopped history to the terminal action a_{c}. Under state i, the
probability of action a_{i} is 1-\alpha_{c}; under state j, it is
\beta_{c}. Thus




I_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}\geq d_{2}(1-\alpha_{c}\|\beta_{c}),

(11)


where




d_{2}(q\|r):=q\log\frac{q}{r}+(1-q)\log\frac{1-q}{1-r}



is binary KL divergence. Similarly,




I_{ji}\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}T_{c}\geq d_{2}(1-\beta_{c}\|\alpha_{c}).






We now lower-bound the first binary KL divergence. Directly from its
definition,




\displaystyle d_{2}(1-\alpha_{c}\|\beta_{c})
\displaystyle=(1-\alpha_{c})\log\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}




\displaystyle\quad+(1-\alpha_{c})\log(1-\alpha_{c})+\alpha_{c}\log\alpha_{c}




\displaystyle\quad-\alpha_{c}\log(1-\beta_{c}).



The final term is nonnegative, while




(1-\alpha_{c})\log(1-\alpha_{c})+\alpha_{c}\log\alpha_{c}\geq-\log 2.



Hence




d_{2}(1-\alpha_{c}\|\beta_{c})\geq(1-\alpha_{c})\log\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}-\log 2.

(12)


If \beta_{c}=0, the left side is +\infty, so the desired lower bound is
immediate. Otherwise, (10) implies that for some
constant C<\infty,




\beta_{c}\leq Cch_{c}



for all sufficiently small c. Therefore




\log\frac{1}{\beta_{c}}\geq\log\frac{1}{Cch_{c}}=h_{c}-\log h_{c}-O(1)=h_{c}-o(h_{c}).



Since \alpha_{c}=o(1), (12) yields




d_{2}(1-\alpha_{c}\|\beta_{c})\geq h_{c}-o(h_{c}).



Combining this with (11) gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}\geq\frac{h_{c}}{I_{ij}}-o(h_{c}).



By symmetry,




\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}T_{c}\geq\frac{h_{c}}{I_{ji}}-o(h_{c}).






The decision-error terms in the risk are nonnegative. Hence





\displaystyle p\,\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(a_{c}=a_{j})+(1-p)\,\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}(a_{c}=a_{i})




\displaystyle\qquad+c\left[p\,\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T_{c}+(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}T_{c}\right]




\displaystyle\geq ch_{c}\left[\frac{p}{I_{ij}}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}}\right]-o(ch_{c}).



Since (T_{c},a_{c}) was chosen within c^{2}=o(ch_{c}) of the optimum,




\liminf_{c\downarrow 0}\frac{R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p})}{ch_{c}}\geq\frac{p}{I_{ij}}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}}.

(13)





Combining (9) and (13), and recalling that
h_{c}=\log(1/c), gives




\lim_{c\downarrow 0}\frac{R_{c}(E;D_{ij}^{p})}{c\log(1/c)}=\frac{p}{I_{ij}(E)}+\frac{1-p}{I_{ji}(E)},



which is (1).
∎






Appendix B Reusable facts for the conversion proof




This appendix collects facts used in the proof of
Theorem 2. They are stated in the order in which they first
enter that proof. Throughout Appendices B and C, a constant
written C_{E} (or c_{E}) depends only on E and the fixed state set, and a
constant written C_{F,G} depends only on the pair (F,G) and that state
set. Such constants may change from line to line. The assumptions in the main text imply




0<I_{ij}(E)<\infty\qquad(i\neq j)



for every experiment E considered below: strict positivity follows from
pairwise identification and finiteness follows because the signal space is finite and has full support.




Fix such an experiment E, and write \mathcal{X}_{E} for its finite
signal space. Let X_{1},X_{2},\ldots be its i.i.d. signals,
and let \mathcal{F}_{n} contain X_{1},\ldots,X_{n} together with the auxiliary
random draws revealed by time n. Every auxiliary draw is independent of
the state and of the entire signal stream. For i\neq j, define




S_{ij}^{E}(n):=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{E_{i}(X_{t})}{E_{j}(X_{t})},\qquad S_{ij}^{E}(0):=0.

(14)


Every
increment in (14) is bounded, and under state i its mean
is I_{ij}(E).




B.1 Classification and simultaneous passage




For \ell\geq 1 and a signal history
x_{1:\ell}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{\ell}), define




\widehat{K}_{\ell}(x_{1:\ell}):=\text{the element with the smallest index of }\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{r\in\Theta}\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\log E_{r}(x_{t}).

(15)


Thus \widehat{K}_{\ell}:=\widehat{K}_{\ell}(X_{1:\ell}) is a
maximum-likelihood estimator. For every ordered pair i\neq j, also put




a_{ij}:=\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{E}}\log\frac{E_{i}(x)}{E_{j}(x)},\qquad b_{ij}:=\max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{E}}\log\frac{E_{i}(x)}{E_{j}(x)},\qquad r_{ij}:=b_{ij}-a_{ij}.



Full support and finiteness of \mathcal{X}_{E} make these quantities finite.
Moreover, r_{ij}>0. Indeed, if the log-likelihood ratio were constant
in x, normalization of E_{i} and E_{j} would force that constant to
be zero and hence E_{i}=E_{j}, contrary to pairwise identification.




Lemma 3 (Exponential classification).



There are constants c_{E},C_{E}>0 such that




\max_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}_{\ell}\neq i)\leq C_{E}e^{-c_{E}\ell}\qquad(\ell\geq 1).

(16)






Proof of Lemma 3.



Fix the true state i, and for j\neq i write




Y_{t}^{ij}:=\log\frac{E_{i}(X_{t})}{E_{j}(X_{t})}.



Under state i, the variables Y_{t}^{ij} are independent, take values in
[a_{ij},b_{ij}], and have mean




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}Y_{t}^{ij}=\sum_{x}E_{i}(x)\log\frac{E_{i}(x)}{E_{j}(x)}=I_{ij}(E)>0.



A misclassification in favor of j can occur only if the accumulated evidence for
i against j has failed to turn positive, so




\{\widehat{K}_{\ell}\neq i\}\subseteq\bigcup_{j\neq i}\{S_{ij}^{E}(\ell)\leq 0\}.



Fix j\neq i. The \ell summands are independent and each takes values in an
interval of length r_{ij}, so Hoeffding’s inequality gives




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}\!\left(S_{ij}^{E}(\ell)\leq 0\right)
\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}\!\left(\sum_{t=1}^{\ell}\bigl(Y_{t}^{ij}-I_{ij}(E)\bigr)\leq-\ell I_{ij}(E)\right)




\displaystyle\leq\exp\!\left\{-\frac{2\ell I_{ij}(E)^{2}}{r_{ij}^{2}}\right\}.



Each state has d=|\Theta|-1 alternatives, and there are finitely many ordered
pairs, so the constant




c_{E}:=\min_{i\neq j}\frac{2I_{ij}(E)^{2}}{r_{ij}^{2}}



is strictly positive. Combining the event inclusion with the Hoeffding bound
and taking a union bound over the d alternatives,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}_{\ell}\neq i)\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{2\ell I_{ij}(E)^{2}}{r_{ij}^{2}}\right\}\leq de^{-c_{E}\ell},



and taking the maximum over i proves (16) with C_{E}=d.
∎





Fix a state i. Given a boundary vector
b=(b_{j})_{j\neq i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\Theta\setminus\{i\}}, define




\tau_{i}(b):=\inf\{n\geq 0:S_{ij}^{E}(n)\geq b_{j}\text{ for every }j\neq i\},\qquad\inf\varnothing:=\infty,

(17)


and define its deterministic first-order benchmark by




t_{i}(b):=\max_{j\neq i}\frac{(b_{j})^{+}}{I_{ij}(E)}.






Lemma 4 (Simultaneous passage).



There is a constant C_{E}<\infty, independent of i and b, such that




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{i}(b)\leq t_{i}(b)+C_{E}\sqrt{1+t_{i}(b)}+C_{E}.

(18)






Proof of Lemma 4.



Define the source-dependent constants




\underline{I}_{E}:=\min_{r\neq q}I_{rq}(E)>0,\qquad\overline{r}_{E}:=\max_{r\neq q}r_{rq}<\infty,\qquad\gamma_{E}:=\frac{2\underline{I}_{E}^{2}}{\overline{r}_{E}^{2}}>0.






Write t_{0}:=t_{i}(b) and n_{0}:=\lceil t_{0}\rceil. If n_{0}=0, then
(b_{j})^{+}=0, and hence \tau_{i}(b)=0. The
desired bound is immediate. We may therefore suppose that n_{0}\geq 1.




Fix an integer s\geq 0. For each j\neq i,




n_{0}I_{ij}(E)\geq t_{0}I_{ij}(E)\geq(b_{j})^{+}\geq b_{j}.



Therefore, with n=n_{0}+s,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{ij}^{E}(n)-b_{j}=nI_{ij}(E)-b_{j}\geq sI_{ij}(E).

(19)





If \tau_{i}(b)>n then the score vector has not yet entered the region \{S^{E}_{ij}(n)\geq b_{j}\ \forall j\neq i\},
so some coordinate S_{ij}^{E}(n) still lies below b_{j}. A union bound over the
lagging coordinate, the mean gap (19), Hoeffding’s
inequality applied to n independent increments of range r_{ij}, and finally
the uniform bounds I_{ij}(E)\geq\underline{I}_{E} and
r_{ij}\leq\overline{r}_{E} over the d alternatives give




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\tau_{i}(b)>n_{0}+s)
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(S_{ij}^{E}(n)<b_{j})




\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}\!\left(S_{ij}^{E}(n)-\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{ij}^{E}(n)\leq-sI_{ij}(E)\right)




\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{2s^{2}I_{ij}(E)^{2}}{nr_{ij}^{2}}\right\}




\displaystyle\leq d\exp\!\left\{-\gamma_{E}\frac{s^{2}}{n_{0}+s}\right\}.

(20)


The right side vanishes as s grows, which incidentally shows that
\tau_{i}(b)<\infty almost surely.




Since \tau_{i}(b)
is nonnegative and integer-valued, its tail-sum formula is




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{i}(b)=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\tau_{i}(b)>m).



The first n_{0} terms are each at most one. Reindexing the remaining terms
as m=n_{0}+s and applying (20) therefore gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{i}(b)\leq n_{0}+d\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left\{-\gamma_{E}\frac{s^{2}}{n_{0}+s}\right\}.

(21)


For 0\leq s\leq n_{0}, one has n_{0}+s\leq 2n_{0}. Since the exponential is
decreasing in its nonnegative exponent,




\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{n_{0}}\exp\!\left\{-\gamma_{E}\frac{s^{2}}{n_{0}+s}\right\}
\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{\gamma_{E}s^{2}}{2n_{0}}\right\}




\displaystyle\leq 1+\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\!\left\{-\frac{\gamma_{E}x^{2}}{2n_{0}}\right\}\,\mathrm{d}x




\displaystyle=1+\sqrt{\frac{\pi n_{0}}{2\gamma_{E}}}.



The integral comparison is legitimate because the summand decreases on
[0,\infty). In the opposite range s>n_{0} we have n_{0}+s<2s, so
s^{2}/(n_{0}+s)>s/2, and enlarging the sum by starting it at s=1 gives




\sum_{s=n_{0}+1}^{\infty}\exp\!\left\{-\gamma_{E}\frac{s^{2}}{n_{0}+s}\right\}\leq\sum_{s=n_{0}+1}^{\infty}e^{-\gamma_{E}s/2}\leq\frac{e^{-\gamma_{E}/2}}{1-e^{-\gamma_{E}/2}}.



Substituting the last two bounds into (21), we obtain
finite constants A_{E},B_{E}, depending only on E, for which




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{i}(b)\leq n_{0}+A_{E}\sqrt{n_{0}}+B_{E}.



Finally, n_{0}=\lceil t_{0}\rceil\leq t_{0}+1 and
\sqrt{n_{0}}\leq\sqrt{1+t_{0}}. Hence




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\tau_{i}(b)\leq t_{0}+1+A_{E}\sqrt{1+t_{0}}+B_{E}.



Choosing C_{E}\geq\max\{A_{E},B_{E}+1\} proves
(18). All constants are uniform in i and b, as
required.
∎






B.2 Likelihoods at a stopping time




Put \mathcal{F}_{\infty}:=\sigma(\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{F}_{n}).
For a stopping time T, its stopped sigma-field is




\mathcal{F}_{T}:=\left\{A\in\mathcal{F}_{\infty}:A\cap\{T\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}\text{ for every }n\geq 0\right\}.






Lemma 5 (Stopped likelihood identity).



If T is a stopping time that is almost surely finite under states i and
j, then on the stopped sigma-field \mathcal{F}_{T},




\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}}=e^{S_{ij}^{E}(T)}.

(22)


If in addition \mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T<\infty, then




\mathrm{D}\!\left(\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}\middle\|\mathbb{P}_{j}^{E}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}}\right)=I_{ij}(E)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T.

(23)






Proof of Lemma 5.



At deterministic time n, the likelihood ratio of the signal history under
state i relative to state j is e^{S_{ij}^{E}(n)}. The auxiliary
randomization is state-independent and therefore contributes no likelihood
factor. If A\in\mathcal{F}_{T}, then
A\cap\{T=n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}.
Consequently,




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A)
\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A\cap\{T=n\})




\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\!\left[e^{S_{ij}^{E}(n)}\mathbf{1}_{A\cap\{T=n\}}\right]




\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{j}^{E}\!\left[e^{S_{ij}^{E}(T)}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right].



Almost sure finiteness of T is used at both ends: under state i to
decompose A over the disjoint events \{T=n\}, and under state j to
recombine them after substituting S_{ij}^{E}(T)=S_{ij}^{E}(n) on \{T=n\}. The
middle step is the deterministic-time likelihood ratio on the
\mathcal{F}_{n}-measurable event A\cap\{T=n\}. Since e^{S_{ij}^{E}(T)} is a stopped
adapted process, hence \mathcal{F}_{T}-measurable, holding the displayed identity for
every A\in\mathcal{F}_{T} identifies the Radon–Nikodym derivative and proves
(22).




For the KL identity, put




Y_{t}:=\log\frac{E_{i}(X_{t})}{E_{j}(X_{t})}.



There is a constant L_{E}<\infty with |Y_{t}|\leq L_{E}. If
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T<\infty, then
the pathwise identity




S_{ij}^{E}(T)=\sum_{t\geq 1}Y_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}



is absolutely integrable: the tail-sum formula gives




\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[|Y_{t}|\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}\right]\leq L_{E}\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(T\geq t)=L_{E}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T<\infty,



so Fubini’s theorem applies to the sum. Moreover \{T\geq t\}\in\mathcal{F}_{t-1},
while X_{t} is independent of \mathcal{F}_{t-1} under state i, whence




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}(Y_{t}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})=I_{ij}(E).



Because \mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}} is known before X_{t} is drawn, the tower property
turns the last identity into
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}[Y_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}]=I_{ij}(E)\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(T\geq t). Summing over t and
using the tail-sum formula once more,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}S_{ij}^{E}(T)=\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[Y_{t}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}\right]=I_{ij}(E)\sum_{t\geq 1}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(T\geq t)=I_{ij}(E)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}T.



The log of the density in (22) is
S_{ij}^{E}(T), which the preceding absolute-integrability calculation shows
to be integrable. Taking its state-i expectation is therefore legitimate
and proves (23).
∎






B.3 Fresh tails and pasted policies




The next fact is the filtration device used whenever one policy is started after another has stopped.




Let \sigma be a stopping time for the augmented E-filtration that is
almost surely finite in every state, and let K be an
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}-measurable random variable with countable range
\mathcal{K}. For each k\in\mathcal{K}, let (N_{k},Y_{k}) be a
stopping policy defined on a fresh E-signal stream, with all Y_{k} taking
values in one common measurable output space.




We now define precisely how fresh auxiliary randomization is added. Implement
the countable family of policies using one product seed U, whose
coordinates supply all random draws required by the different policies. On a
product extension, take U independent of the pre-existing
\mathcal{F}_{\infty} and with a law that does not depend on the state. If
(\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B}_{U}) is its measurable space, define the enlarged
filtration by




\mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}:=\mathcal{F}_{n}\vee\sigma\!\left(\{\sigma=r\}\cap\{U\in B\}:0\leq r\leq n,\ B\in\mathcal{B}_{U}\right).

(24)


Thus the fresh seed is not in the pre-restart sigma-field
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}; on the event \{\sigma=r\}, it is revealed at time r
in the enlarged filtration. For a deterministic r, denote by
(N_{k}^{(r)},Y_{k}^{(r)}) the result of feeding policy k the signal tail
(X_{r+1},X_{r+2},\ldots) and the appropriate coordinates of U. On
\{K=k\}, define the selected tail
duration and output by




(\bar{N},\bar{Y}):=(N_{k}^{(\sigma)},Y_{k}^{(\sigma)}).



If a zero-duration branch is needed, extend \mathcal{K} by an idle value
\dagger, fix one output y_{\dagger} in the common output space, and let the
idle policy be (N_{\dagger},Y_{\dagger})=(0,y_{\dagger}).




Lemma 6 (Pasting after a stopping time).



Relative to (\mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}), the pasted terminal time \sigma+\bar{N} is a
stopping time, and \bar{Y} is measurable at that time. Moreover, for every
state i, every bounded measurable \varphi, and every
k\in\mathcal{K},




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}[\varphi(\bar{N},\bar{Y})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\varphi(N_{k},Y_{k})\quad\text{on }\{K=k\}.

(25)


If N_{k} is integrable under state i, then in particular




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}[\bar{N}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}N_{k}\quad\text{on }\{K=k\}.

(26)






Proof of Lemma 6.



We first prove that the unused signal tail is fresh. Fix m\geq 1, a bounded
function \psi of m signals, and an event A\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}. Since
A\cap\{\sigma=n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}, independence of the post-n signal block
from \mathcal{F}_{n} gives





\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{A}\psi(X_{\sigma+1},\ldots,X_{\sigma+m})\right]




\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{A\cap\{\sigma=n\}}\psi(X_{n+1},\ldots,X_{n+m})\right]




\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A\cap\{\sigma=n\})\mathbb{E}_{E_{i}^{\otimes m}}\psi




\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(A)\mathbb{E}_{E_{i}^{\otimes m}}\psi.

(27)


Here the outer equalities only partition and recombine the disjoint events
A\cap\{\sigma=n\}, which is legitimate because \sigma is almost surely
finite. Thus every finite cylinder of the unused tail carries its product-law
probability and is independent of \mathcal{F}_{\sigma}, so the tail itself is
independent of \mathcal{F}_{\sigma} with law E_{i}^{\otimes\mathbb{N}}. The fresh
auxiliary seed was chosen independently of both the old sigma-field and the
signal stream, so adjoining it preserves this conclusion.




For fixed k, the pair (N_{k},Y_{k}) is a measurable function of a fresh signal
stream and its fresh randomization, so the preceding paragraph delivers its
fresh-start law conditional on \mathcal{F}_{\sigma}; restricting to \{K=k\}, which is
legitimate because K is \mathcal{F}_{\sigma}-measurable, proves
(25). For (26), apply what we have
just proved to the bounded function (n,y)\mapsto n\wedge m, which gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}[\bar{N}\wedge m\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}]=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}(N_{k}\wedge m)\quad\text{on }\{K=k\}.



Letting m\to\infty and applying conditional monotone convergence on the
left and ordinary monotone convergence on the right proves the asserted mean
identity.




It remains to verify the stopping and output measurability claims. For every
deterministic n,




\{\sigma+\bar{N}\leq n\}=\bigcup_{r=0}^{n}\ \bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\left(\{\sigma=r,K=k\}\cap\{N_{k}^{(r)}\leq n-r\}\right).

(28)


Since K is \mathcal{F}_{\sigma}-measurable,
\{\sigma=r,K=k\}\in\mathcal{F}_{r}. Since the shifted policy is adapted to the
tail after r and its seed is revealed on \{\sigma=r\}, the
intersection




\{\sigma=r,K=k\}\cap\{N_{k}^{(r)}\leq n-r\}



belongs to \mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}. Here the signal portion is in \mathcal{F}_{n}, while the
seed portion is measurable with respect to the generators in
(24) having restart time r.
Every event inside the countable union in
(28) is therefore in \mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}, proving that
\sigma+\bar{N} is a stopping time.




Finally, for any measurable set B in the output space,




\displaystyle\{\bar{Y}\in B,\ \sigma+\bar{N}\leq n\}=\bigcup_{r=0}^{n}\ \bigcup_{k\in\mathcal{K}}\bigl(
\displaystyle\{\sigma=r,K=k\}\cap\{N_{k}^{(r)}\leq n-r,\ Y_{k}^{(r)}\in B\}\bigr).



For the same reason, every intersection inside this union is in
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}: the stopped-output event depends only on the signal tail through
time n and on the seed revealed at restart time r. Thus the left side
is in \mathcal{F}_{n}^{+} for every n, which is exactly measurability of \bar{Y} at
\sigma+\bar{N}. The zero-duration special case is included by taking its
duration and output to be determined at the selection time.
∎





After a paste, one may relabel (\mathcal{F}_{n}^{+}) as the current filtration and
repeat the construction with another independent product seed. This is the
convention used in the stagewise recursion later.





B.4 Behavioral policies




Let (T,Z) be a finite-output stopping E-policy. Write \mathcal{Z} for the
finite range of Z, and define the sets of finite histories and possible
decisions by




\mathcal{H}_{E}:=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{X}_{E}^{n},\qquad\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{Z}}:=\{\mathsf{CONTINUE}\}\cup\{(\mathsf{STOP},z):z\in\mathcal{Z}\}.






Lemma 7 (Behavioral representation).



There are state-independent probability distributions q_{h} on
\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{Z}}, one for each h\in\mathcal{H}_{E}, whose
behavioral policy has the same joint law of
(T,Z,X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}) as the original policy in every state.





Proof of Lemma 7.



Let U denote the original policy’s entire auxiliary random seed. Its law
does not depend on the state. Let (\mathcal{U},\mathcal{B}_{U},\nu_{U}) be its
probability space. For every n\geq 0, the original policy has a measurable
decision map




d_{n}:\mathcal{X}_{E}^{n}\times\mathcal{U}\longrightarrow\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{Z}},



where d_{n}(h,u) is the decision after history h when the seed is u.
Define the map arbitrarily when the policy would already have stopped at a
strict prefix. On the original probability space, the actual decision is
D_{n}=d_{n}(X_{1:n},U).




Fix h=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}), and write
h_{t}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t}), with h_{0}=\varnothing. Define the seed event




C_{h}:=\{u\in\mathcal{U}:d_{t}(h_{t},u)=\mathsf{CONTINUE}\text{ for }t=0,\ldots,n-1\}.



For n=0, the list of restrictions is empty and C_{\varnothing}=\mathcal{U}.
If \nu_{U}(C_{h})>0, define




q_{h}(a):=\frac{\nu_{U}\{u\in C_{h}:d_{n}(h,u)=a\}}{\nu_{U}(C_{h})},\qquad a\in\mathcal{A}_{\mathcal{Z}}.

(29)


This is exactly the original policy’s next conditional decision law after
history h and continuation at all strict prefixes. Indeed,





\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(X_{1:n}=h,\ U\in C_{h},D_{n}=a)=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}E_{i}(x_{t})\right)\nu_{U}\{u\in C_{h}:d_{n}(h,u)=a\},




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(X_{1:n}=h,\ U\in C_{h})=\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}E_{i}(x_{t})\right)\nu_{U}(C_{h}).



Full support makes the signal-likelihood factor strictly positive, and it
cancels when the first line is divided by the second. The remaining ratio
is (29) and does not depend on the state.
If \nu_{U}(C_{h})=0, the policy never reaches h after continuing at all
strict prefixes in any state; in that case define q_{h} arbitrarily.




Now use fresh independent randomization to draw from q_{h} whenever h is
reached.
For n\geq 1, the behavioral policy assigns the stopped record
(T,Z,X_{1:n})=(n,z,h_{n}) probability




q_{h_{0}}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}E_{i}(x_{t})\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}q_{h_{t}}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\right)q_{h_{n}}\bigl((\mathsf{STOP},z)\bigr).



For n=0, the corresponding probability is
q_{\varnothing}\bigl((\mathsf{STOP},z)\bigr). Each signal factor in the
display is the correct state-i i.i.d. probability. Each decision factor
is the corresponding conditional probability in
(29). The chain rule therefore gives
exactly the same formula for the original policy. Likewise, the probability of the
surviving record \{T>n,X_{1:n}=h_{n}\} is




q_{h_{0}}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}E_{i}(x_{t})\right)\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}q_{h_{t}}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\right)



under both policies. These stopped and surviving cylinder probabilities
agree for every n and every finite history. Summing the survival
probabilities over histories gives the same value of \mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(T>n) for the
two policies. The original T is finite almost surely, so continuity from
above shows that the behavioral policy also has zero probability of
continuing forever. Finally, the displayed stopped-record probabilities,
summed over the countable collection of stopping times, histories, and
outputs, determine the joint law of (T,Z,X_{1},\ldots,X_{T}). This proves
the asserted outcome equivalence in every state.
∎





For any finite experiment




\bar{R}=(\bar{R}_{i})_{i\in\Theta}



with finite common support \mathcal{Z}_{\bar{R}}, define its
likelihood-ratio diameter by




\Delta(\bar{R}):=\max_{r,s\in\Theta}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{\bar{R}}}\left|\log\frac{\bar{R}_{r}(y)}{\bar{R}_{s}(y)}\right|.

(30)





Lemma 8 (Bounded-diameter reproduction).



Let \bar{R} be any finite experiment with finite common support.
There is a constant C_{E}<\infty, depending only on the source experiment
E, such that \bar{R} has an exact E-reproduction using a deterministic
number n(\bar{R}) of source observations satisfying




n(\bar{R})\leq C_{E}\bigl(1+\Delta(\bar{R})\bigr).

(31)






Proof of Lemma 8.



For n\geq 1, let \widehat{K}_{n} be the classifier in
(15), and let its confusion matrix be




A_{n}(i,k):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}_{n}=k).



Let I denote the identity matrix.
Use the row-sum matrix norm




\|M\|_{\infty}:=\max_{i}\sum_{k}|M(i,k)|.



For each row i, stochasticity of A_{n} gives




\displaystyle\sum_{k}|A_{n}(i,k)-I(i,k)|
\displaystyle=(1-A_{n}(i,i))+\sum_{k\neq i}A_{n}(i,k)




\displaystyle=2\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}_{n}\neq i).



It follows from Lemma 3 that there are
c_{0},C_{0}>0, depending only on E, such that




\delta_{n}:=\|A_{n}-I\|_{\infty}=2\max_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}_{n}\neq i)\leq C_{0}e^{-c_{0}n}.

(32)





Choose an integer n_{*}\geq 1 such that \delta_{n}\leq 1/2 whenever
n\geq n_{*}. For such n, submultiplicativity of the row-sum norm makes
the Neumann series converge:




A_{n}^{-1}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}[-(A_{n}-I)]^{m}.



Consequently,




\|A_{n}^{-1}-I\|_{\infty}\leq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\|A_{n}-I\|_{\infty}^{m}=\frac{\delta_{n}}{1-\delta_{n}}\leq 2C_{0}e^{-c_{0}n},

(33)


where the first step is the triangle inequality applied term by term and the
last uses \delta_{n}\leq 1/2 together with (32). Put
C_{1}:=2C_{0}.




View \bar{R} as a matrix with state rows and output columns, let
\bm{1} denote the all-ones column vector of the required dimension, and set




K_{n}:=A_{n}^{-1}\bar{R}.



Both A_{n} and \bar{R} have row sums one. Hence
A_{n}\bm{1}=\bm{1}, \bar{R}\bm{1}=\bm{1}, and invertibility gives
A_{n}^{-1}\bm{1}=\bm{1}. Therefore




K_{n}\bm{1}=A_{n}^{-1}\bar{R}\bm{1}=\bm{1}.

(34)


For every state i, output y, and state j, common support and
(30) imply
\bar{R}_{j}(y)\leq e^{\Delta(\bar{R})}\bar{R}_{i}(y). Using
K_{n}=[I+(A_{n}^{-1}-I)]\bar{R}, we obtain




\displaystyle K_{n}(i,y)
\displaystyle=\bar{R}_{i}(y)+\sum_{j}(A_{n}^{-1}-I)(i,j)\bar{R}_{j}(y)




\displaystyle\geq\bar{R}_{i}(y)-\sum_{j}|(A_{n}^{-1}-I)(i,j)|\bar{R}_{j}(y)




\displaystyle\geq\bar{R}_{i}(y)\left[1-e^{\Delta(\bar{R})}\|A_{n}^{-1}-I\|_{\infty}\right].

(35)


The first bound is the triangle inequality; the second replaces every
\bar{R}_{j}(y) using the likelihood-ratio inequality and then collects the row
sum.




Define the deterministic integer




n(\bar{R}):=\max\left\{n_{*},\left\lceil\frac{\Delta(\bar{R})+\log(C_{1}\vee 1)}{c_{0}}\right\rceil\right\}.

(36)


Then
e^{\Delta(\bar{R})}C_{1}e^{-c_{0}n(\bar{R})}\leq 1. Hence
(33) and
(35) show that every entry of
K_{n(\bar{R})} is nonnegative, and
(34) shows that it is a Markov kernel. The ceiling and
the maximum in (36) also imply




\displaystyle n(\bar{R})
\displaystyle\leq n_{*}+1+\frac{\Delta(\bar{R})+\log(C_{1}\vee 1)}{c_{0}}




\displaystyle\leq C_{E}\bigl(1+\Delta(\bar{R})\bigr)



after fixing a sufficiently large source-dependent C_{E}. This proves
(31).




The reproducing policy takes n(\bar{R}) observations, computes
\widehat{K}_{n(\bar{R})}, and, conditional on the classification k,
draws the output from K_{n(\bar{R})}(k,\cdot) using state-independent
randomization. Under state i, its output probability at y is




\sum_{k}A_{n(\bar{R})}(i,k)K_{n(\bar{R})}(k,y)=(A_{n(\bar{R})}K_{n(\bar{R})})(i,y)=\bar{R}_{i}(y),



where the last equality holds because K_{n}=A_{n}^{-1}\bar{R}. The reproduction is
therefore exact.
∎







Appendix C Proof of the uniform exact-conversion theorem




This appendix proves Theorem 2 in three stages: an exact reproduction for a finite experiment (Lemma 9), its application to
a target policy with a bounded stopping time (Proposition 1), and a
dyadic argument that extends the result to arbitrary integrable stopping policies.




C.1 Exact reproduction of a finite experiment




Fix a finite, full-support, pairwise identified source experiment E, and
let H=(H_{i})_{i\in\Theta} be a finite common-support experiment.
Denote the common support by \mathcal{Z}_{H}, and write




h_{i}(y):=H_{i}(y),\qquad\lambda_{ij}(y):=\log\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}.



Let c_{\rm cl},C_{\rm cl}>0 be fixed constants for which
(16) holds. Choose once and for all




0<\eta_{E}\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2d}\right\},

(37)


fix \eta\in(0,\eta_{E}], and put




g:=\log(1/\eta).



Then g\geq\log 2>0. This lower bound will let us absorb fixed additive
constants into constant multiples of g. Finally, define




B_{i}(H;E):=\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}\max_{j\neq i}\frac{[\lambda_{ij}(Y)]^{+}}{I_{ij}(E)}.

(38)





Lemma 9 (Exact multistate reproduction).



Fix a finite, full-support, pairwise identified source experiment E. Let
H=(H_{i})_{i\in\Theta} be a finite experiment, denote its common
support by \mathcal{Z}_{H}, and put




\Delta(H):=\max_{r,s\in\Theta}\max_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}}\left|\log\frac{H_{r}(y)}{H_{s}(y)}\right|.



There are constants C_{E}<\infty and \eta_{E}\in(0,1), depending only
on E and the state set, such that, for every such H and every
\eta\in(0,\eta_{E}], an exact E-reproduction (N,Y) of H exists
and satisfies, for every state i,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}N\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\eta\bigl(1+\Delta(H)\bigr)\right].

(39)


The constant is independent of H, its output space, and \eta.





Proof of Lemma 9.



We construct the reproduction and verify its law and cost in seven steps.





Step 1: Proposing an output




Define the deterministic classification length and the corresponding
classifier by




\ell_{\eta}:=\max\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{1}{c_{\rm cl}}\log\frac{C_{\rm cl}}{\eta}\right\rceil\right\},\qquad\widehat{K}:=\widehat{K}_{\ell_{\eta}}(X_{1:\ell_{\eta}}),

(40)


where \widehat{K}_{\ell}, including its tie-breaking rule, was formally
defined in (15). By construction,




\ell_{\eta}\geq\frac{1}{c_{\rm cl}}\log\frac{C_{\rm cl}}{\eta}.



Therefore Lemma 3 gives




\max_{i}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}\neq i)\leq\eta.

(41)


The ceiling in (40) also gives




\displaystyle\ell_{\eta}
\displaystyle\leq 1+\max\left\{1,\frac{\log C_{\rm cl}+g}{c_{\rm cl}}\right\}




\displaystyle\leq 2+\frac{|\log C_{\rm cl}|}{c_{\rm cl}}+\frac{g}{c_{\rm cl}}.



Since
c_{\rm cl} and C_{\rm cl} depend only on E, this proves




\ell_{\eta}\leq C_{E}(1+g)

(42)


for a constant depending only on E.




Conditional on \widehat{K}=k, use state-independent randomization to draw a
proposed output




Y^{\rm p}\sim H_{k}.



Thus, under state i,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}=k,Y^{\rm p}=y)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}=k)h_{k}(y).

(43)






Step 2: Constructing the verifier and proving termination




Fix for the moment a proposed pair (k,y). On a fresh source stream, define
the success-region passage time




\tau^{+}_{k,y}:=\inf\left\{n\geq 0:S_{kj}^{E}(n)\geq\lambda_{kj}(y)+g\text{ for every }j\neq k\right\}.



The success region requires enough evidence for the proposed state k against
every alternative, including the output-specific requirements
\lambda_{kj}(y).




For each \ell\neq k, define the passage time of the failure region indexed by
\ell as




\tau^{\rm fail}_{\ell}:=\inf\left\{n\geq 0:S_{\ell r}^{E}(n)\geq g\text{ for every }r\neq\ell\right\}.



The verifier rejects the proposal as soon as any alternative state reaches
its failure region, so put




\tau^{-}_{k}:=\min_{\ell\neq k}\tau^{\rm fail}_{\ell}.



Each displayed passage time is a stopping time: whether the corresponding
region has been entered by time n is determined by the relevant cumulative log-likelihood-ratio processes through time n. The minimum is over finitely many stopping times and is
therefore also a stopping time.




The verifier stops at




V_{k,y}:=\tau^{+}_{k,y}\wedge\tau^{-}_{k}.



It declares success on




\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y}:=\{\tau^{+}_{k,y}<\tau^{-}_{k}\};



thus failure wins a tie.




Under state k, \tau^{+}_{k,y} is the simultaneous passage time in
(17) with boundaries
(\lambda_{kj}(y)+g)_{j\neq k}, so it has finite expectation by
Lemma 4. Under a state i\neq k, the minimum defining
\tau^{-}_{k} includes the failure region indexed by i. Hence
\tau^{-}_{k} is no larger than the simultaneous passage time at which




S_{ir}^{E}(n)\geq g\qquad\text{for every }r\neq i.



That latter time also has finite expectation by Lemma 4. Since
V_{k,y}\leq\tau^{+}_{k,y} in state k, and
V_{k,y}\leq\tau^{-}_{k} in every state i\neq k, the verifier is integrable,
and therefore finite almost surely, under every state.




Return now to the random proposed pair. Run
(V_{\widehat{K},Y^{\rm p}},\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Succ}_{\widehat{K},Y^{\rm p}}}) on the unused source tail after
\ell_{\eta}, and denote its selected duration and success event by




V:=V_{\widehat{K},Y^{\rm p}}^{(\ell_{\eta})},\qquad\mathsf{Succ}:=\mathsf{Succ}_{\widehat{K},Y^{\rm p}}^{(\ell_{\eta})}.



The selector (\widehat{K},Y^{\rm p}) is known at the deterministic time
\ell_{\eta} and has finite range. Lemma 6 therefore shows that




\sigma^{\rm v}:=\ell_{\eta}+V



is a stopping time, that \mathsf{Succ} is known at \sigma^{\rm v}, and that
each selected verifier has the fresh-start law just analyzed. (That
\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y} belongs to \mathcal{F}_{V_{k,y}}, which the lemma requires, is
checked in Step 3.) It is also integrable in every state. Indeed, the proposal
set is finite and the conditional mean identity in Lemma 6 gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V=\sum_{k\in\Theta}\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}=k,Y^{\rm p}=y)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{k,y}\leq\max_{k,y}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{k,y}<\infty.



Adding the deterministic \ell_{\eta} shows that \sigma^{\rm v} is
integrable.
As permitted after Lemma 6, relabel the enlarged
filtration as the current source filtration.





Step 3: Bounding the verifier’s two kinds of error




Let




p_{i}(k,y):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y})



be its success probability in state i. We need one lower bound in the
proposed state and one upper bound in every other state.




Under state k, failure means that \tau^{-}_{k}\leq\tau^{+}_{k,y}. If the minimum in \tau^{-}_{k} is attained by the region
indexed by \ell\neq k, then S_{\ell k}^{E}\geq g when that region is
reached. Thus failure through that region implies




\sup_{n\geq 0}S_{\ell k}^{E}(n)\geq g.



The process \exp(S_{\ell k}^{E}(n)) is a nonnegative martingale under state
k, since each one-step multiplicative increment has state-k mean




\sum_{x}E_{k}(x)\frac{E_{\ell}(x)}{E_{k}(x)}=1.



Ville’s inequality gives




\mathbb{P}_{k}^{E}\!\left(\sup_{n\geq 0}e^{S_{\ell k}^{E}(n)}\geq e^{g}\right)\leq e^{-g}=\eta.



There are d alternative failure regions. A union
bound therefore yields




p_{k}(k,y)\geq 1-d\eta.

(44)





Now fix i\neq k. On \mathsf{Succ}_{k,y},




S_{ki}^{E}(V_{k,y})\geq\lambda_{ki}(y)+g.



The stopping time V_{k,y} is almost surely finite in states i and
k. Moreover, for every n\geq 0,




\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y}\cap\{V_{k,y}\leq n\}=\bigcup_{r=0}^{n}\{\tau^{+}_{k,y}=r\}\cap\{\tau^{-}_{k}>r\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}.



By the definition of a stopped sigma-field, this proves
\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y}\in\mathcal{F}_{V_{k,y}}. Thus
Lemma 5, applied in the direction from k to
i, gives




\displaystyle p_{i}(k,y)
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{k}^{E}\!\left[e^{-S_{ki}^{E}(V_{k,y})}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{Succ}_{k,y}}\right]




\displaystyle\leq e^{-\lambda_{ki}(y)-g}p_{k}(k,y)




\displaystyle=\eta\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{k}(y)}p_{k}(k,y).

(45)


The inequality uses the
success-region bound on S_{ki}^{E}(V_{k,y}).





Step 4: Bounding the verification cost




Fix the true state i, and let




A_{i}(k):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}=k).



If the classification is correct and the proposed output is y, then




V_{i,y}\leq\tau_{i}((\lambda_{ij}(y)+g)_{j\neq i}).



Write I_{*}:=\min_{r\neq s}I_{rs}(E)>0 for the slowest pairwise evidence rate of
the source.
Because g\geq 0 we have [\lambda+g]^{+}\leq[\lambda]^{+}+g, so the benchmark




\displaystyle t_{i}^{y}
\displaystyle:=\max_{j\neq i}\frac{[\lambda_{ij}(y)+g]^{+}}{I_{ij}(E)}




\displaystyle\leq\max_{j\neq i}\left\{\frac{[\lambda_{ij}(y)]^{+}}{I_{ij}(E)}+\frac{g}{I_{ij}(E)}\right\}




\displaystyle\leq\max_{j\neq i}\frac{[\lambda_{ij}(y)]^{+}}{I_{ij}(E)}+\frac{g}{I_{*}}

(46)


separates the output-specific requirement from the safety margin. Averaging
(46) under Y\sim H_{i} and using
(38) yields




\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}t_{i}^{Y}\leq B_{i}(H;E)+\frac{g}{I_{*}}.






Apply Lemma 4 for each y and then average. Jensen’s
inequality for the concave square root gives




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{i,Y}
\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}t_{i}^{Y}+C_{E}\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}\sqrt{1+t_{i}^{Y}}+C_{E}




\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}t_{i}^{Y}+C_{E}\sqrt{1+\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}t_{i}^{Y}}+C_{E}




\displaystyle\leq B_{i}(H;E)+\frac{g}{I_{*}}+C_{E}\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)+\frac{g}{I_{*}}}+C_{E}.



Finally,
\sqrt{u+v}\leq\sqrt{u}+\sqrt{v} for u,v\geq 0, and both
g/I_{*} and \sqrt{g/I_{*}} are bounded by an E-dependent constant
times 1+g. Enlarging C_{E} therefore gives




\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim H_{i}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{i,Y}\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+1+g\right].

(47)





If the classification is k\neq i, then the verifier stops no later
than the time at which state i reaches the failure region indexed by i.
That is the simultaneous state-i passage time with every boundary equal to
g. Its benchmark is




\max_{r\neq i}\frac{g}{I_{ir}(E)}\leq\frac{g}{I_{*}}.



Substitution into (18), followed by
\sqrt{1+g/I_{*}}\leq C_{E}(1+g), gives, uniformly in k\neq i and y,




\sup_{k\neq i}\sup_{y}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{k,y}\leq C_{E}(1+g).

(48)





The selected verifier has the fresh-start conditional laws established in
Step 2, so conditioning first on \widehat{K} and then on the proposal,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V=A_{i}(i)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}}h_{i}(y)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{i,y}+\sum_{k\neq i}A_{i}(k)\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}}h_{k}(y)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}V_{k,y}.



The correct branch is bounded by (47) together
with A_{i}(i)\leq 1. For the remaining branches, (41)
gives \sum_{k\neq i}A_{i}(k)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(\widehat{K}\neq i)\leq\eta, so
(48) and \sum_{y}h_{k}(y)=1 show that together they
contribute at most C_{E}\eta(1+g). This is uniformly bounded because
\eta g=\eta\log(1/\eta)\leq e^{-1} for 0<\eta\leq 1.




Classification contributes \ell_{\eta}\leq C_{E}(1+g) observations by
(42). Since \sigma^{\rm v}=\ell_{\eta}+V, the preceding estimates
first give




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\sigma^{\rm v}\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+1+g+\eta(1+g)\right].



Here the last term is the total contribution of the wrong-classification
branches. Because 0<\eta\leq 1, it is at most 1+g. Moreover,
g\geq\log 2, so 1+g\leq(1+1/\log 2)g. Enlarging C_{E} therefore
gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\sigma^{\rm v}\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+\log\frac{1}{\eta}\right].

(49)






Step 5: Bounding the accepted mass




The state-i probability of proposing y through
candidate k and passing verification is




r_{i}(k,y):=A_{i}(k)h_{k}(y)p_{i}(k,y).



This product follows from (43) and the fresh-start
conditional success probability of the selected verifier. The own-state
term satisfies




r_{i}(i,y)=A_{i}(i)h_{i}(y)p_{i}(i,y)\leq h_{i}(y).



For k\neq i, (45) gives




r_{i}(k,y)\leq A_{i}(k)h_{k}(y)\left[\eta\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{k}(y)}p_{k}(k,y)\right]\leq\eta h_{i}(y).






Set




a_{\eta}:=\frac{1-\eta}{1+d\eta}.



By (37), a_{\eta}\in(0,1). At time
\sigma^{\rm v}, draw a Bernoulli random variable B^{\eta} with success
probability a_{\eta}, independently of all signals and earlier
randomization. Enlarge the filtration at \sigma^{\rm v} by revealing
this draw there, exactly as in (24). The time
\sigma^{\rm v} remains a stopping time, and B^{\eta} is measurable in
its stopped sigma-field. Define




\mathsf{Acc}:=\mathsf{Succ}\cap\{B^{\eta}=1\},\qquad Y^{(0)}:=\begin{cases}Y^{\rm p},&\text{on }\mathsf{Acc},\\
\bot,&\text{on }\mathsf{Acc}^{\mathsf{c}}.\end{cases}



Thus both a verification failure and a rejected thinning draw produce the
temporary flag \bot, and Y^{(0)} is
\mathcal{F}_{\sigma^{\rm v}}-measurable. Independence of the thinning draw shows that the
state-i accepted mass at output y is




Q_{i}(y):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(Y^{(0)}=y)=a_{\eta}\sum_{k\in\Theta}r_{i}(k,y).



There is one own-state term and d cross-state terms. The preceding bounds
therefore imply, pointwise in i and y,




Q_{i}(y)\leq a_{\eta}(1+d\eta)h_{i}(y)=(1-\eta)h_{i}(y).

(50)





Let




\delta_{i}:=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(Y^{(0)}=\bot)=1-\sum_{y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}}Q_{i}(y)



be the state-i probability of \bot. Summing
(50) over y gives \delta_{i}\geq\eta.




For the reverse bound, keep only the nonnegative term k=i in the sum
defining Q_{i}. Then




\displaystyle\sum_{y}Q_{i}(y)
\displaystyle\geq a_{\eta}A_{i}(i)\sum_{y}h_{i}(y)p_{i}(i,y)




\displaystyle\geq a_{\eta}(1-\eta)(1-d\eta).

(51)


Here the second step uses A_{i}(i)\geq 1-\eta from (41),
p_{i}(i,y)\geq 1-d\eta from (44), and \sum_{y}h_{i}(y)=1.
Substituting the definition of a_{\eta} into
(51) and taking complements gives




\displaystyle\delta_{i}
\displaystyle\leq 1-\frac{(1-\eta)^{2}(1-d\eta)}{1+d\eta}




\displaystyle=\frac{(2d+2)\eta-(2d+1)\eta^{2}+d\eta^{3}}{1+d\eta}




\displaystyle\leq 2(d+1)\eta.



For the final inequality, use
-(2d+1)\eta^{2}+d\eta^{3}\leq 0, which holds because
d\eta\leq 1/2<2d+1, and then use 1+d\eta\geq 1.
Thus, with C_{d}:=2(d+1),




\eta\leq\delta_{i}\leq C_{d}\eta.

(52)






Step 6: Defining and bounding the residual




Define the residual experiment




R_{i}(y):=\frac{h_{i}(y)-Q_{i}(y)}{\delta_{i}}.

(53)


The numerator is nonnegative by
(50), and




\sum_{y}\bigl(h_{i}(y)-Q_{i}(y)\bigr)=\delta_{i}.



Thus R_{i} is a probability distribution. Moreover,
(50) implies




\eta h_{i}(y)\leq h_{i}(y)-Q_{i}(y)\leq h_{i}(y),\qquad y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}.



The lower bound is strictly positive because \eta>0 and H has common
support. Hence every R_{i} has the same support \mathcal{Z}_{H}.




For any i,j and y\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}, the two residual numerators satisfy




\eta\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}\leq\frac{h_{i}(y)-Q_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)-Q_{j}(y)}\leq\frac{1}{\eta}\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}.



Separately, (52) gives




\frac{1}{C_{d}}\leq\frac{\delta_{j}}{\delta_{i}}\leq C_{d}.



Multiplying these two displays and using




\frac{R_{i}(y)}{R_{j}(y)}=\frac{h_{i}(y)-Q_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)-Q_{j}(y)}\frac{\delta_{j}}{\delta_{i}}



gives




\frac{\eta}{C_{d}}\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}\leq\frac{R_{i}(y)}{R_{j}(y)}\leq\frac{C_{d}}{\eta}\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}.

(54)


Taking logarithms in the lower and upper bounds of
(54) yields, respectively,




\log\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}-\log\frac{C_{d}}{\eta}\leq\log\frac{R_{i}(y)}{R_{j}(y)}\leq\log\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}+\log\frac{C_{d}}{\eta}.



As a result,




\left|\log\frac{R_{i}(y)}{R_{j}(y)}\right|\leq\left|\log\frac{h_{i}(y)}{h_{j}(y)}\right|+\log\frac{C_{d}}{\eta}.



Maximizing over states and outputs gives




\Delta(R)\leq\Delta(H)+\log\frac{C_{d}}{\eta}.

(55)






Step 7: Repairing the residual and defining the reproduction




Apply Lemma 8 to the residual experiment in
(53); we refer to this step as repairing the residual.
Its deterministic repair length satisfies




n(R)\leq C_{E}\bigl(1+\Delta(R)\bigr)\leq C_{E}\left[1+\Delta(H)+\log\frac{1}{\eta}\right],

(56)


by (55), with the fixed term \log C_{d} absorbed into
C_{E}.




Run this residual reproduction on the unused source tail after
\sigma^{\rm v} only on the event \{Y^{(0)}=\bot\}. On the acceptance
event, select instead the zero-duration policy with a fixed output
y_{\star}\in\mathcal{Z}_{H}. This selector is known at
\sigma^{\rm v}, so Lemma 6 supplies globally
defined selected variables (\bar{N}^{\rm r},\bar{Y}^{\rm r}) and a valid
pasted stopping time. On failure, \bar{Y}^{\rm r} has the residual law;
on acceptance, (\bar{N}^{\rm r},\bar{Y}^{\rm r})=(0,y_{\star}). Define the
final reproduction explicitly by




N:=\sigma^{\rm v}+\bar{N}^{\rm r},\qquad Y:=\begin{cases}Y^{\rm p},&\text{if }Y^{(0)}\neq\bot,\\
\bar{Y}^{\rm r},&\text{if }Y^{(0)}=\bot.\end{cases}



Lemma 6 shows that N is a stopping time. To verify the
output claim explicitly, let B\subseteq\mathcal{Z}_{H}. For every
deterministic n,




\displaystyle\{Y\in B,N\leq n\}={}
\displaystyle\{Y^{(0)}\neq\bot,\ Y^{\rm p}\in B,\ \sigma^{\rm v}\leq n\}




\displaystyle\cup\{Y^{(0)}=\bot,\ \bar{Y}^{\rm r}\in B,\ N\leq n\}.



On acceptance, \bar{N}^{\rm r}=0, so N=\sigma^{\rm v}; all events
in the first line are therefore in \mathcal{F}_{n}, because Y^{\rm p} was
known at time \ell_{\eta}\leq\sigma^{\rm v} and Y^{(0)} is measurable
at \sigma^{\rm v}. The second line is the
stopped-output event supplied by Lemma 6 for the repair branch,
intersected with its selector event. It too belongs to \mathcal{F}_{n}. Thus
\{Y\in B,N\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n} for every B,n, proving
Y\in\mathcal{F}_{N}.




Under state i, the accepted first stage contributes mass Q_{i}(y) to output
y, while the failure event has probability \delta_{i} and carries the fresh
repair output, whose law is R_{i}. Partitioning on acceptance and then using
the definition (53) of the residual,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(Y=y)=Q_{i}(y)+\delta_{i}R_{i}(y)=h_{i}(y).



The reproduction is thus exact output by output.




Repair is run only on failure, and there the conditional mean duration is the
deterministic length n(R). Since \mathbb{P}_{i}^{E}(Y^{(0)}=\bot)=\delta_{i}, the
conditional mean assertion in Lemma 6 gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\bar{N}^{\rm r}=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\{Y^{(0)}=\bot\}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}(\bar{N}^{\rm r}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma^{\rm v}})\right]=\delta_{i}n(R)\leq C_{E}\eta\left[1+\Delta(H)+\log\frac{1}{\eta}\right],

(57)


the last step combining \delta_{i}\leq C_{d}\eta from (52)
with (56) and absorbing C_{d} into C_{E}.




Because N=\sigma^{\rm v}+\bar{N}^{\rm r}, adding
(49) and (57) first gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}N\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\eta\left(1+\Delta(H)+\log\frac{1}{\eta}\right)\right].



The repair contribution
C_{E}\eta\log(1/\eta) is uniformly bounded by C_{E}/e; this fixed quantity
is absorbed by C_{E}\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}, whose square-root factor is at least
one. The other repair contributions are bounded by
C_{E}\eta(1+\Delta(H)). Hence, simultaneously for every state i,




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{E}N\leq B_{i}(H;E)+C_{E}\left[\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;E)}+\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\eta\bigl(1+\Delta(H)\bigr)\right].



Every quantity on the right is finite. Thus N is integrable and in
particular finite almost surely in every state. This proves
(39) and completes the proof of
Lemma 9.
∎






C.2 Policies with bounded stopping times




Fix the source F and target G from Theorem 2, and write
\mathcal{X}_{G} for the finite signal space of G.




We first treat target policies with bounded stopping times.
Consider a finite-output stopping G-policy (T,Z) with
T\leq m pathwise, where m\geq 1 is an integer.




Proposition 1 (Conversion of policies with bounded stopping times).



There is C_{F,G}<\infty, independent of m and the policy, for which an
exact F-reproduction (N,Y) exists and satisfies




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}\sqrt{m}\qquad(i\in\Theta).

(58)


The reproduction may be chosen with N\geq 1 pathwise.





Proof of Proposition 1.



By Lemma 7 we may represent the randomization of (T,Z) by
state-independent behavioral kernels. Let




H_{i}:=\mathcal{L}_{i}^{G}(Z).



These laws have common support. Indeed, a terminal output with positive
probability in state i has the explicit decomposition




\displaystyle H_{i}(z)={}
\displaystyle q_{\varnothing}\bigl((\mathsf{STOP},z)\bigr)




\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{m}\ \sum_{x_{1:n}\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{n}}q_{\varnothing}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n}G_{i}(x_{t})\right)




\displaystyle\qquad{}\times\left(\prod_{t=1}^{n-1}q_{x_{1:t}}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\right)q_{x_{1:n}}\bigl((\mathsf{STOP},z)\bigr).



The first term is stopping at time zero. In a summand indexed by n\geq 1,
the first behavioral factor records continuation at time zero, the middle
behavioral product records continuation after the first n-1 signals, and
the final factor records stopping with output z after signal n. Empty
products are understood to equal one. All summands are nonnegative and there
are finitely many. If H_{i}(z)>0, at least one summand is positive. Its
behavioral factors are state-independent, while its signal factors are
strictly positive in every state by full support. The same summand is
therefore positive in every other state. This proves common support.
For z in this common support, write




\lambda_{ij}(z):=\log\frac{H_{i}(z)}{H_{j}(z)}.






Let X_{1}^{G},X_{2}^{G},\ldots denote the target signals and let
(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{G})_{n\geq 0} be their filtration augmented by the target policy’s
state-independent randomization. Fix i\neq j, and define




S_{ij}^{G}(n):=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\log\frac{G_{i}(X_{t}^{G})}{G_{j}(X_{t}^{G})},\qquad M_{ij}(n):=S_{ij}^{G}(n)-I_{ij}(G)n.



Then M_{ij} is a martingale with bounded centered increments, and at the
target stopping time,




S_{ij}^{G}(T)=I_{ij}(G)T+M_{ij}(T).

(59)





By Lemma 5,




\frac{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{j}^{G}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}^{G}}}{\,\mathrm{d}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}|_{\mathcal{F}_{T}^{G}}}=e^{-S_{ij}^{G}(T)}.



Because Z is \mathcal{F}_{T}^{G}-measurable, for every output z in the common
support,




H_{j}(z)=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[e^{-S_{ij}^{G}(T)}\mathbf{1}_{\{Z=z\}}\right],



and division by H_{i}(z)>0 gives




\frac{H_{j}(z)}{H_{i}(z)}=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[e^{-S_{ij}^{G}(T)}\mid Z=z\right].



Hence \lambda_{ij}(z)=-\log\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[e^{-S_{ij}^{G}(T)}\mid Z=z], and since
x\mapsto-\log x is convex, conditional Jensen bounds the likelihood
requirement of an output by the evidence the target policy actually
accumulated before reporting it:




\lambda_{ij}(z)\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\bigl[S_{ij}^{G}(T)\mid Z=z\bigr].






Fix j\neq i. Dividing by I_{ij}(F)>0 preserves this inequality, and
monotonicity and convexity of x\mapsto x^{+} then give




\displaystyle\left[\frac{\lambda_{ij}(Z)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}
\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left(\left.\frac{S_{ij}^{G}(T)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right|Z\right)\right]^{+}




\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[\left.\left[\frac{S_{ij}^{G}(T)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}\right|Z\right].



Taking the maximum over the
finitely many j’s yields




\max_{j\neq i}\left[\frac{\lambda_{ij}(Z)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[\left.\max_{j\neq i}\left[\frac{S_{ij}^{G}(T)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}\right|Z\right].

(60)





For every j\neq i,




\left[\frac{I_{ij}(G)}{I_{ij}(F)}T+\frac{M_{ij}(T)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)T+\frac{|M_{ij}(T)|}{I_{ij}(F)}.



To verify this inequality, put
a_{j}:=I_{ij}(G)/I_{ij}(F). Both a_{j} and T are nonnegative, so
[a_{j}T+b]^{+}\leq a_{j}T+|b|. The definition of
\rho_{i}(F\to G) gives a_{j}\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G).




Average (60) over Z. The tower property removes
the conditioning. Apply (59) and the last pathwise inequality, and then use
\max_{j}u_{j}\leq\sum_{j}u_{j} for nonnegative u_{j}. This gives




\displaystyle B_{i}(H;F)
\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\max_{j\neq i}\left[\frac{S_{ij}^{G}(T)}{I_{ij}(F)}\right]^{+}




\displaystyle\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}|M_{ij}(T)|}{I_{ij}(F)}.

(61)





Define




\xi_{t}^{ij}:=\log\frac{G_{i}(X_{t}^{G})}{G_{j}(X_{t}^{G})}-I_{ij}(G),\qquad v_{ij}:=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[(\xi_{t}^{ij})^{2}].



Full support and finiteness of the signal space make the increments bounded.
They are independent of \mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{G}, have conditional mean zero, and have
constant conditional second moment v_{ij}. Since T\leq m,




M_{ij}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{m}\xi_{t}^{ij}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}.



If s<t, then
\xi_{s}^{ij}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq s\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}} is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{G}-measurable. Therefore the corresponding cross term vanishes:





\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[\xi_{s}^{ij}\xi_{t}^{ij}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq s\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}\right]




\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\!\left[\xi_{s}^{ij}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq s\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}(\xi_{t}^{ij}\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}^{G})\right]=0.



For a diagonal term, predictability of \mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}} gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[(\xi_{t}^{ij})^{2}\mathbf{1}_{\{T\geq t\}}]=v_{ij}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T\geq t).



Expanding the square, using the vanished cross terms, and then applying the
tail-sum formula yields




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[M_{ij}(T)^{2}]=v_{ij}\sum_{t=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T\geq t)=v_{ij}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T.



Thus Cauchy–Schwarz gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}|M_{ij}(T)|\leq\sqrt{v_{ij}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T}.



The constant




C_{F,G}^{(1)}:=\max_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\sqrt{v_{ij}}}{I_{ij}(F)}



is finite because there are finitely many ordered pairs and every
I_{ij}(F)>0. Substitution into (61) proves




B_{i}(H;F)\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}^{(1)}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T}.

(62)





Let




L_{G}:=\max_{r,s\in\Theta}\ \max_{x\in\mathcal{X}_{G}}\left|\log\frac{G_{r}(x)}{G_{s}(x)}\right|.



This constant is finite by full support and finiteness of the signal space.
Since T\leq m,




|S_{ij}^{G}(T)|\leq mL_{G}\qquad\text{pathwise}.



Therefore
e^{-S_{ij}^{G}(T)}\in[e^{-mL_{G}},e^{mL_{G}}] pathwise. A conditional
expectation of a random variable in this interval remains in the same
interval. The conditional likelihood-ratio formula thus gives




e^{-mL_{G}}\leq\frac{H_{j}(z)}{H_{i}(z)}\leq e^{mL_{G}}.



Taking logarithms yields
|\log(H_{i}(z)/H_{j}(z))|\leq mL_{G}. Maximizing over states and outputs gives




\Delta(H)\leq mL_{G}.

(63)





Let \eta_{F} denote the constant \eta_{E} supplied by
Lemma 9 when its source experiment is E=F. Apply
that lemma with source F and




\eta=\frac{\eta_{F}}{m}.



Because m\geq 1, this choice belongs to (0,\eta_{F}]. Put




t_{i}:=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T\leq m.



Equation
(62) gives




B_{i}(H;F)\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)t_{i}+C_{F,G}^{(1)}\sqrt{t_{i}}.



Since t_{i}\leq m, \sqrt{m}\leq m, and m\geq 1,




\displaystyle 1+B_{i}(H;F)
\displaystyle\leq 1+\rho_{i}(F\to G)m+C_{F,G}^{(1)}\sqrt{m}




\displaystyle\leq\bigl(1+\rho_{i}(F\to G)+C_{F,G}^{(1)}\bigr)m.



There are finitely many states. Taking their maximum and then taking square
roots gives a policy-independent constant such that




\sqrt{1+B_{i}(H;F)}\leq C_{F,G}\sqrt{m}.

(64)


Moreover,




\log\frac{1}{\eta}=\log\frac{1}{\eta_{F}}+\log m\leq C_{F,G}\sqrt{m}.

(65)


Here we used \log m\leq\sqrt{m} and 1\leq\sqrt{m} for m\geq 1.
Finally, (63) gives




\displaystyle\eta(1+\Delta(H))
\displaystyle\leq\frac{\eta_{F}}{m}(1+mL_{G})




\displaystyle\leq\eta_{F}(1+L_{G}).

(66)





Apply Lemma 9 with this \eta. Its leading boundary
term is at most
\rho_{i}(F\to G)t_{i}+C_{F,G}^{(1)}\sqrt{t_{i}}. Its three remainder terms
are bounded by constant multiples of \sqrt{m} by
(64), (65), and
(66). Since \sqrt{t_{i}}\leq\sqrt{m}, adding
these bounds proves (58) with a constant depending
only on F and G.




It remains to enforce the positive-duration clause. On any path on which the
reproduction just constructed stops at source time zero, wait for one unused
source observation and then report the output already determined. Formally,
from the reproduction (N,Y) just obtained, define




N^{+}:=N+\mathbf{1}_{\{N=0\}},\qquad Y^{+}:=Y.



Then N^{+}\geq 1 pathwise. For n=0, the event \{N^{+}\leq n\} is empty;
for every n\geq 1, it equals \{N\leq n\}. Thus N^{+} is a stopping
time. The same identities with an additional intersection by
\{Y\in B\} show that Y^{+} is measurable at N^{+}. Its output law
is unchanged, and




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N^{+}=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N+\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(N=0)\leq\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N+1.



Since 1\leq\sqrt{m}, enlarging C_{F,G} absorbs the additional cost.
Relabel (N^{+},Y^{+}) as (N,Y); this proves the positive-duration clause.
∎






C.3 Extending to an arbitrary integrable target policy




Let (T,Z) now be an arbitrary finite-output stopping G-policy that is
integrable in every state, and write \mathcal{Z} for the finite range of
Z and \mathcal{X}_{G} for the finite signal space of G. We construct
its exact F-reproduction. All auxiliary randomizations used in different
source stages are mutually independent, independent of both signal streams,
and revealed only when used.




Proof of Theorem 2.



We assemble the reproductions of Proposition 1 into a
single infinite-horizon policy, then verify the recursion, its exactness, and
its expected cost.





Step 1: Specifying every bounded target continuation




Use Lemma 7 to represent the target policy by
state-independent kernels q_{h} at finite G-signal histories. A draw
from q_{h} is either \mathsf{CONTINUE} or
(\mathsf{STOP},z). We use independent behavioral draws at the different
histories. This representation has the same joint law of the target stopping
time, terminal output, and observed signals under every state.
Let (\mathcal{F}_{n}^{G})_{n\geq 0} be the filtration generated by the first n
target signals and all behavioral draws revealed through the decision after
signal n, including the time-zero draw when n=0.




Set




L_{k}:=2^{k},\qquad s_{-1}:=0,\qquad s_{k}:=\sum_{a=0}^{k}L_{a}=2^{k+1}-1\quad(k\geq 0).



We call the target signals X^{G}_{s_{k-1}+1},\ldots,X^{G}_{s_{k}} the kth
block; the source observations used to reproduce block k
constitute source stage k. The target enters block k on the target-space event




A_{k}^{G}:=\{T>s_{k-1}\}.



Because T is a stopping time,
A_{k}^{G}=\{T\leq s_{k-1}\}^{\mathsf{c}}\in\mathcal{F}_{s_{k-1}}^{G}. Thus entry into the block is known at its checkpoint, before any signal from that block is observed.
For k=0, this means that the behavioral draw from q_{\varnothing} at time
zero was \mathsf{CONTINUE}. For k\geq 1, it means that the target policy
continued after seeing its first s_{k-1} signals.




Fix k\geq 0 and a history




h\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}},



where \mathcal{X}_{G}^{0}=\{\varnothing\}. Starting after the decision to
continue at h, define a bounded target continuation




(\tau_{k,h},W_{k,h})



as follows. Draw fresh G-signals one at a time and apply the behavioral
kernel after each enlarged history. If the policy stops after
r\in\{1,\ldots,L_{k}\} new signals
x_{1:r}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}) and reports z, set




\tau_{k,h}=r,\qquad W_{k,h}=(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z).



If it chooses \mathsf{CONTINUE} after all L_{k} new signals, set




\tau_{k,h}=L_{k},\qquad W_{k,h}=(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}}).



In particular, stopping after the last signal of the block produces a stop
report, while a continuation report records that the behavioral decision at
the block endpoint was \mathsf{CONTINUE}. Thus there is no ambiguity at a
checkpoint.




This is an ordinary finite-output stopping G-policy with
\tau_{k,h}\leq L_{k}. Its report includes all the target-side information
needed either to identify the stopped record or to resume at the next
checkpoint. Its report laws have common support, as we now verify locally.
For a fixed stop report
(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z), its state-i probability is




\left(\prod_{u=1}^{r}G_{i}(x_{u})\right)\left(\prod_{u=1}^{r-1}q_{(h,x_{1:u})}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\right)q_{(h,x_{1:r})}\bigl((\mathsf{STOP},z)\bigr),



where the second product is one when r=1, and
(h,x_{1:u}) denotes concatenation. For a continuation report
(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}}), the probability is




\left(\prod_{u=1}^{L_{k}}G_{i}(x_{u})\right)\left(\prod_{u=1}^{L_{k}}q_{(h,x_{1:u})}(\mathsf{CONTINUE})\right).



The first display records continuation after the first r-1 new signals
and a stop after the rth; the second records continuation after every new
signal, including the block endpoint. Every behavioral factor in these
displays is state-independent, and every signal factor is strictly positive
in every state by full support. Therefore a report has positive probability
in one state if and only if its behavioral product is positive, in which case
the same report has positive probability in every state. Histories that are
never reached by the original target policy cause no problem: the kernels
q_{h} were defined arbitrarily there, and the same formulas still determine
a valid bounded continuation with common-support report laws.




Let




\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}:=\mathcal{L}_{i}^{G}(W_{k,h}),\qquad e_{i,k}(h):=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\tau_{k,h}.



Apply Proposition 1 to every pair (k,h). It supplies an exact
source policy




(N_{k,h}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k,h})



such that, simultaneously for all states i,




\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{i}^{F}(\widehat{W}_{k,h})
\displaystyle=\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h},

(67)


\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k,h}^{F}
\displaystyle\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)e_{i,k}(h)+C_{F,G}\sqrt{L_{k}}.

(68)


For each fixed k, there are finitely many histories h, and their union
over k\geq 0 is countable. We therefore fix one reproduction for every pair
(k,h) once and for all. The final clause of Proposition 1 lets us
choose each of them with N_{k,h}^{F}\geq 1 pathwise.




For later pasting, place every report in one common countable space. Define




\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{k}:={}
\displaystyle\left\{(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z):1\leq r\leq L_{k},\ x_{1:r}\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{r},\ z\in\mathcal{Z}\right\}




\displaystyle\cup\left\{(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}}):x_{1:L_{k}}\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{L_{k}}\right\},



\displaystyle\mathcal{W}:={}
\displaystyle\{\dagger\}\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\mathcal{W}_{k}.



Thus every \widehat{W}_{k,h} is regarded as \mathcal{W}-valued, and
the idle branch has the fixed output y_{\dagger}:=\dagger.





Step 2: Defining the recursive source construction




We now work on the source F-signal space, with augmented filtration
(\mathcal{F}_{n}^{F})_{n\geq 0}, and set the initial source completion time to




\sigma_{-1}:=0.



Draw from the state-independent kernel
q_{\varnothing}, using auxiliary randomization. If its outcome is
(\mathsf{STOP},z), set Y=z; the construction stops at source time zero
and enters no source stage. If its outcome is \mathsf{CONTINUE}, enter
source stage zero with this stored history.
This handles a policy that mixes stopping and continuation at time zero.




Let \widetilde{A}_{k} denote the source-space event that source stage k
is entered. In particular, \widetilde{A}_{0} is the event that the initial
draw says \mathsf{CONTINUE}. Define a global checkpoint record
\widetilde{H}_{0} by setting it equal to \varnothing on
\widetilde{A}_{0} and equal to the idle symbol \dagger on
\widetilde{A}_{0}^{\mathsf{c}}. Recursively, \widetilde{H}_{k} will belong
to \mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}} on \widetilde{A}_{k} and will equal
\dagger off \widetilde{A}_{k}. This convention makes every checkpoint
record a formally defined random variable on the whole source space.




Recursively, suppose source stage k is entered and the stored simulated
target history is




\widetilde{H}_{k}=h\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}}.



Run the fixed reproduction
(N_{k,h}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k,h}) on the unused F-signal tail after
\sigma_{k-1}, with fresh state-independent randomization. Denote the
selected duration and report on the source probability space by
(N_{k}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k}). Thus this pair is, by definition, the shifted
application of (N_{k,h}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k,h}) on the event that stage k
is entered with stored history h.
If the source stage is not entered because the construction has already
terminated, set N_{k}^{F}:=0 and \widehat{W}_{k}:=\dagger, where
\dagger is the idle report. In both cases put




\sigma_{k}:=\sigma_{k-1}+N_{k}^{F}.






When the stage report is




\widehat{W}_{k}=(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z),



set Y=z and terminate at source time \sigma_{k}. When it is




\widehat{W}_{k}=(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}}),



store the enlarged simulated target history




\widetilde{H}_{k+1}:=(h,x_{1:L_{k}})



and enter stage k+1. If stage k is not entered or its report is a
stop report, set \widetilde{H}_{k+1}:=\dagger. Hence, on every path,
\widetilde{A}_{k+1}=\{\widetilde{H}_{k+1}\neq\dagger\}.




To define the terminal variables on every source path, fix an arbitrary
z_{0}\in\mathcal{Z}. On a path on which the recursive construction never
terminates, set Y:=z_{0}; in all cases define




N:=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sigma_{k}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{N}_{0}\cup\{\infty\}.

(69)


Because every entered stage consumes at least one source observation, N is
infinite on a path with infinitely many entered stages. Step 4 will show that
such paths have probability zero in every state, and Step 6 will show that
N is integrable.





Step 3: Verifying every restart and filtration claim




We prove by induction on k that \sigma_{k-1} is an almost surely
finite F-stopping time in every state and that the event
\widetilde{A}_{k}, together with the stored history on that event, is
measurable at \sigma_{k-1}. These are exactly the hypotheses needed to
restart stage k.




For k=0, \sigma_{-1}=0. The event \widetilde{A}_{0} is determined
by the time-zero draw from q_{\varnothing}, and the stored history is the
deterministic empty history. Thus the induction assertions hold at the base
stage.




Suppose they hold at stage k. The event \widetilde{A}_{k} and, on that
event, the value of \widetilde{H}_{k}, are known at source time
\sigma_{k-1} by the induction hypothesis. Hence the selector




J_{k}:=\begin{cases}(k,h),&\text{on }\{\widetilde{A}_{k},\widetilde{H}_{k}=h\},\\
\dagger,&\text{on }\widetilde{A}_{k}^{\mathsf{c}}\end{cases}



is \mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F}-measurable and countable-valued. The value \dagger
selects the idle policy; every other value selects the fixed integrable reproduction for (k,h).




Apply Lemma 6 at \sigma_{k-1}. It proves that
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k-1}+N_{k}^{F} is a stopping time and that
\widehat{W}_{k} is measurable at \sigma_{k}. It also gives, under state
i, for every bounded measurable test function \varphi, and on the
reach event
\{\widetilde{A}_{k},\widetilde{H}_{k}=h\}, the identity




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}\!\left[\varphi(N_{k}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k})\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}\varphi(N_{k,h}^{F},\widehat{W}_{k,h}).

(70)


For the unbounded duration coordinate, the conditional-mean clause of that
lemma separately gives




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}[N_{k}^{F}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F}]=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k,h}^{F}\quad\text{on the same reach event}.

(71)





We must also check finiteness before invoking the lemma at the next stage. For
fixed k, the set \mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}} is finite. Each
N_{k,h}^{F} is integrable in every state by
(68). Because N_{k}^{F}=0 off \widetilde{A}_{k}, the tower property and
(71) give




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k}^{F}=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{A}_{k},\widetilde{H}_{k}=h)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k,h}^{F}.



This is a finite sum of finite terms, so N_{k}^{F} is integrable and hence
finite almost surely. Since \sigma_{k-1} is finite almost surely, so is
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k-1}+N_{k}^{F}.




Finally, \widehat{W}_{k}, together with the old record \widetilde{H}_{k},
determines whether the construction terminates or enters stage k+1; in
the latter case it determines the concatenated record \widetilde{H}_{k+1}.
The old record is measurable at \sigma_{k-1}, hence also at
\sigma_{k}, and \widehat{W}_{k} is measurable at \sigma_{k}. Therefore
both \widetilde{A}_{k+1} and the globally defined
\widetilde{H}_{k+1} are \mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k}}^{F}-measurable. This closes the induction and proves every
restart, stopping-time, finiteness, and conditional-law claim for finite
stages.





Step 4: Verifying exactness by induction




The target event A_{k}^{G} and the source event \widetilde{A}_{k} live on
different probability spaces. We compare their probabilities and records;
we never identify the events themselves. For
h\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}}, define the target and source reach events




\displaystyle B_{k,h}^{G}
\displaystyle:=\{A_{k}^{G},X_{1:s_{k-1}}^{G}=h\},



\displaystyle\widetilde{B}_{k,h}
\displaystyle:=\{\widetilde{A}_{k},\widetilde{H}_{k}=h\}.



The checkpoint measurability proved in Step 3 gives
\widetilde{B}_{k,h}\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F}. On the target side,
A_{k}^{G}\in\mathcal{F}_{s_{k-1}}^{G} and the signal record through that checkpoint is
also measurable there, so B_{k,h}^{G}\in\mathcal{F}_{s_{k-1}}^{G}. Define the
target reach mass by




\pi_{i,k}(h):=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(B_{k,h}^{G}).



We claim that, for every state i, every stage k, and every such history
h,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k,h})=\pi_{i,k}(h).

(72)





For k=0, the only history is \varnothing. Both sides of
(72) are the probability that a draw from the same
state-independent kernel q_{\varnothing} says
\mathsf{CONTINUE}. This proves the base case.




Suppose (72) holds at stage k, and fix a reached
history h. The reach event is measurable at \sigma_{k-1} by Step 3, so
the tower property applies; feeding the bounded test function
\varphi(n,u)=\mathbf{1}_{\{u=w\}} into (70) and then
invoking (67) and the induction hypothesis, we get, for
every possible report w,




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k,h},\widehat{W}_{k}=w)
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{B}_{k,h}}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widehat{W}_{k}=w\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F})\right]




\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k,h})\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}(w)




\displaystyle=\pi_{i,k}(h)\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}(w).

(73)





On the target probability space, define the block report on every path by




W_{k}^{G}:=\begin{cases}\dagger,&T\leq s_{k-1},\\
(\mathsf{STOP},T-s_{k-1},X_{s_{k-1}+1:T}^{G},Z),&s_{k-1}<T\leq s_{k},\\
(\mathsf{CONTINUE},X_{s_{k-1}+1:s_{k}}^{G}),&T>s_{k}.\end{cases}



If \pi_{i,k}(h)=0, then both sides of the next display are zero. Suppose
instead that \pi_{i,k}(h)>0. The event B_{k,h}^{G} depends only on
X_{1:s_{k-1}}^{G} and on the behavioral draws at the prefixes of h, whereas
the block (\tau_{k,h},W_{k,h}) is built from the signals after s_{k-1} and
from the draws at strict extensions of h. A path visits each history at most
once, so these two index sets are disjoint, and the draws at different
histories were taken independently in Step 1. Conditioning on B_{k,h}^{G}
therefore leaves the subsequent signals and draws with their unconditional
laws, and the conditional law of W_{k}^{G} is \mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}. Multiplying that conditional
probability by \pi_{i,k}(h) gives, for every possible report w,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(B_{k,h}^{G},W_{k}^{G}=w)=\pi_{i,k}(h)\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}(w).

(74)


Comparing (73) and
(74) proves equality of every complete report conditional on its reach record.




Fix an extended history
h^{\prime}=(h,x_{1:L_{k}})\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k}}. By the recursive definition,




\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k+1,h^{\prime}})
\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}\!\left(\widetilde{B}_{k,h},\widehat{W}_{k}=(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}})\right)




\displaystyle=\pi_{i,k}(h)\mathsf{K}_{i,k,h}(\mathsf{CONTINUE},x_{1:L_{k}})




\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(B_{k+1,h^{\prime}}^{G}).



The first equality is what it means to enter the next source stage with
history h^{\prime}, the second is (73), and the third is
(74), since on the target side a
continuation report is exactly the event T>s_{k} with
X_{1:s_{k}}^{G}=h^{\prime}. This proves (72) at stage k+1.




For a stop report
w=(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z), comparison of
(73) and
(74) likewise gives equality of the source
and target probabilities of the complete stopped record. Summing this
equality over the finitely many histories
h\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{s_{k-1}}, stopping positions
r\in\{1,\ldots,L_{k}\}, and signal strings
x_{1:r}\in\mathcal{X}_{G}^{r} yields




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(Y=z,\text{ source stops in stage }k)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(Z=z,s_{k-1}<T\leq s_{k}).

(75)


The events being summed on each side are disjoint, and the report records
every index over which we sum. At time zero, both policies use the same
kernel q_{\varnothing}, so their probabilities of stopping immediately
with output z also agree. Summing that time-zero equality and
(75) over k=0,\ldots,K gives, for every K\geq 0,




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}\left(Y=z,\,\text{the source stops at time zero or in a stage }k\leq K\right)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(Z=z,T\leq s_{K}).

(76)


The target events on the right are disjoint and their union is
\{Z=z,T\leq s_{K}\}; this explains the right side of the display. We have
therefore proved both the reach induction and exactness at every finite
checkpoint.




Because T is integrable under state i, it is finite almost surely, and




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T>s_{k-1})\longrightarrow 0.



Summing (72) over h gives




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{A}_{k})=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T>s_{k-1}).



The source reach events \widetilde{A}_{k} are decreasing in k. Hence




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\text{infinitely many source stages are entered})=\lim_{k\to\infty}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{A}_{k})=0.



Thus the source construction terminates almost surely. Letting K\to\infty
in (76), the source events increase to
\{Y=z,\text{ the construction terminates}\}, while the target events
increase to \{Z=z,T<\infty\}. The first event differs from \{Y=z\}
only on the null nontermination event. The second equals \{Z=z\} almost
surely because integrability of T implies T<\infty. Continuity from
below on both probability spaces therefore gives




\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(Y=z)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(Z=z)



for every z. The terminal reproduction is exact in every state.





Step 5: Decomposing and summing the stage costs




Define the target-side number of observations used in block k by




D_{k}:=(T\wedge s_{k})-(T\wedge s_{k-1}).



These variables are nonnegative. For every K,




\sum_{k=0}^{K}D_{k}=T\wedge s_{K},



so, because s_{K}\uparrow\infty,




\sum_{k\geq 0}D_{k}=T\qquad\text{pathwise}.

(77)





Off A_{k}^{G}, the target has stopped by s_{k-1}, and the definition gives
D_{k}=0. On B_{k,h}^{G}, the policy has just continued at history h.
If it stops during the next block, D_{k} is the number of new target signals
until that stop; if it survives the block, D_{k}=L_{k}. Thus its conditional
law is exactly the law of \tau_{k,h}, and its conditional mean is
e_{i,k}(h). Conditioning on the reach-event partition gives




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}D_{k}
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[D_{k}\mathbf{1}_{A_{k}^{G}}]




\displaystyle=\sum_{h:\,\pi_{i,k}(h)>0}\pi_{i,k}(h)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}[D_{k}\mid B_{k,h}^{G}]




\displaystyle=\sum_{h}\pi_{i,k}(h)e_{i,k}(h),

(78)


using in turn that D_{k}=0 off A_{k}^{G}, total expectation over the finite
partition of A_{k}^{G}, and the conditional law just identified. Zero-mass
histories may be restored to the sum because their coefficients vanish.




On the source side, N_{k}^{F}=\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k-1} vanishes off
\widetilde{A}_{k}. Partitioning on the source reach events and applying the
tower property gives




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k}^{F}
\displaystyle=\sum_{h}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}[N_{k}^{F}\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{B}_{k,h}}]




\displaystyle=\sum_{h}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}\!\left[\mathbf{1}_{\widetilde{B}_{k,h}}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}(N_{k}^{F}\mid\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k-1}}^{F})\right]




\displaystyle=\sum_{h}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k,h})\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k,h}^{F}




\displaystyle=\sum_{h}\pi_{i,k}(h)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k,h}^{F}




\displaystyle\leq\sum_{h}\pi_{i,k}(h)\left[\rho_{i}(F\to G)e_{i,k}(h)+C_{F,G}\sqrt{L_{k}}\right]




\displaystyle=\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}D_{k}+C_{F,G}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(A_{k}^{G})\sqrt{L_{k}},

(79)


where the conditional mean (71) evaluates the
inner expectation, the reach induction (72) replaces
\mathbb{P}_{i}^{F}(\widetilde{B}_{k,h}) by \pi_{i,k}(h), and the inequality is the
stage cost (68). The last line then follows from
(78) together with
\sum_{h}\pi_{i,k}(h)=\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(A_{k}^{G}).




The sequence (\sigma_{k}) is nondecreasing and remains constant after source
termination. By (69) and the definition of its
increments, for every finite K,




\sigma_{K}=\sigma_{-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{K}N_{k}^{F}=\sum_{k=0}^{K}N_{k}^{F},



telescoping N_{k}^{F}=\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k-1} and using \sigma_{-1}=0. Letting
K\to\infty gives N=\sum_{k\geq 0}N_{k}^{F} pathwise in [0,\infty], and the
summands are nonnegative, so monotone convergence gives
\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N=\sum_{k}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k}^{F}. Summing the stagewise bounds
(79) — legitimate before finiteness is known, again by
nonnegativity — and simplifying the two resulting series yields




\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N
\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N_{k}^{F}




\displaystyle\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}D_{k}+C_{F,G}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T>s_{k-1})\sqrt{L_{k}}




\displaystyle=\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T>2^{k}-1)2^{k/2}.

(80)


For the final equality, Tonelli’s theorem and (77) give
\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}D_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\sum_{k\geq 0}D_{k}=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T.
Also A_{k}^{G}=\{T>s_{k-1}\}, while
s_{k-1}=2^{k}-1 and L_{k}=2^{k}. These identities produce the final
series in (80).





Step 6: Controlling the dyadic remainder and finishing the policy




Define




C_{\rm geo}:=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}-1}.



We first prove the following pathwise bound for every nonnegative integer
t:




\sum_{k:\,t>2^{k}-1}2^{k/2}\leq C_{\rm geo}\sqrt{t}.

(81)


When t=0, both sides are zero. When t\geq 1, choose the unique integer
r\geq 0 such that




2^{r}\leq t<2^{r+1}.



Because t is an integer,




t>2^{k}-1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad t\geq 2^{k}.



Thus the indices on the left side of (81) are exactly
k=0,\ldots,r, and




\sum_{k=0}^{r}2^{k/2}=\frac{2^{(r+1)/2}-1}{\sqrt{2}-1}\leq C_{\rm geo}\,2^{r/2}\leq C_{\rm geo}\sqrt{t}.



This proves (81).




Using Tonelli’s theorem to write the series in
(80) as an expectation, then applying
(81), gives




\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbb{P}_{i}^{G}(T>2^{k}-1)2^{k/2}
\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\sum_{k\geq 0}\mathbf{1}_{\{T>2^{k}-1\}}2^{k/2}




\displaystyle\leq C_{\rm geo}\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}\sqrt{T}




\displaystyle\leq C_{\rm geo}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T}.



The second line applies (81) with t=T on the almost
sure event \{T<\infty\}, and the third is Jensen’s inequality for the concave
square root. Substituting into
(80) produces the remainder coefficient
C_{F,G}C_{\rm geo}; since C_{\rm geo} is a numerical constant, we may
enlarge C_{F,G} once more and write




\mathbb{E}_{i}^{F}N\leq\rho_{i}(F\to G)\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T+C_{F,G}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}_{i}^{G}T}.






It remains only to verify the terminal measurability assertions. Step 3 proved
inductively that every \sigma_{k} is a stopping time. The sequence is
increasing, so in discrete time




\{N\leq n\}=\bigcap_{k\geq 0}\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{F}.



Hence N is a stopping time. The expectation bound just proved makes it
integrable, and therefore finite almost surely, in every state. On the event
of a stage-k stop, Y is part of the report measurable at
\sigma_{k}=N; on a time-zero stop it is \mathcal{F}_{0}^{F}-measurable. More
explicitly, for B\subseteq\mathcal{Z}, let E_{k,B} be the event that
stage k reports
(\mathsf{STOP},r,x_{1:r},z) for some r,x_{1:r} and some z\in B.
Step 3 gives E_{k,B}\in\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{k}}^{F}, and therefore




E_{k,B}\cap\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{F}.



The nontermination event contributes nothing to \{Y\in B,N\leq n\},
because the positive-duration convention makes N=\infty whenever
infinitely many stages are entered. Consequently,




\displaystyle\{Y\in B,N\leq n\}={}
\displaystyle\{\text{the time-zero draw is }(\mathsf{STOP},z)\text{ for some }z\in B\}




\displaystyle\cup\bigcup_{k\geq 0}\bigl(E_{k,B}\cap\{\sigma_{k}\leq n\}\bigr).



The time-zero event is in \mathcal{F}_{0}^{F}, and every event in the countable union
is in \mathcal{F}_{n}^{F}. Thus
\{Y\in B,N\leq n\}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{F} for every B,n, which is exactly
\mathcal{F}_{N}^{F}-measurability of Y. Together with the exact terminal law
established in Step 4, this completes the proof of
Theorem 2.
∎
