The physical test
The investigation reached a concrete question about cosmic acceleration:
Do the measured expansion rates require physics outside a broad class of stable, separately conserved cosmological fluids—without first choosing a fitted dark-energy equation of state?
I initially examined a galaxy-dynamics discriminator, but its distinguishing signature already had published predecessors and could arise under competing physical explanations. That was insufficient original leverage to justify developing it as a new mechanism. academic.oup.com
For the expansion test, the assumed setting was a homogeneous, spatially flat universe governed by general relativity. Each nonconstant fluid has pressure determined by density, \(p_i=p_i(\rho_i)\), and satisfies, in units with \(c=1\),
\[
\rho_i+p_i\geq 0,
\qquad
0\leq c_{s,i}^{\,2}
=\frac{dp_i}{d\rho_i}
\leq 1.
\]
A constant vacuum contribution can be included separately. This is a restricted physical class—not every stable scalar-field theory, interacting dark sector, or modification of gravity. Barotropic dark-energy models of this kind were developed well before this investigation, including by Linder and Scherrer. DOI
Derivation
Define
\[
x=a^{-3}=(1+z)^3.
\]
Separate energy conservation gives
\[
\dot\rho_i=-3H(\rho_i+p_i),
\qquad
\dot x=-3Hx,
\]
and therefore
\[
x\rho_i'(x)=\rho_i+p_i.
\]
Differentiating once more,
\[
x\rho_i''(x)=c_{s,i}^{\,2}\rho_i'(x).
\]
The assumptions consequently imply
\[
\rho_i'\geq0,
\qquad
0\leq x\rho_i''\leq\rho_i'.
\]
In the spatially flat Friedmann equation, \(H^2\) is proportional to the sum of the densities. Thus, writing \(Y(x)=H^2(x)\),
\[
Y'\geq0,
\qquad
0\leq xY''\leq Y'.
\]
This produces two observable shape restrictions. First, \(Y\) is convex in \(x\): its successive secant slopes cannot decrease. Second, \(Y\) is concave when expressed as a function of \(u=x^2\), because
\[
\frac{d^2Y}{du^2}
=
\frac{xY''-Y'}{4x^3}
\leq0.
\]
For three ordered redshifts, the first restriction becomes
\[
\frac{Y_3-Y_2}{x_3-x_2}
\geq
\frac{Y_2-Y_1}{x_2-x_1}.
\]
These are consequences of the stated assumptions, not a newly established law of nature.

1. The substantive change: test whether a fitted mechanism can exist physically
A recent proposed explanation of the gallium anomaly allows the nuclear transition density to change sign within the nucleus. Different electron wave functions then weight its positive and negative regions differently, potentially reducing neutrino-capture rates while preserving the measured germanium electron-capture lifetime. The September 30, 2026 version of the paper supplies several fitted profiles, but explicitly describes its compact profiles as phenomenological surrogates—not microscopic nuclear calculations. arXiv
I investigated a more restrictive question:
Can those fitted functions arise from normalized nuclear quantum states, even before specifying a nuclear interaction model?

This changes the calculation from finding another successful fit to imposing a necessary condition on any proposed one-body mechanism.
The physical restriction
Let \(\rho(r)\) be a reduced one-body Gamow–Teller transition density, with the axial coupling \(g_A\) factored out. Its normalization is defined by
\[
M[h]=\int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2 h(r)\rho(r)\,dr,
\]
where \(M[h]\) is the reduced matrix element of the radially weighted spin–isospin operator. This explicit normalization matters: the comparison with a phenomenological scalar density is conditional on that density having this meaning.
For the ground-state transition
\[
{}^{71}\mathrm{Ga}\longrightarrow{}^{71}\mathrm{Ge},
\]
the initial and final spins are \(3/2\) and \(1/2\). The conventional capture-rate formulas use a reduced nuclear strength divided by the initial spin multiplicity. arXiv
The bound I derived is
\[
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2|\rho(r)|\,dr
\leq
\sqrt{\frac{16}{3}N_iZ_f}
}
\]
where \(N_i\) is the initial neutron number and \(Z_f\) the final proton number. With \(N_i=40\) and \(Z_f=32\),
\[
\boxed{
\int_0^\infty 4\pi r^2|\rho(r)|\,dr
\leq 82.6236447191.
}
\]
This bound does not assume a Gaussian nuclear profile, a shell-model interaction, or a particular nuclear radius. It follows from normalized quantum states, the one-body current, and angular-momentum algebra.
2. Derivation of the bound
For any bounded radial function satisfying \(|h(r)|\leq1\), define
\[
O_q[h]
=
\int d^3r\,
h(r)\,
\psi_p^\dagger(\mathbf r)\sigma_q\psi_n(\mathbf r),
\]
where \(\sigma_q\) is a spherical component of the Pauli spin operator.
Removing a proton from the final germanium state and removing a neutron from the initial gallium state leave the same residual particle numbers. The resulting vectors therefore belong to the same residual-nucleus Hilbert space.
Collect those vectors over nuclear magnetic substates and nucleon spin:
\[
U_{M_f,s}(\mathbf r)
=
\psi_{p,s}(\mathbf r)|f,J_f,M_f\rangle,
\]
\[
V_{M_i,t}(\mathbf r)
=
\psi_{n,t}(\mathbf r)|i,J_i,M_i\rangle.
\]
Particle-number normalization gives
\[
\|U\|^2=(2J_f+1)Z_f,
\qquad
\|V\|^2=(2J_i+1)N_i.
\]
The Wigner–Eckart relation is
\[
\langle f,J_f,M_f|O_q[h]|i,J_i,M_i\rangle
=
\frac{
\langle J_iM_i;1q|J_fM_f\rangle
}{
\sqrt{2J_f+1}
}
M[h].
\]
Define the finite spin-coupling matrix
\[
K_{(M_f,s),(M_i,t)}
=
\sum_q
\langle J_iM_i;1q|J_fM_f\rangle^*
(\sigma_q)_{st}.
\]
Combining these expressions and applying Cauchy–Schwarz yields
\[
|M[h]|^2
\leq
(2J_i+1)N_iZ_f\,\|K\|^2.
\]
For \(J_i=3/2\) and \(J_f=1/2\), exact symbolic calculation gives
\[
\operatorname{spec}(KK^\dagger)
=
\left\{\frac43,\frac43,\frac43,0\right\}.
\]
Consequently,
\[
|M[h]|^2\leq\frac{16}{3}N_iZ_f.
\]
Choosing the phase of \(h(r)\) to align the contributions from \(\rho(r)\) gives the stated absolute-integral bound.
I also checked the angular coefficient independently using a single-particle \(p_{3/2}\to p_{1/2}\) spin transition. Its reduced spin matrix element squared is exactly \(16/3\), consistent with the matrix calculation.
This is a consequence of established quantum mechanics, not a newly discovered fundamental law.

It is unnecessary to optimize the sign of every profile. The bound must hold for every radial test function with \(|h|\leq1\). Simple fixed step functions therefore provide sufficient tests.
For example, for the modified double-Gaussian profile, I used
\[
h(r)=
\begin{cases}
+1,&r<3.4\ \mathrm{fm},\\
-1,&r\geq3.4\ \mathrm{fm}.
\end{cases}
\]

What this does—and does not—establish
The conditional conclusion is:
Those two printed functions cannot be conventional reduced one-body Gamow–Teller densities of normalized nuclear states under the normalization above.

The application still needs an independent audit of the paper’s scalar-density and electron-wave-function conventions. It also does not cover effective multi-nucleon axial currents.
Most importantly, it does not establish that every nuclear explanation fails. The DG profile does not fail this global test. Nor does passing the test establish that DG is physically realizable. The original authors already distinguish their phenomenological profiles from a demonstrated microscopic explanation. arXiv
Thus this is a candidate consistency objection to particular realizations—not a discovery of sterile neutrinos, and not a resolution of the gallium anomaly.
4. I extended the test beyond individual fitted functions
Rejecting selected profiles would be insufficient. I therefore derived a restriction on an entire class of transition densities.
The same argument gives a local necessary inequality,
\[
|\rho(r)|^2
\leq
\frac{16}{3}p(r)n(r),
\]
where \(p(r)\) and \(n(r)\) are appropriately averaged final-proton and initial-neutron number densities. Consequently,
\[
\int dV\,
\frac{|\rho(r)|^2}{(16/3)p(r)}
\leq N_i.
\]
Supplying \(p(r)\) turns this into a norm constraint on the unknown transition density.
Let \(b(r)\) be the electron-capture kernel and \(c(r)\) a neutrino-capture kernel. The measured lifetime constrains one weighted projection of \(\rho\); the capture amplitude probes another.
Using the weighted inner product
\[
\langle f,g\rangle_w
=
\int dV\,\frac{16}{3}p(r)f(r)^*g(r),
\]
and defining
\[
x(r)=\frac{\rho(r)}{(16/3)p(r)},
\]
we have
\[
\|x\|_w^2\leq N_i,
\qquad
B=\langle b,x\rangle_w.
\]
For real kernels, write
\[
\lambda=\frac{\langle c,b\rangle_w}{\|b\|_w^2},
\qquad
c_\perp=c-\lambda b.
\]
Orthogonal decomposition then gives
\[
\boxed{
\left|\langle c,x\rangle_w-\lambda B\right|
\leq
\sqrt{
N_i-\frac{|B|^2}{\|b\|_w^2}
}\,
\|c_\perp\|_w.
}
\]
This bounds how much the capture amplitude can differ from the lifetime-calibrated projection without selecting a Gaussian ansatz.
I evaluated its ingredients in an exploratory finite-nucleus Dirac–Coulomb calculation. It did not produce an assumption-independent experimental exclusion. The full point-proton distribution is not fixed by the measured charge radius, and the exploratory calculation did not include a self-consistent atomic treatment or multi-nucleon currents. The published electron-wave-function analysis itself uses an assumed two-parameter nuclear profile in addition to the measured rms radius. arXiv
There is also a logical limitation: this norm constraint is necessary, not sufficient. A function satisfying it need not correspond to an actual nuclear state. Conversely, failure to exclude the observations with this relaxed constraint does not establish a successful nuclear mechanism.
The extension therefore did not close the physical inference.
5. The other completed test: adding more force mediators does not rescue the calcium explanation
Before the gallium investigation, I tested the calcium isotope-shift data against a broader force construction.
Suppose a weak extra interaction is additive over neutrons and has an isotope-independent electronic response. At first perturbative order, its contribution to transition \(j\) has the form
\[
\delta\nu_j^{\mathrm{new}}(A,40)
=
X_j(A-40).
\]
A sum—or an integrable continuum—of mediator potentials merely changes \(X_j\). It does not create another independent isotope pattern under these assumptions.
After eliminating the leading nuclear-radius contribution between two transitions, I tested
\[
y=Fx+K\mu+Q\mu_2+C\gamma,
\]
with \(x,y\) the measured 729 nm and 570 nm shifts,
\[
\mu=\frac{1-1/R}{m_{40}},
\qquad
\mu_2=\frac{1-1/R^2}{m_{40}^2},
\qquad
\gamma=A-40.
\]
The four-isotope algebraic solution required
\[
Q_{\mathrm{required}}
=
+1.979934960\ \mathrm{GHz\,u^2}.
\]
Using the published central recoil coefficients, the corresponding physical recoil combination was
\[
Q_{\mathrm{recoil}}
=
-1.012869689\ \mathrm{GHz\,u^2}.
\]
The opposite-sign discrepancy was not removed by the measurement-error sensitivity checks I performed. However, this calculation deliberately omitted nuclear polarization. The primary experiment already identifies nuclear polarization as a potentially adequate Standard Model contribution to the observed nonlinearity. arXiv
The conclusion is therefore limited: adding more mediators within this factorized force class does not make the incomplete explanation sufficient. It is not an exclusion of arbitrary new physics, and the exact four-parameter fit is not evidence for a force.
