Horospherically convex optimization for
fractional subspace packing and its applications
Hiroshi Hirai
Abstract
In this paper, we address a semi-infinite LP relaxation of the vector-subspace packing problem.
This is a higher-dimensional generalization of the fractional linear matroid parity problem
and is closely related to Brascamp-Lieb polytopes.
We show that the dual of this LP can be formulated as “linear programming on a Euclidean building,” namely, the problem of minimizing a Busemann function over an intersection of horoballs.
This provides a natural example of horospherically convex optimization, recently introduced by Goodwin et al. (2026) and Criscitiello and Kim (2025).
By applying the incremental Busemann subgradient method,
we obtain an additive FPTAS for the problem.
As applications, we obtain a new and simpler polynomial-time algorithm
for fractional linear matroid parity, and new algorithms
for the membership problem of Brascamp-Lieb polytopes.
Keywords: Horospherically convex optimization, geodesically convex optimization, Busemann function, Hadamard space, Euclidean building, fractional matroid parity, Brascamp-Lieb polytope

1 Introduction
A vector-space generalization of the well-known set packing problem
and its dual, the hitting set problem (or set cover problem) can be formulated as follows:
Let \mathbb{K} be a field. We are given a collection of vector subspaces S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{m} in \mathbb{K}^{n}.
Subspace packing problem:
Find a subset I\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,m\} of indices with the maximum cardinality
such that the vector-space sum \sum_{k\in I}S_{k} is equal to the direct sum \bigoplus_{k\in I}S_{k}.
Hitting subspace problem:
Find a vector subspace X in \mathbb{K}^{n} with the minimum dimension
such that it
intersects all S_{k} nontrivially (S_{k}\cap X\neq\{{\bf 0}\}).
When each S_{k} is a coordinate subspace,
they are the set packing and hitting set problems, respectively.
As in this case,
the two problems are in weak duality \max_{I}|I|\leq\min_{X}\dim X.
When \mathbb{K}^{n}=U\oplus V and S_{k} is the 2-dimensional subspace spanned by u_{k}\in U,v_{k}\in V for k=1,2,\ldots,m, the subspace packing problem is the linear matroid intersection problem,
and the strong duality is implied by the matroid intersection theorem.
For general 2-dimensional subspaces S_{k}, though the strong duality does not hold,
the problem is known as the linear matroid parity problem and a famous result by Lovász [28] says that it is polynomial-time solvable.
When each S_{k} is d-dimensional,
the problem is viewed as the matroid d-parity problem [26] on a linearly represented matroid.
For the hitting subspace problem,
we could not find any work from an optimization point of view.
This may be because it appears to be an intractably difficult infinite discrete optimization problem.
We found one paper [13] which studies this problem
from different perspectives of low-rank matrix completion;
the name is taken from that paper.
The focus of the present paper is their fractional relaxations.
If \sum_{k\in I}S_{k}=\bigoplus_{k\in I}S_{k}, then
for every vector subspace X it holds \sum_{k\in I}S_{k}\cap X=\bigoplus_{k\in I}S_{k}\cap X,
and hence \sum_{k\in I}\dim S_{k}\cap X=\dim\sum_{k\in I}S_{k}\cap X\leq\dim X.
This leads to the following semi-infinite LP relaxation of the subspace packing problem:
Fractional subspace packing problem:
Max.
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}
s.t.
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\dim S_{k}\cap X\leq\dim X\quad(X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})),
(1.1)
\displaystyle x_{k}\geq 0\quad(k=1,2,\ldots,m),
where {\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}) denotes the set of all vector subspaces of \mathbb{K}^{n}.
This LP was introduced by Vande Vate [32]
as an LP relaxation of the matroid parity problem,
where a feasible solution is a fractional matroid matching.
It was also considered by Lee, Sviridenko, and Vondrák [26] for matroid d-parity.
Notice that the matroid for our setting
is the (infinite) matroid of all vectors of \mathbb{K}^{n}.
As we will explain later,
LP (1.1) has renewed interest due to
its connections to the noncommutative rank (nc-rank) [24] and the Brascamp-Lieb inequality [5, 27],
which are the main motivation for this work.
On the other hand, from X\cap S_{k}\neq\{{\bf 0}\}\Leftrightarrow\dim X\cap S_{k}\geq 1,
an LP relaxation of the hitting subspace problem is naturally obtained as:
Fractional hitting subspace problem:
Min.
\displaystyle\sum_{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}\lambda(X)\dim X
s.t.
\displaystyle\sum_{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})}\lambda(X)\dim S_{k}\cap X\geq 1\quad(k\in[m]),
(1.2)
\displaystyle\lambda:{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0},\ |\mathop{\rm supp}\lambda|<\infty,
where \mathop{\rm supp}\lambda denotes the nonzero support \{X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})\mid\lambda(X)\neq 0\}.
We call a feasible solution a fractional hitting subspace.
Observe that (1.2) is LP-dual to (1.1).
Furthermore, the strong duality holds,
as they are implicitly finite-dimensional LPs. Indeed, the possible coefficients of inequalities of these two LPs are finite:
(\dim X,\dim S_{1}\cap X,\ldots,\dim S_{m}\cap X)\in\{0,1,2\ldots,n\}^{m+1}.
(1.3)
The optimal value (the fractional packing/hitting number) is denoted by \tau^{*}.
Although they reduce to finite-dimensional LPs,
executing the reduction is extremely difficult; determining the possible coefficients
requires to solve algebraic equations exponentially many times.
We know nothing about
the computational complexity of these LPs,
except for the 2-dimensional case, namely, the fractional linear matroid parity problem.
For this case, Chang, Llewellyn, and Vande Vate [8, 9] gave a polynomial-time algorithm (CLV-algorithm),
and Oki and Soma [30] gave a faster randomized algorithm based on the connection to the nc-rank.
In this paper, we tackle the semi-infinite LP (1.2) for the general case of S_{k},
where computation on an abstract field \mathbb{K} is performed by an algebraic RAM.
That is, we assume:
(A)
Every element in \mathbb{K} is kept in constant space,
and arithmetic operations +,-,\times,/ (and comparison) in \mathbb{K} are done in constant time.
The main result of this paper
is an additive FPTAS for the fractional hitting subspace problem (1.2):
Theorem 1.1.
For \epsilon>0, we can compute in O(n^{5}m^{3}\epsilon^{-2}) time a fractional hitting subspace \lambda with
\sum_{X}\lambda(X)\dim X\leq\tau^{*}+\epsilon.
(1.4)
Since \tau^{*}\geq 1, this also yields a multiplicative FPTAS.
Note that LP (1.2) is very fractional;
it has no (half-)integrality property of extreme points.
To the best of our knowledge, there are no comparable results
on the computational complexity for such semi-infinite linear programming.
For proving this result, we utilize
geodesically convex optimization
on nonpositively curved spaces called Hadamard spaces.
Deriving an explicit computational complexity
via optimization on such spaces was pioneered by the work of
Garg et al.[15] who developed a polynomial-time algorithm for the nc-rank
by operator scaling.
It turned out that operator scaling is a convex optimization on the Hadamard manifold of positive definite matrices; see e.g., [7] for subsequent wonderful developments.
Another polynomial-time algorithm for nc-rank due to Hamada and Hirai [20] is based on convex optimization on a
Euclidean building—a representative example of Hadamard spaces without manifold structure.
Our approach to (1.2) is fully inspired by this work.
By the well-known uncrossing argument,
the support of \lambda in (1.2) can be assumed to be a flag—a chain of vector subspaces.
Then, the (nonconvex) subset of feasible solutions with flag-support has the structure of a Euclidean building.
Further, the objective function is geodesically convex on this space.
As done in [20],
the splitting proximal point algorithm by Bačák [2] and Ohta-Pálfia [29] is applicable to obtain an approximate solution.
However, due to the lack of the strong convexity of the objective,
the desired complexity estimate cannot be deduced.
Instead, we exploit another convexity property,
called the horospherical convexity (or Busemann subdifferentiability).
This notion was introduced independently by Goodwin et al. [18] and Criscitiello and Kim [10] for
overcoming the curvature obstruction in first-order methods in Hadamard spaces/manifolds; see also
a preceding work [17].
Analogously to Euclidean convex functions,
horospherically convex functions are defined as those supported everywhere
by affine-function analogues in Hadamard spaces, called Busemann functions.
We show that (1.2) is naturally formulated as “linear programming on Euclidean building”—the problem of
minimizing a Busemann function over a convex set defined by Busemann functions (i.e., intersection of horoballs).
This is an ideal example of horospherically convex optimization
for which the incremental Busemann subgradient method [18]
is applicable.
Then Theorem 1.1 is obtained from the convergence result in [18].
Since the derivation is surprisingly natural,
we believe that (1.2)
will serve as a standard example of horospherically convex optimization in the future.
The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Section 2,
we introduce necessary terminologies, lemmas, and
tools on Hadamard spaces, horospherically
convex optimization, and Euclidean buildings.
In Section 3,
we consider a slightly general version of (1.2),
formulate it as a horospherically convex optimization,
and obtain a refined version of Theorem 1.1.
This also yields an FPT-algorithm computing \tau, where the dimension n is the fixed parameter.
In Section 4,
we present applications. The first application is
a new deterministic polynomial-time algorithm for solving
fractional linear matroid parity.
This is an immediate application of the incremental subgradient method and the half-integrality of the problem.
The resulting algorithm is slower than the CLV-algorithm [8, 9] but considerably simpler.
The second application concerns the computational complexity of Brascamp-Lieb polytopes (BL-polytopes) [3],
which is our motivating target.
The BL-polytope is the parameter space for which the BL-inequality is nontrivial.
Since the pseudo-polynomial time membership algorithm by Garg et al. [14],
the computational complexity of BL-polytopes has attracted interest in
theoretical computer science.
As pointed out by Franks, Soma, and Goemans [12],
the BL-polytope is the face of the feasible region of (1.1)
determined by \sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\dim S_{k}\cap X=\dim X for X=\mathbb{K}^{n}(=\mathbb{R}^{n}).
By using our results, we present new algorithms for
approximate membership of BL-polytopes.
However, assumption (A) may not be appropriate for studying the computational complexity of BL-polytopes.
Also our algorithms have no guarantee of polynomial bit-complexity when
dealing with rational inputs in the standard RAM model, unfortunately.
Nevertheless, they are conceptually different from existing operator-scaling-based algorithms [12, 14], and are expected to stimulate future research.
In the additional Section 5, we verify that our problems actually generalize fractional set packing and cover problems.
Specifically,
we show the not-quite-obvious fact that if each S_{k} is a coordinate subspace,
then \mathop{\rm supp}\lambda in (1.2) can be assumed to consist of coordinate subspaces.
We point out as a consequence of this fact that the BL-polytope defined by coordinate projections
is equal to the perfect fractional set-packing polytope,
which sharpens a result of Finner [11] on a generalization of Hölder’s inequality.
2 Preliminaries
For positive integers m,n, let [n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\} and [m,n]:=\{m,m+1,\ldots,n\}.
For a subset J\subseteq[n],
let {\bf 1}_{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} denote the characteristic vector
defined by ({\bf 1}_{J})_{i}:=1 if i\in J and ({\bf 1}_{J})_{i}:=0 otherwise.
If J=\{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\}, then {\bf 1}_{J} is also written as
{\bf 1}_{j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}}.
The all-one vector {\bf 1}_{[n]} is simply denoted by {\bf 1}. The zero vector is denoted by {\bf 0}.
For two vectors p,q\in\mathbb{R}^{n}, define \min(p,q),\max(p,q)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
by (\min(p,q))_{i}:=\min(p_{i},q_{i}), (\max(p,q))_{i}:=\max(p_{i},q_{i}) (i\in[n]).
Let \mathbb{R}^{n}_{\downarrow} denote the set of vectors
p\in\mathbb{R}^{n} with p_{1}\geq p_{2}\geq\cdots\geq p_{n}.
2.1 Linear algebra
For vectors v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\in\mathbb{K}^{n},
let \langle v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}\rangle denote the vector subspace spanned by v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{k}.
The following property (supermodularity)
of the dimension function is elementary.
Lemma 2.1 (see [23, Lemma 4.5]).
Let S,X,Y\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}) be vector subspaces.
(1)
\dim S\cap X+\dim S\cap Y\leq\dim S\cap(X\cap Y)+\dim S\cap(X+Y).
(2)
(\dim X)^{2}+(\dim Y)^{2}<(\dim X\cap Y)^{2}+(\dim X+Y)^{2} if X\not\subseteq Y and Y\not\subseteq X.
Proof.
(1). Use \dim U+\dim V=\dim U\cap V+\dim U+V and \dim((S\cap X)+(S\cap Y))\leq\dim S\cap(X+Y).
(2). There is a basis v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
such that X=\langle v_{i}\mid i\in I\rangle and Y=\langle v_{j}\mid j\in J\rangle for some I,J\subseteq[n] ; see Lemma 2.2 below. Then
\dim X=|I|, \dim Y=|J|, \dim X\cap Y=|I\cap J|, and \dim X+Y=|I\cup J|.
Then the statement follows
from the strict convexity x\mapsto x^{2}
and |I\cap J|<\min(|I|,|J|)\leq\max(|I|,|J|)<|I\cup J|.
∎
A flag is a family of vector subspaces X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{m} with X_{1}\subset X_{2}\subset\cdots\subset X_{m}.
If m=n, \dim X_{i}=i, and X_{n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}, then
it is called a complete flag.
A complete flag is denoted by a calligraphic letter, such as {\cal X},
where the vector subspace with dimension i in {\cal X} is denoted by X_{i}.
The following property is also standard.
Lemma 2.2 (See e.g., [6, Part II, Lemma 10.80]).
For two complete flags {\cal X},{\cal Y},
there is a basis v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n} of \mathbb{K}^{n}
such that every vector subspace in {\cal X}\cup{\cal Y}
is spanned by some subset of v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}.
We need an algorithm for constructing such a basis in the following special case.
Lemma 2.3.
Let v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n} be a basis of \mathbb{K}^{n} and let s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}
be linearly independent vectors in \mathbb{K}^{n}.
We can compute by O(n^{3}) arithmetic operations of \mathbb{K} a basis u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{n} and indices j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}\in[n]
such that
\langle v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{i}\rangle=\langle u_{1},u_{2},\ldots,u_{i}\rangle for i\in[n]
and \langle s_{1},s_{2},\ldots,s_{k}\rangle=\langle u_{j_{1}},u_{j_{2}},\ldots u_{j_{k}}\rangle.
Proof.
The algorithm is based on Gaussian elimination.
Let V:=(v_{1}\ v_{2}\ \cdots\ v_{n}) and S:=(s_{1}\ s_{2}\ \cdots\ s_{k}), and
consider the block matrix (V\ S).
Multiply V^{-1} from the left to obtain (I\ V^{-1}S).
Next compute the column echelon form L of V^{-1}S,
where there are 1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{k}\leq n
such that L_{ih}=0 for i>j_{h} and h\in[k].
Let e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{n} be the column vectors of I(the standard basis).
Define another basis e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{n}
by e^{\prime}_{j}:=\ell_{i} if j=j_{i} and e^{\prime}_{j}:=e_{j} otherwise,
where \ell_{i} is the i-th column vector of L.
Since (e^{\prime}_{1}\ e^{\prime}_{2}\ \cdots e^{\prime}_{n}) is an upper triangular matrix
with nonzero diagonals, it holds \langle e_{1},e_{2},\ldots,e_{i}\rangle=\langle e^{\prime}_{1},e^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{i}\rangle
for i\in[n].
Also \langle e^{\prime}_{j_{1}},e^{\prime}_{j_{2}},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{j_{k}}\rangle=\langle V^{-1}s_{j}\mid j\in[k]\rangle.
Then we obtain a desired basis u_{i}:=Ve^{\prime}_{i} (i\in[n]) and indices j_{1},j_{2},\ldots,j_{k}.
This requires O(n^{3}) arithmetic operations.
∎
2.2 Hadamard spaces
We recall the basic notions of Hadamard spaces and Busemann functions; see [2, 6] for details.
In a metric space ({\cal H},d), a geodesic is a path \gamma:[a,b]\to{\cal H}
such that d(\gamma(s),\gamma(t))=d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))|s-t|/|a-b| holds for s,t\in[a,b],
where s_{\gamma}:=d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))/|a-b| is called the speed of \gamma.
If s_{\gamma}=1, then \gamma is called a unit-speed geodesic.
Note that t\mapsto\gamma(t/s_{\gamma}) is a unit-speed geodesic for any geodesic \gamma.
The constant function t\mapsto p is regarded as a geodesic with zero speed.
A geodesic metric space is a metric space ({\cal H},d)
such that every pair of points x,y is connected by a geodesic.
A geodesic metric space ({\cal H},d) is called CAT(0) if for every y\in{\cal H} and geodesic \gamma:[a,b]\to{\cal H}
the function t\mapsto\frac{1}{2}d(y,\gamma(t))^{2}
is 1-strongly convex.
In a CAT(0) space, a geodesic connecting any pair of points is uniquely determined.
A complete CAT(0) space is particularly called a Hadamard space.
Let ({\cal H},d) be a Hadamard space.
A subset K\subseteq{\cal H} is called convex if
a geodesic connecting any pair of points in K belongs to K.
Let K\subseteq{\cal H} be a closed convex set.
For a point x in {\cal H},
there is a unique point x^{*} in K
having the minimum distance \min_{y\in K}d(x,y) from x to K.
This gives rise to the projection \mathop{\rm proj}_{K}:{\cal H}\to K
defined by x\mapsto x^{*}.
A function f:{\cal H}\to\mathbb{R} is called convex
if for every geodesic \gamma:[a,b]\to{\cal H}
one-dimensional function t\mapsto f(\gamma(t)) is convex.
We recall an important class of convex functions, called Busemann functions,
which are determined by geodesic rays.
By a geodesic ray we mean \gamma:[0,\infty)\to{\cal H}
such that the restriction \gamma|_{[a,b]} for each a,b\in[0,\infty) is a geodesic, where
the speed of \gamma is (well-)defined by s_{\gamma}:=d(\gamma(a),\gamma(b))/|a-b| (a\neq b).
The Busemann function b_{\gamma}:{\cal H}\to\mathbb{R} associated with a geodesic ray \gamma is defined by
b_{\gamma}(x):=s_{\gamma}\lim_{t\to\infty}d(x,\gamma(t/s_{\gamma}))-t\quad(x\in{\cal H}),
(2.1)
where we let b_{\gamma}(x):=0 if s_{\gamma}=0.
Busemann functions are analogues of affine functions in Euclidean space.
To see this, suppose that {\cal H}=\mathbb{R}^{n} and \gamma(t)=a+vt for
a,v\in\mathbb{R}^{n} with v\neq{\bf 0}.
Letting u:=v/{\|v\|_{2}}, we have
\displaystyle b_{\gamma}(x)=\|v\|_{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\|x-a-ut\|_{2}-t=\|v\|_{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\|x-a-ut\|^{2}_{2}-t^{2}}{\|x-a-ut\|_{2}+t}
\displaystyle=\|v\|_{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\|x-a\|_{2}^{2}-2u^{\top}(x-a)t}{\|x-a-ut\|_{2}+t}=\|v\|_{2}\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\|x-a\|_{2}^{2}/t-2u^{\top}(x-a)}{\|(x-a)/t-u\|_{2}+1}
\displaystyle=-\|v\|_{2}u^{\top}(x-a)=-v^{\top}(x-a).
(2.2)
2.3 Horospherically convex optimization
Here we briefly introduce horospherically convex optimization [10, 18].
Let ({\cal H},d) be a Hadamard space and K\subseteq{\cal H} a convex set.
Let f:K\to\mathbb{R} be a real-valued function.
A Busemann subgradient of f at point x\in K is
a geodesic ray \gamma:[0,\infty)\to{\cal H} with \gamma(0)=x satisfying
f(y)\geq f(x)+b_{\gamma}(y)\quad(y\in K).
(2.3)
Note that [18] defines the Busemann subgradient as a pair (s,\gamma) of s\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} and unit-speed geodesic
ray \gamma issuing at x such that f(y)\geq f(x)+sb_{\gamma}(y) for all y\in{\cal H}.
The equivalence is clear by the correspondence \gamma(t)\Leftrightarrow(s_{\gamma},\gamma(t/s_{\gamma})).
Then f:K\to\mathbb{R} is called horospherically convex [10],
or Busemann subdifferentiable [18],
if it admits a Busemann subgradient at every point in K.
Suppose that
{\cal H} has the geodesic extension property, that is,
every geodesic can be extended to a geodesic ray.
As in [18],
we consider the minimization of a sum of horospherically convex functions f_{0},f_{1},\dots,f_{m} defined on a closed convex set K\subseteq{\cal H}
\mbox{Min.}\quad f(x):=\sum_{k=0}^{m}f_{k}(x)\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad x\in K.
(2.4)
To apply the incremental Busemann subgradient method in [18], we assume:
•
\inf_{x\in K}f(x)=:f^{*}>-\infty, and there is x^{*}\in K with f(x^{*})=f^{*}.
•
The upper bound D of the diameter of K is known in advance.
•
An initial point x^{0}\in K is available.
•
A Busemann subgradient \gamma of each f_{k} at any x\in K is available,
where the speed s_{\gamma} is bounded by some constant L known in advance.
•
The projection \mathop{\rm proj}_{K}:{\cal H}\to K is available.
Incremental Busemann subgradient method
[18, Algorithm 2]
0:
x^{0}\leftarrow any point in K, i\leftarrow 0.
1:
t_{i}\leftarrow step size.
2:
For k=0,1,2,\ldots,m, do
2-1:
\gamma\leftarrow a Busemann subgradient of f_{k} at x^{i}, where s_{\gamma}\leq L.
2-2
x^{i}\leftarrow\mathop{\rm proj}_{K}(\gamma(t_{i})).
3:
x^{i+1}\leftarrow x^{i}, i\leftarrow i+1 and go to 1.
Then, the following convergence result holds,
where we state it in a slightly weaker form to allow rational step sizes.
Theorem 2.4 ([18, Theorem 6.4]).
Suppose that the step size t_{i} satisfies
\frac{D}{2L(m+1)\sqrt{i+1}}\leq t_{i}\leq\frac{D}{L(m+1)\sqrt{i+1}}.
(2.5)
Then it holds
\min_{i=1,2,\ldots,N}f(x^{i})-f^{*}\leq\frac{4(1+\log 3)(m+1)LD}{{\sqrt{N+2}}}.
(2.6)
Proof.
This follows from modifying the last step of their proof as
\displaystyle\min_{i=1,2,\ldots,N}f(x^{i})-f^{*}\leq\frac{D^{2}+L^{2}(m+1)^{2}\sum_{j=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N}t_{j}^{2}}{2\sum_{j=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N}t_{j}}
\displaystyle\leq(m+1)LD\frac{1+\sum_{j=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N}1/(j+1)}{\sum_{j=\lceil N/2\rceil}^{N}1/\sqrt{j+1}}\leq\frac{4(1+\log 3)(m+1)LD}{{\sqrt{N+2}}}.
∎
2.4 Euclidean buildings
Euclidean buildings [1] constitute a representative class of Hadamard spaces without manifold structure. We will utilize special Euclidean buildings obtained
from complete flags of \mathbb{K}^{n}; for our purpose,
[6, p.340–346] is a useful reference.
The Euclidean building used in this paper, denoted by {\cal B}={\cal B}(\mathbb{K}^{n}), is the set of all pairs (p,{\cal X}) of nonincreasing vectors p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\downarrow} and complete flags {\cal X} of \mathbb{K}^{n},
modulo the equivalence relation:
(p,{\cal X})\sim(q,{\cal Y}) \Leftrightarrow p=q
and X_{i}=Y_{i} if p_{i}>p_{i+1}.
An equivalence class [(p,{\cal X})] is also denoted by (p,{\cal X}).
The space {\cal B} can be viewed as the product of \mathbb{R}
and the Euclidean cone over the spherical building of type A; in particular, it is a Euclidean building.
For a fixed flag {\cal X},
the set of all (p,{\cal X}) for p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\downarrow} is called a chamber.
Then {\cal B} is
obtained by gluing chambers along \sim.
The distance on each chamber \Delta is induced by the bijection (p,{\cal X})\mapsto p
from \Delta to a convex cone \mathbb{R}^{n}_{\downarrow} in Euclidean space \mathbb{R}^{n}.
Extending the distance d on each chamber geodesically,
the building {\cal B} becomes a geodesic metric space, and is actually a Hadamard space.
A point (p,{\cal X}) is uniquely represented by
a formal sum
of vector subspaces as
(p,{\cal X})\ \longleftrightarrow\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}(p_{i}-p_{i+1})X_{i},
(2.7)
where p_{n+1}:=0, and the coefficient of X_{i} is nonnegative except that of X_{n}=\mathbb{K}^{n}.
In this expression, the zero vector space \{{\bf 0}\} corresponds to the zero element.
An apartment {\cal A}={\cal A}(v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})
associated with an ordered basis (v_{1},v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}) of \mathbb{K}^{n}
is the union of n! chambers for flags of form
\{0\}<\langle v_{i_{1}}\rangle<\langle v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}}\rangle<\cdots<\langle v_{i_{1}},v_{i_{2}},\ldots,v_{i_{n}}\rangle=\mathbb{K}^{n},
where (i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{n}) is a permutation of [n].
An apartment is an isometric subspace isometric to \mathbb{R}^{n}.

3 Formulation and algorithm
3.1 Formulation
Let S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{m}
be vector subspaces of \mathbb{K}^{n}.
For applications in Section 4,

Observe that the inequality system in (1.1) includes
an upper bound condition x_{k}\leq 1, which is implied by \sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\dim(S_{k}\cap X)\leq\dim X for X=S_{k}.
So we allow a possibly stricter rational upper bound u\in([0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q})^{m}.
Further we consider a nonnegative integer weight c\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m}, where c_{\rm max}:=\max_{k\in[m]}c_{k}.
Our target LP is the following:
Max.
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}c_{k}x_{k}
s.t.
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}\dim(S_{k}\cap X)\leq\dim X\quad(X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n})),
(3.1)
\displaystyle 0\leq x_{k}\leq u_{k}\quad(k\in[m]).

Min.

Define d^{(k)}\in\{0,1\}^{n} by (d^{(k)})_{i}:=\dim S_{k}\cap X_{i}-\dim S_{k}\cap X_{i-1}.
Then we obtain a contradiction to the feasibility:
1\leq p^{\top}d^{(k)}=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\rho_{i}\left(\frac{{\bf 1}_{[\alpha_{i}]}+{\bf 1}_{[\beta_{i}]}}{2}\right)^{\top}d^{(k)}\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\rho_{i}\frac{\dim X_{\alpha_{i}}\cap S_{k}+\dim X_{\beta_{i}}\cap S_{k}}{2}<1.\qed
Therefore,
by the incremental Busemann subgradient method with \epsilon<1/2 and
u={\bf 1},
we obtain a solution (p,{\cal X}) with
\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}-\tau^{*}<1/2.
Decompose p as in (4.3),
and obtain 2-covers (X_{\alpha_{i}},X_{\beta_{i}}), one of which is an optimal 2-cover.
Thus, we can obtain an optimal 2-cover and optimal value \tau^{*} in O(n^{5}m^{3}) time.
For k=1,2,\ldots,m,
apply (again) the incremental Busemann subgradient method with u_{k}=0,1/2.
Fix u_{k} as the minimum of u_{k}=0,1/2,1 so that the optimal value does not decrease.
Then the resulting u is a half-integral optimal fractional matching.
The whole procedure is done
in O(n^{5}m^{4}) time.
This is slower than the CLV-algorithm [8] that requires m^{2} calls of a linear matroid intersection algorithm; the current fastest one [21] has time complexity O(n^{\omega-1}m), where \omega denotes the matrix multiplication exponent.
An advantage of our algorithm is its simplicity,
whereas the CLV-algorithm is complicated and needs a rather involved proof of correctness.
We hope that further development
on horospherically convex optimization
will lead to a better algorithm.
4.2 Brascamp-Lieb polytopes
Our motivation for studying the fractional subspace packing problem (1.1) comes from the Brascamp-Lieb inequality [5, 27].
Given surjective linear maps B_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}} and nonnegative reals
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} for k\in[m] (BL-datum),
the Brascamp-Lieb inequality says that
for some constant C\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\cup\{+\infty\} it holds
\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(B_{k}x)^{u_{k}}{\rm d}x\leq C\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\int_{x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}}}f_{k}(x_{k}){\rm d}x_{k}\right)^{u_{k}}
(4.7)
for any nonnegative-valued measurable functions f_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} (k\in[m]).
The minimum possible C, denoted by C_{\rm BL}, is called
the BL-constant.
The interesting case of the BL-inequality is
when C_{\rm BL}<\infty, and is characterized by Bennett et al. [3] as follows:
Theorem 4.3 ([3]).
Given BL-datum B_{k}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}}, u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} (k\in[m]),
the BL-constant is finite if and only if
it holds
n=\sum_{k=1}^{m}r_{k}u_{k} and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim B_{k}V\geq\dim V
for every V\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
The BL-polytope {\rm BLP}({\cal B}) associated with {\cal B}=\{B_{k}\}_{k\in[m]} is defined as a rational polytope of
all vectors u=(u_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0} satisfying the condition in Theorem 4.3.
We suppose that each B_{k} is given as an r_{k}\times n matrix with full row rank.
The computational complexity of BL-polytopes is an intriguing open problem in theoretical computer science.
It is not known whether the membership problem for BL-polytopes is NP-hard or polynomial-time solvable.
The current best result is due to Garg et al. [14] who gave a polynomial-time membership algorithm
for input rational vectors u having a bounded common denominator of entries.
For the special case of rank-2 matrices B_{k},
Hirai et al. [23] showed a polynomial-time membership algorithm by utilizing the connection
to the fractional linear matroid parity.
As suggested in [12, Lemma 7.1] (see also [4, Proposition 4.3]),
the inequalities \sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim B_{k}V\geq\dim V
are linked to the subspace packing condition
\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim S_{k}\cap X\leq\dim X as follows:
For k\in[m], define subspaces S_{k}\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}) by
S_{k}:={\rm Im}B_{k}^{\top},
(4.8)
Then \dim S_{k}=r_{k}.
Observe that u\in(B_{k}V)^{\bot} if and only if B_{k}^{\top}u\in V^{\bot}, where (\cdot)^{\bot} denotes
the orthogonal complement with respect to the standard inner product.
Since u\mapsto B_{k}^{\top}u is injective, it holds
\dim(B_{k}V)^{\bot}=\dim\{u\in V\mid B_{k}^{\top}u\in V^{\bot}\}=\dim{\rm Im}B_{k}^{\top}\cap V^{\bot}=\dim S_{k}\cap V^{\bot}.
With X:=V^{\bot} and \dim B_{k}V=r_{k}-\dim S_{k}\cap X, we have
\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim B_{k}V-\dim V
\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim S_{k}\cap X+\dim X-\left(n-\sum_{k=1}^{m}r_{k}u_{k}\right).
(4.9)
The third term of RHS is zero if and only if \dim\mathbb{R}^{n}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim S_{k}\cap\mathbb{R}^{n}.
Thus, the BL-polytope is the face of the feasible region of (1.1).
In particular, it is
viewed as the polytope of “perfect” fractional subspace packings.
To formulate approximate membership,
we consider a relaxation of the BL-polytope.
For \epsilon\geq 0, the \epsilon-relaxed BL-polytope {\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B})
is defined as the set of all vectors u satisfying
n\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}r_{k}\geq n-\epsilon
and \sum_{k=1}^{m}u_{k}\dim B_{k}V\geq\dim V-\epsilon for every V\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).
Consider LP (3.2) with weight r=(r_{k}) and capacity u.
From the relation (4.9), we have:
Lemma 4.4.
For \epsilon\geq 0 and u\in[0,1]^{m}, if \tau_{r,u}^{*}\geq n-\epsilon, then {\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B})\cap[0,u]\neq\emptyset.
Consider a rational vector u\in\mathbb{Q}^{m} satisfying necessary conditions \sum_{k=1}^{m}r_{k}u_{k}=n and 0\leq u_{k}\leq 1 (k\in[m]) for
belonging to {\rm BLP}({\cal B}).
Apply the incremental Busemann subgradient method for (3.9) with accuracy \epsilon>0.
If the obtained solution (p,{\cal X}) with f(p,{\cal X})\leq\tau^{*}_{r,u}+\epsilon also satisfies n\leq f(p,{\cal X}),
then there is x\in{\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B}) with x\leq u, and necessarily u\in{\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B}).
Otherwise n>f(p,{\cal X})\geq\tau^{*}_{r,u}.
This means that no feasible solution in (3.1) satisfies \sum_{k=1}^{m}r_{k}x_{k}=n, and hence
u\not\in{\rm BLP}({\cal B}).
Thus, we have the following result on the membership of the BL-polytope, which does not depend on the common denominator of an input vector u.
Theorem 4.5.
For \epsilon>0,
we can decide in O(n^{7}m^{3}\epsilon^{-2}) time
whether a given rational vector u\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}_{\geq 0} belongs to
{\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B}) or does not belong to {\rm BLP}({\cal B}).
The BL-polytope {\rm BLP}({\cal B}) can be empty, that is,
the BL-inequality (4.7) fails for every u and every finite C.
This motivates us to consider the problem of deciding nonemptiness of the BL-polytope.
With u={\bf 1}, apply Lemma 4.4 and Theorems 3.5 and 3.7.
Then we have:
Theorem 4.6.
(1)
For \epsilon>0,
we can decide in O(n^{7}m^{3}\epsilon^{-2}) time
whether {\rm BLP}_{\epsilon}({\cal B})\neq\emptyset or
{\rm BLP}({\cal B})=\emptyset.
(2)
We can decide nonemptiness of
{\rm BLP}({\cal B}) in O(n^{2n+7}m^{3}) time.
These appear be the first algorithms that run on a real RAM with real inputs ({\cal B},u).
However, for rational inputs, assumption (A) may not be appropriate from the viewpoint of bit-complexity,
and a polynomial-time algorithm in the standard RAM model would be desirable.
Our algorithms also run on the RAM model when \mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
but they cannot be polynomial-time algorithms, unfortunately.
This is because
successive flag operations (Lemma 2.3)
can cause an exponential explosion of the bit-length of
the basis representing flag {\cal X}, even if the number of iterations is polynomially bounded.
This bit-complexity issue arises in the nc-rank computation algorithm in [20]
and its generalization in [23].
In these settings, the rational arithmetic can be avoided
by a reduction to finite fields of polynomial size. We do not know whether
such a finite-field reduction is applicable to the BL-polytope problems.
Further analysis must be left to future research.
5 Relation to set packing and covering LPs
In this section,
we verify that, when each S_{k} is a coordinate subspace,
(1.1) and (1.2)
reduce to the usual primal-dual pair of set packing and covering LPs.
For a subset J\subseteq[n], let \mathbb{K}^{J} denote
the coordinate subspace \{v\in\mathbb{K}^{n}\mid v_{i}=0\ (i\in[n]\setminus J)\}.
Lemma 5.1.
Let X\in{\cal S}(\mathbb{K}^{n}), and
let A be an n\times k matrix whose column vectors are a basis of X.
If the submatrix of A with row indices i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k} is nonsingular,
then for any J\subseteq[n] it holds
\dim X\cap\mathbb{K}^{J}\leq|\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}\cap J|=\dim\mathbb{K}^{\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}}\cap\mathbb{K}^{J}.
(5.1)
Proof.
We can suppose that the first \ell columns of A form a basis of X\cap\mathbb{K}^{J}, where
\dim X\cap\mathbb{K}^{J}=\ell.
Then, A_{ij}=0 holds for (i,j)\in([n]\setminus J)\times[\ell], and every maximal linearly independent subset of row vectors of A must include
\ell rows in J. This implies (5.1).
∎
Therefore, if each S_{k} is the coordinate subspace \mathbb{K}^{J_{k}} for J_{k}\subseteq[n],
then we can replace vector subspaces X by the above coordinate subspaces \mathbb{K}^{\{i_{1},i_{2},\ldots,i_{k}\}}
in
(1.1), (1.2), and the hitting subspace problem.
Since the inequality \sum_{k}x_{k}|J_{k}\cap I|\leq|I| for \mathbb{K}^{I} is implied
by \sum_{k=1}^{m}x_{k}|J_{k}\cap\{i\}|\leq 1 for \mathbb{K}^{\{i\}}, (1.1) is the set packing LP:
\mbox{Max.}\quad{\bf 1}^{\top}x\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\bf 1}_{J_{k}}x_{k}\leq{\bf 1},\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}.
(5.2)
One can also see that (1.2) is equivalent to the set cover LP:
\mbox{Min.}\quad{\bf 1}^{\top}p\quad\mbox{s.t.}\quad{\bf 1}_{J_{k}}^{\top}p\geq 1\ (k\in[m]),\ p\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\geq 0}.
(5.3)
To see this, from \lambda,
consider p by p_{i}:=\sum_{X\subseteq[n]:i\in X}\lambda(X).
This relation has the following implication for BL-polytopes.
Proposition 5.2.
Suppose that each full-row-rank matrix B_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r_{k}\times n}
is a coordinate projection; that is, there exists J_{k}\subseteq[n] such that |J_{k}|=r_{k} and (B_{k})_{ij}=0
for all (i,j)\in[r_{k}]\times([n]\setminus J_{k}).
Then {\rm BLP}({\cal B}) is equal to
the perfect fractional set-packing polytope:
{\rm BLP}({\cal B})=\left\{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}_{\geq 0}\mid\sum_{k=1}^{m}{\bf 1}_{J_{k}}u_{k}={\bf 1}\right\}.
(5.4)
Although the expression (5.4) appears to be new in the literature,
the essential part (\supseteq) is (a part of)
a result of Finner [11, Theorem 2.1] on a generalization of Hölder’s inequality.
The reverse inclusion (\subseteq) is immediate
from the argument leading to (5.2).
In particular, the membership problem for such BL-polytopes can be solved in polynomial time.
