Abstract
We present algorithms for diffusion model sampling which obtain  $\delta$ -error in  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  steps, given access to  $\widetilde{O}(\delta)$ -accurate score estimates in  $L^{2}$ . This is an exponential improvement over all previous results. Specifically, under minimal data assumptions, the complexity is  $\widetilde{O}(d\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta))$  where  $d$  is the dimension of the data; under a non-uniform  $L$ -Lipschitz condition, the complexity is  $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{dL}\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta))$ ; and if the data distribution has intrinsic dimension  $d_{\star}$ , then the complexity reduces to  $\widetilde{O}(d_{\star}\,\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta))$ . Our approach also yields the first  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  complexity sampler for general log-concave distributions using only gradient evaluations.
1 Introduction
What is the complexity of sampling from a continuous probability distribution, given access to evaluations of the gradient of the log-density?
In particular, can one design algorithms whose iteration complexity scales as  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$ , where  $\delta$  is the target accuracy, or must they necessarily take  $\mathrm{poly}(1/\delta)$  steps?
Complexity bounds which scale as  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$ , indicating that algorithms converge exponentially fast, are known as “high-accuracy” guarantees, as they ensure that one can draw an extremely accurate sample without too many iterations.
When access to the log-density of the target distribution is available, and not just its gradient, then high-accuracy samplers abound, based on accept-reject mechanisms such as rejection sampling or the Metropolis–Hastings filter.
Without density evaluations, the answer is less clear.
In this setting, existing sampling methods are typically based on discretizations of stochastic differential equations, and the need to control the discretization error precludes high-accuracy guarantees.
(Note that this is unlike the setting of optimization, in which gradient descent enjoys a high-accuracy guarantee under strong convexity—there, discretization does not bias the algorithm.)
A notable exception is discretization of piecewise deterministic Markov processes (PDMPs), for which Lu and Wang (2022) show that  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  evaluations of the gradient suffice from a warm-start.
This question has become particularly interesting in light of recent developments on diffusion-based generative modeling.
Such models are based on implementing a certain reverse Markov process, in which each iteration requires evaluation of a score function—the gradient of the log-density along a diffusion process.
Since these models only learn the score function and not the density function itself (unlike earlier approaches such as energy-based models), it is natural to ask what the best achievable complexity is using score evaluations alone.
More precisely, we are interested in bounding the number of steps (and queries to the score function) required to sample from a distribution  $\widehat{p}$  such that
$\displaystyle D(p_{\mathsf{data}},\widehat{p})\leq\delta+C_{\texttt{apx}}\cdot\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}\,,$
where  $D$  is a measure of discrepancy,  $\delta\in(0,1)$  is the target accuracy,  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}$  measures the error in the score function estimates, and  $C_{\texttt{apx}}$  is a target approximation factor.
The initial work of Chen et al. (2023c); Lee et al. (2023) showed that with  $L^{2}$ -accurate score estimates, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) achieve a query complexity of  $1/\delta^{2}$  in total variation distance for sampling from an early stopped distribution, provided that  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  has bounded support, and with an overhead that is polynomial in the dimension and radius and with  $C_{\texttt{apx}}=\widetilde{O}(1)$ .
By a standard argument, this can be converted to guarantees for sampling from  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  itself in a weaker metric.
Importantly, these results impose almost no assumptions on the data distribution, providing strong theoretical justification for diffusion models.
Since then, there has been an explosion of works aimed at extending and refining these guarantees (Chen et al., 2023a; Li et al., 2023; Benton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025; Conforti et al., 2025; Jain and Zhang, 2025; Li and Yan, 2025; Chen et al., 2023b; Li et al., 2024b; Gao and Zhu, 2024; Huang et al., 2025a, e.g.,), which we cannot fully survey here.
However, it is known that the complexity of  $1/\delta$  is unimprovable for DDPM (Jiao et al., 2025), which motivates changing the algorithm.
Under minimal assumptions, the algorithm of Li and Cai (2024) achieves a query complexity of  $1/\delta^{1/2}$ .
Other works achieved similar speed-ups assuming that the Jacobian of the score error is bounded (Li et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2025b).
A recent line of works (Huang et al., 2025a; Huang et al., 2025b; Li et al., 2025b, e.g.,) studied higher-order discretization methods, with the resulting query complexity scaling roughly as  $C_{\rm P}\,d^{1+1/p}/\delta^{1/p}$ ,

Therefore, we state our main question of interest:
Is there a diffusion model sampler which achieves a high-accuracy guarantee using only score evaluations, under minimal assumptions on both the data distribution and the score error?
1.1 Our contribution
We answer this question affirmatively via a new meta-algorithm, which we call first-order rejection sampling (FORS); see Algorithm 1.
This algorithm aims at simulating rejection sampling using only first-order (gradient) queries.
We give consequences of our method for diffusion sampling and for log-concave sampling.
Diffusion sampling
We show the following results, where the error is measured in the bounded Lipschitz metric and  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}$  denotes the  $L^{2}$ -error of the score estimates (Definition 2.1).
•
Under minimal data assumptions—namely,  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  has a finite second moment  $\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}$ —we obtain  $\delta$  error in  $O(d\log^{2}(M/\delta)+\log^{3}(M/\delta))$  queries with  $C_{\texttt{apx}}=O(1)$ , where  $M\coloneqq d+\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}$ .
This result strictly improves upon all prior results in the literature in this setting.
Beyond this setting, many works in the literature aim to sample with a number of steps which is sublinear in the dimension (Chen et al., 2023b; Jiao and Li, 2024; Jiao et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2025).
In an orthogonal direction, other works aim to show that the number of steps depend on the intrinsic dimension of the data (Azangulov et al., 2024; Huang et al., 2024b; Li and Yan, 2024; Potaptchik et al., 2024; Li et al., 2025a; Liang et al., 2025; Tang and Yan, 2025).
We also incorporate these advances into our framework.
•
Assuming Lipschitz score functions—where we use the significantly weaker notion of a “non-uniform” Lipschitz constant  $L$  (Assumption 4.3)—we obtain  $\delta$  error in  $O(\sqrt{dL}\log^{3/2}(M/\delta)+L\log^{2}(M/\delta))$  steps, where  $L$  is the Lipschitz constant, and with  $C_{\texttt{apx}}=O(\sqrt{d/L})$ .
•
For low-dimensional data distributions, we obtain  $\delta$  error in  $O(\mathsf{d}_{\star}\log^{2}(M/\delta)+\log^{3}(M/\delta))$  steps, where  $\mathsf{d}_{\star}$  is the intrinsic dimension (Definition 4.5), and with  $C_{\texttt{apx}}=O(d/\mathsf{d}_{\star})$  under a slightly stronger notion of error in the score estimates.
Log-concave sampling
For sampling from log-concave (and isoperimetric) densities with gradient evaluations of the log-density, we recover state-of-the-art results from Fan et al. (2023), except that we do not require density evaluations; see Section 5.
1.2 Related work
As we do not have space to survey the vast literature on diffusion model guarantees, we focus on the ones most relevant to our work.
High-accuracy diffusion sampling
Prior to this work, Huang et al. (2024a); Wainwright (2025) studied diffusion sampling with additional zeroth-order queries. Specifically, given access to estimates of the log-densities with bounded error, query complexity bounds of  $\widetilde{O}(d^{2}\log(1/\delta))$  (Huang et al., 2024a) and  $\widetilde{O}(\sqrt{d}\log^{3}(1/\delta))$  (Wainwright, 2025) under additional Lipschitz conditions on the score functions. Both works resort to standard high-accuracy sampling methods (e.g., Metropolis-adjusted Langevin). However, estimating the densities can be extremely challenging in practice.
Concurrent work
In concurrent work, Gatmiry et al. (2026) also obtains a high-accuracy guarantee for diffusion sampling.
Assuming that  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  is a Gaussian convolution, i.e.,  $p_{\mathsf{data}}=p_{\star}*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I\right)$  where  $p_{\star}$  is supported on a ball of radius  $R$ , and that the score errors are bounded in the sub-exponential norm, they obtain a query complexity of  $\widetilde{O}((R/\sigma)^{2}\log^{2}(1/\delta))$ .
While their assumptions are stronger than ours, the final complexity bounds are incomparable. We defer a lengthier comparison to
Section A.1.
Log-concave sampling
Our application to log-concave sampling is based on the proximal sampler (Lee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022), and our results can be compared to Altschuler and Chewi (2024); Fan et al. (2023).
See the book draft Chewi (2026) for an overview of the subject.
1.3 Notation
We make use of the following divergences between probability distributions: the total variation (TV) distance  $D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int|\mu-\nu|$ ; the Hellinger distance  $D^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\int(\sqrt{\mu}-\sqrt{\nu})^{2}$ ; the KL divergence  $D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\mu\,\|\,\nu\right)\coloneqq\En_{\mu}\log(\mu/\nu)$ ; the chi-squared divergence  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu\;\|\;{}\nu\right)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(\mu/\nu)^{2}-1$ ; and the Wasserstein distance  $W_{2}^{2}(\mu,\nu)\coloneqq\inf_{\gamma~\text{coupling of }\mu,\nu}\En_{(X,Y)\sim\gamma}\|X-Y\|^{2}$ .
In addition, we consider the bounded Lipschitz metric which metrizes weak convergence:
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{BL}}\left(\mu,\nu\right)\coloneqq\sup\{\mathbb{E}_{\mu}f-\mathbb{E}_{\nu}f:|f|\leq 1,\,\|f\|_{\rm Lip}\leq 1\}.$
We define
$\displaystyle\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(x)\coloneqq\max\{-B,\min\{B,x\}\}\,,\qquad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}\,.$
We use  $\lesssim$  and  $O(\cdot)$  to hide absolute constant, i.e.,  $f\lesssim g$  (and  $f=O(g)$ ) if there is an absolute constant such that  $f\leq Cg$ .
The notation  $\widetilde{O}(\cdot)$  hides logarithmic factors.
2 Background on diffusion models
Recall that Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) are based on the following forward process that transforms a sample  $X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}$  to noise:
$\displaystyle X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}\,,\quad X_{t+1}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\alpha_{t}X_{t},\alpha_{t}^{2}\eta_{t}I\right)\,,~~t\in[T]\,.$
(1)
Note that this is a Markov chain, and it is easy to see that  $X_{t}\mid X_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t}X_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}I\right)$ , where
$\displaystyle\bar{\alpha}_{t}=\prod_{s=0}^{t-1}\alpha_{s}\,,\qquad\sigma_{t}^{2}=\alpha_{t-1}^{2}\,(\sigma_{t-1}^{2}+\eta_{t-1})\,.$
Conversely, given the parameter sequence  $(\bar{\alpha}_{t},\sigma_{t})_{t\in[T]}$ , the corresponding  $(\alpha_{t},\eta_{t})_{t\in[T]}$  is given by
$\displaystyle\alpha_{t}=\frac{\bar{\alpha}_{t+1}}{\bar{\alpha}_{t}}\,,\qquad\eta_{t}=\frac{\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{\alpha_{t}^{2}}-\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{\bar{\alpha}_{t+1}^{2}}\,\bar{\alpha}_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t}^{2}\,.$
We say that the DDPM is variance-preserving if  $\bar{\alpha}_{t}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}=1$ , and hence  $\alpha_{t}^{2}=\frac{1-\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t}^{2}}$ ,  $\sigma_{t+1}^{2}=\frac{1-\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,(\sigma_{t}^{2}+\eta_{t})$ .
We let  $p_{t}$  be the probability density function of  $X_{t}$ , and let  $\rho_{t}(\cdot\mid x^{\prime})$  be the probability density function of the backward transition kernel  $\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=\cdot\mid X_{t+1}=x^{\prime})$ . Then, by Bayes rule, it holds that
$\displaystyle\rho_{t}(x\mid x^{\prime})\propto_{x}p_{t}(x)\exp\Big(-\frac{\|x-\alpha_{t}^{-1}x^{\prime}\|^{2}}{2\eta_{t}}\Big)\,.$
(2)
In this work, we focus on the task of sampling from  $p_{1}$  with extremely small values of  $1-\alpha_{0}$  and  $\sigma_{1}$ .
This corresponds to early stopping; see Section 4.1 for further discussion.
Score function and estimates
As above, we assume that we have access to approximate score functions  $(\mathsf{s}_{t})_{t\in[T]}$  such that  $\mathsf{s}_{t}\approx\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}\vcentcolon=\nabla\log p_{t}$  with controlled mean-squared error. Recall that by Tweedie’s identity,
$\displaystyle\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}(x)=\nabla\log p_{t}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\En[\bar{\alpha}_{t}X_{0}-X_{t}\mid X_{t}]\,,$
(3)
where the conditional expectation is taken over  $X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}$ ,  $X_{t}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\bar{\alpha}_{t}X_{0},\sigma_{t}^{2}\right)$ .
Definition 2.1 (Score estimation error).
Given the score estimates  $(\mathsf{s}_{t})_{t\in[T]}$ , we define
$\displaystyle\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\vcentcolon=\En_{X_{t}\sim p_{t}}\|\mathsf{s}_{t}(X_{t})-\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\,,\qquad t\in[T]\,.$
Roadmap
To approximately generate a sample from  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$ , we learn approximate score functions  $\mathsf{s}_{t}\approx\nabla\log p_{t}$ .
DDPM uses the approximation  $\log p_{t}(x)\approx\log p_{t}(x^{\prime})+\langle\mathsf{s}_{t}(x^{\prime}),x-x^{\prime}\rangle$ , yielding
$\displaystyle\rho_{t}(\cdot\mid x^{\prime})\approx\mathsf{N}\left(\alpha_{t}^{-1}x^{\prime}+\eta_{t}\mathsf{s}_{t}(x^{\prime}),\eta_{t}I\right).$
(4)
In our work, we develop algorithms for sampling from (2) directly. In Section 3, we motivate our approach by assuming that  $\mathsf{s}_{t}=\nabla\log p_{t}$  is exact, which has implications for log-concave sampling described in Section 5. In Section 4, we instantiate our methods on diffusion sampling and show that the convergence guarantees are robust with respect to the error in the score function estimates.
3 Key subroutine: Gaussian tilts
In this section, we focus on the problem of sampling from a Gaussian tilt of the form
$\displaystyle\nu(x)\propto\exp\Big(-f(x)-\frac{\|x-x_{0}\|^{2}}{2\eta}\Big)\,,$
(5)
where we assume access to first-order queries for  $f$ . For our eventual application to diffusion models, we will choose  $f=\log p_{t}$  in view of Eq. 2 and approximate  $\nabla f\approx\mathsf{s}_{t}$ .
3.1 First-order rejection sampling (FORS)
We motivate our approach by considering the simple problem of sampling from a density  $p\propto e^{-f}$ , where  $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ ,  $f(0)=0$ , and  $-1\leq f^{\prime}\leq 1$ .
Our goal is to develop a high-accuracy sampler—a sampler whose sampled distribution has  $\delta$  error in total variation distance to  $p$ —in  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  steps, using only queries to  $f^{\prime}$ .
Consider performing rejection sampling with the base measure  $\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ . To do so, we must generate randomness  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{-f(x)})$  for any given  $x\in[0,1]$ . However, we only have access to  $f^{\prime}$ . The identity  $f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(y)\,dy$ , written as
$\displaystyle f(x)=\En_{y\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,x])}[xf^{\prime}(y)]\,,$
(6)
suggests an unbiased estimate of  $f(x)$ . Is it possible to sample from  $\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{-f(x)})$  if we have access to an unbiased estimate of  $f(x)$ ? A general version of this question is known as the “Bernoulli factory” problem (Keane and O’Brien, 1994; Nacu and Peres, 2005). For our purposes, this can be stated as the following abstract task:
Task: Given i.i.d. random variables  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\dotsc$  in  $[-1,1]$ , generate a sample  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{\En W_{1}})$ .
To solve this, we write the Taylor series as
$\displaystyle e^{\En W_{1}}=e^{-1}\cdot e^{\En[1+W_{1}]}=\sum_{j\geq 0}\frac{e^{-1}}{j!}\,\bigl(\En[1+W_{1}]\bigr)^{j}\,.$
Suppose that  $J\sim\mathsf{Poisson}(2)$  is independent of the i.i.d. sequence  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3},\dotsc$ . Then we notice that
$\displaystyle e^{\En W_{1}}=e\En\Big[\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)\Big]\,,$
and simply set  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}\big(\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)\big)$ .
Indeed,  $\mathbb{P}(b=1)=\mathbb{E}\prod_{j=1}^{J}\bigl(\frac{1+W_{j}}{2}\bigr)=e^{-1+\En W_{1}}$ .
In summary, we can generate a sample  $b\sim\mathsf{Ber}(ce^{-f(x)})$  without having to compute or accurately approximate  $f(x)$  (this requires the integration step  $f(x)=\int_{0}^{x}f^{\prime}(y)\,dy$  and can be expensive). Instead, it is sufficient to have access to (a random number of) unbiased estimates of  $f(x)$ . The latter can be achieved with derivative information, in view of (6).
We now generalize this setup via the following meta-algorithm, called first-order rejection sampling (FORS). Given a proposal distribution  $q$  and a tilt function  $w$ , the goal of Algorithm 1 is to produce a sample from  $\widehat{p}(x)\propto q(x)\,e^{w(x)}$  without having access to the value  $w(x)$ . Instead, for each  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we can generate i.i.d. samples  $W_{1},W_{2},W_{3}\dotsc$  such that  $\En[W_{1}\mid x]=w(x)$ .
Let  $\mathcal{W}_{x}$  denote the conditional distribution of  $W_{1}$  given  $x$ .
Algorithm 1  First-order rejection sampling (FORS)
Input: Parameter  $B>0$ , proposal distribution  $q$  over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , estimator distributions  $(\mathcal{W}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}$  supported on  $[-B,B]$
for  $i=1,2,3,\dotsc$  do
Sample  $x\sim q$ .
Sample  $J\sim\mathsf{Poisson}(2B)$ .
Sample i.i.d.  $W_{1},\dotsc,W_{J}\sim\mathcal{W}_{x}$ .
Output  $x$  with probability  $\prod_{j=1}^{J}\frac{B+W_{j}}{2B}$ .
end for
Theorem 3.1 (FORS guarantee).
Algorithm 1 outputs a random point with density  $\widehat{p}(x)\propto q(x)\,e^{\En[W_{1}\mid x]}$ .
The number of sampled  $W_{j}$ ’s is bounded, with probability at least  $1-\delta$ , by  $3Be^{2B}\log(2/\delta)$ .
Moreover, if Algorithm 1 is called  $T$  times, then with probability at least  $1-\delta$ , the total number of sampled  $W_{j}$ ’s is  $O(Be^{2B}\,(T+\log(1/\delta)))$ .
3.2 Sampling from Gaussian tilts with an exact oracle
To perform rejection sampling, we have to appropriately choose the proposal distribution  $q$ . By Taylor’s expansion, for a fixed  $x_{+}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , we have
$\displaystyle f(x)\approx f(x_{+})+\langle x-x_{+},\nabla f(x_{+})\rangle\,,$
and hence it is natural to choose  $q=\mathsf{N}\left(x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+}),\eta I\right)$  so that
$\displaystyle q(x)\propto\exp\Bigl(-\langle x-x_{+},\nabla f(x_{+})\rangle-\frac{1}{2\eta}\left\lVert x-x_{0}\right\rVert^{2}\Bigr)\,.$
Then, we can express
$\displaystyle\log\nu(x)-\log q(x)-\mathrm{const}=$
$\displaystyle~\langle x-x_{+},\nabla f(x_{+})\rangle-f(x)+f(x_{+})$
$\displaystyle=$
$\displaystyle~\int_{0}^{1}\langle x-x_{+},\nabla f(x_{+})-\nabla f(rx+(1-r)x_{+})\rangle\,dr\,.$
In particular, we can set
$\displaystyle W_{r,x}\vcentcolon=\langle x-x_{+},\nabla f(x_{+})-\nabla f(rx+(1-r)x_{+})\rangle\,,$
so that
$\displaystyle\nu(x)\propto q(x)\exp\left(\En_{r}W_{r,x}\right)\,,$
where the expectation  $\En_{r}[\cdot]$  is taken over  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ .
Further, assuming that  $\nabla f$  is  $\beta$ -Lipschitz, we can bound  $\lvert W_{r,x}\rvert\leq\beta\,\|x-x_{+}\|^{2}$ . Therefore, as long as  $x_{+}\approx x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+})$  and  $\beta d\eta\ll 1$ , we can guarantee that  $\sup_{r}{\lvert W_{r,x}\rvert}\leq 1$  with high probability over  $x\sim q$ . This implies the following distribution is a good approximation of  $\nu$ :
$\displaystyle\widehat{\nu}(x)\propto q(x)\exp(\En_{r}\widehat{W}_{r,x})\,,$
where  $\widehat{W}_{r,x}=\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{r,x})$  and  $B=\Theta(1)$  is a tuneable parameter.
General path integral
We can generalize the above pipeline with any path integral and demonstrate (a) that in the gradient Lipschitz case, we in fact only need to ensure  $\beta\sqrt{d}\eta\ll 1$ , and (b) the Lipschitz continuity of  $\nabla f$  can be weakened to Hölder continuity for any exponent  $s\in[0,1]$ .
Fix a distribution  $P$  (to be determined later) over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  and a path function  $\gamma_{z,r}(x)\vcentcolon=\gamma(x;z,r)$  such that  $\gamma_{z,1}(x)=x$  and  $\gamma_{z,0}(x)=\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z)$  is independent of  $x$ . Then we can express any smooth function  $h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$  as
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} h(x)-\En_{z\sim P}[h(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}(z))]=&~\int_{0}^{1}\En_{z\sim P}\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),\nabla h(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle\,dr\\
=&~\En_{z\sim P,\,r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])}\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),\nabla h(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle\,,\end{aligned}$
(7)
where  $\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x)=\frac{d}{dr}\gamma_{z,r}(x)$  is the path derivative with respect to  $r$ .
This inspires us to consider
$\displaystyle W_{r,z,x}\vcentcolon=\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x),\nabla f(x_{+})-\nabla f(\gamma_{z,r}(x))\rangle\,,$
(8)
so that
$\displaystyle\nu(x)\propto q(x)\exp\left(\En_{r,z}W_{r,z,x}\right)\,,$
where the expectation  $\En_{r,z}[\cdot]$  is taken over  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$  and  $z\sim P$ .
Again, we will truncate  $W_{r,z,x}$  to ensure that the estimator lies in  $[-B,B]$ .
For better dimension dependence, we consider the following path function ( $\widehat{x}=x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+})$ ):
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &~\gamma_{z,r}(x)=a_{r}x+(1-a_{r})\widehat{x}+b_{r}z\,,\\
&~a_{r}=\sin(\pi r/2)\,,~~b_{r}=\cos(\pi r/2)\,,\end{aligned}$
(9)
so that  $\dot{\gamma}_{z,r}(x)=a_{r}^{\prime}(x-x_{0})+b_{r}^{\prime}z$ .
Assumption 3.2.
There exists  $s\in[0,1]$  and  $\beta_{s}\geq 0$  such that  $\left\lVert\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\right\rVert\leq\beta_{s}\left\lVert x-y\right\rVert^{s}$  for all  $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ .
Theorem 3.3.
Suppose that Assumption 3.2 holds,  $B=\Theta(1)$ , and  $\|x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+})-x_{+}\|\leq(d\eta)^{1/2}$ .
Consider instantiating Algorithm 1 with the choices  $q=\mathsf{N}\left(x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+}),\eta I\right)$ , and  $\mathcal{W}_{x}$  the law of  $\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{r,z,x})$ , where  $W_{r,z,x}$  is defined in (8)-(9) and  $z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right)$ ,  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ .
Then, the law  $\widehat{\nu}$  of Algorithm 1 satisfies  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\nu\;\|\;{}\widehat{\nu}\right)\leq\delta^{2}$ , provided that
$\displaystyle\eta^{-1}\gg\Big(\beta_{s}^{2}d^{s}\log(1/\delta)+\frac{s\beta_{s}^{2}}{d^{1-s}}\log^{2}(1/\delta)\Big)^{1/(1+s)}.$
Note that  $x_{0}-\eta\nabla f(x_{+})-x_{+}=0$  when  $x_{+}=\text{prox}_{\eta f}(x_{0})$ , that is, the requirement of Theorem 3.3 is that we can approximately take a proximal step on  $f$  from  $x_{0}$ .
Theorem 3.3 interpolates between the Lipschitz case  $s=0$ , which requires  $\eta^{-1}\gg\beta_{1}^{2}\log(1/\delta)$ , and the smooth case  $s=1$ , which requires  $\eta^{-1}\gg\beta_{2}\,d^{1/2}\log(1/\delta)$ .
This recovers the result of Fan et al. (2023), except that we only use first-order queries; we spell out the implications for log-concave sampling in Section 5.
4 Diffusion sampling
A key insight is that sampling from the backward transition kernel (2), given access to score estimate  $\mathsf{s}_{t}$ , is a special case of the setup of Section 3, except that we only have approximately correct first-order evaluations.
This leads to the template Algorithm 2 for high-accuracy diffusion sampling.
In the following subsections, we show how to choose  $q_{t}$  and  $\mathcal{W}_{x}^{t}$  for  $t\in[T]$ .
Algorithm 2  Backward diffusion sampling
Input: Score estimates  $\{\mathsf{s}_{t}\}_{t\in[T]}$ , initial distribution  $\widehat{p}_{T}$ , parameters  $(\alpha_{t},\eta_{t})_{t\in[T]}$
Sample  $X_{T}\sim\widehat{p}_{T}$ .
for  $t=T-1,\dotsc,1$  do
Sample  $X_{t}\leftarrow\textsf{FORS}(B,q_{t},(\mathcal{W}^{t}_{x})_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}})$ .
end for
Output:  $X_{1}$
4.1 A simple method
We first propose a simple method that uses as a proposal the Gaussian portion of (2).
We state all our guarantees for sampling from the early stopped distribution  $p_{1}$ , the law of  $X_{1}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\alpha_{0}X_{0},\sigma_{1}^{2}\right)$  with  $X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}$ ; see the discussion after the theorem.
Theorem 4.1.
Suppose that  $\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]$ ,  $B=\Theta(1)$ , and
$\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\eta_{t}}\gg d\log(1/\delta)+\log^{2}(1/\delta)\,.$
(10)
Consider instantiating the subroutine
FORS in Algorithm 2 with the following choices:  $q_{t}=\mathsf{N}(\bar{x}_{t},\eta_{t}I)$ , where  $\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\alpha_{t}^{-1}X_{t+1}$ , and  $\mathcal{W}_{x}^{t}$  is the law of
$\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{r,x}\coloneqq\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle x-\bar{x}_{t},\mathsf{s}_{t}(rx+(1-r)\bar{x}_{t})\rangle)\,,$
for  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ .
Let  $\widehat{p}_{1}$  be the law of  $X_{1}$  generated by Algorithm 2. Then,
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{1}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{1}\right)\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{T}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{T}\right)+T\delta+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\,.$
Next, we describe the implications of Theorem 4.1 for diffusion model sampling.
We should always choose  $B=\Theta(1)$  and we denote  $G\coloneqq C(d+\log(1/\delta))\log(d/\delta)$  for a sufficiently large constant  $C$  from Eq. 10.
Then, in the variance-preserving setting:
•
Recall that  $\alpha_{t}^{2}=\frac{1-\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t}^{2}}$  and  $\sigma_{t+1}^{2}=\frac{1-\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t}^{2}}\,(\sigma_{t}^{2}+\eta_{t})$ .
•
The condition  $\eta_{t}\leq\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{G}$  reduces to  $\frac{\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t+1}^{2}}\leq\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{1-\sigma_{t}^{2}}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{G}\right)$ .
•
This implies that as long as  $T\geq O(G\log(1/(\bar{\delta}\sigma_{0}^{2})))$ , we can guarantee that  $1-\sigma_{T}^{2}\leq\bar{\delta}$ .
•
From known results on the convergence of the forward process,  $1-\sigma_{T}^{2}\leq\bar{\delta}$  and  $\widehat{p}_{T}=\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma_{T}^{2}I\right)$  imply that  $D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{T}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{T}\right)\lesssim\bar{\delta}\En\|X_{0}\|^{2}$ .
By the above calculation, we can show the following corollary. We denote  $\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}}\|X_{0}\|^{2}$  to be the second moment of  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$ .
Corollary 4.2.
In the variance-preserving setting, for any  $\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]$ ,  $\sigma_{0}>0$ , there exists a schedule  $(\sigma_{t})_{t\in[T]}$  such that
$\displaystyle T\leq O\big((d+\log(1/\delta))\log^{2}(\kappa/\delta)\big)\,,$
where  $\kappa\coloneqq(d+M_{2}^{2})/\sigma_{0}^{2}$ ,
and Algorithm 2 can be instantiated with  $\widehat{p}_{T}=\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma_{T}^{2}I\right)$ , so that
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{1}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{1}\right)\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~\delta^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\,.$
Further, the number of queries made by Algorithm 2 is  $O(T)$  with probability at least  $1-\delta$ .
This governs the complexity of sampling from the early stopped distribution  $p_{1}$ .
To convert this into a guarantee for sampling from  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  itself, we can note that  $W_{2}^{2}(p_{\mathsf{data}},p_{1})\leq(1-\alpha_{0})^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\|X_{0}\|^{2}+\sigma_{0}^{2}d$ .
This can be made at most  $\delta^{2}$  by choosing  $\sigma_{0}^{2}\asymp\delta^{2}/(d+\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2})$ .
In terms of the bounded Lipschitz metric, this implies that  $D_{\mathsf{BL}}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}},\widehat{p}_{1}\right)^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2}+\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}$  with a total complexity of
$\displaystyle\boxed{\max\{d,\log(1/\delta)\}\log^{2}\Big(\frac{d+\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\Big)}.$
Note that this guarantee imposes no assumptions on  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  beyond a second moment bound.
This improves upon prior works Benton et al. (2024); Conforti et al. (2025) which achieved  $\widetilde{O}(d/\delta^{2})$  under these assumptions, and Jain and Zhang (2025); Li and Yan (2025) which recently improved the complexity to  $\widetilde{O}(d/\delta)$ .
4.2 A DDPM-like method
Next, we present a method which we call a “DDPM-like” method because the proposal  $q$  takes the form  $\mathsf{N}\left(\alpha_{t}^{-1}X_{t+1}+\eta_{t}\mathsf{s}_{t}(\alpha_{t}^{-1}X_{t+1}),\eta_{t}I\right)$ , which is similar but slightly different from a step of DDPM (4).
We show that our method, similar to the one from Section 3, achieves  $\sqrt{d}$ -complexity under the non-uniform Lipschitz condition, following Jiao and Li (2024); Jiao et al. (2025).
Assumption 4.3 (Non-uniform Lipschitz condition).
For any  $\delta>0$ , there is a parameter  $L_{\delta}\geq 1$  such that for any  $t\in[T]$ ,
$\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X_{t}\sim p_{t}}\Big(\|\nabla\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}(X_{t})\|_{\mathrm{op}}>\frac{L_{\delta}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Big)\leq\frac{\delta^{2}}{d^{8}}\,.$
(11)
We note that by Proposition E.8, Assumption 4.3 holds unconditionally with  $L_{\delta}=O(d+\log(1/\delta))$ . In other words, the score function  $\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}$  is smooth “with high probability”. Further, when  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  is Gaussian, Assumption 4.3 holds with  $L_{\delta}\equiv 1$  for any  $\delta\geq 0$ .
As shown in Jiao et al. (2025), when  $p_{\mathsf{data}}=\sum_{h=1}^{H}p_{h}\mathsf{N}\left(\mu_{h},\sigma_{h}^{2}\right)$  is a mixture of  $H$  Gaussian distributions, it holds that  $L_{\delta}\leq O(\log(dH/\delta))$  (see also Proposition E.7).
Theorem 4.4.
Suppose that Assumption 4.3 holds,  $\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]$ ,  $B=\Theta(1)$ , and
$\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\eta_{t}}\gg\sqrt{dL_{\delta}\log(d/\delta)}+L_{\delta}\log(d/\delta)\,.$
(12)
Consider instantiating the subroutine FORS in Algorithm 2 with the following choices:  $q_{t}=\mathsf{N}\left(\bar{x}_{t}+\eta_{t}\mathsf{s}_{t}(\bar{x}_{t}),\eta_{t}I\right)$ , where  $\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\alpha_{t}^{-1}X_{t+1}$ , and  $\mathcal{W}_{x}^{t}$  is the law of
$\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{r,z,x}\coloneqq\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x),\mathsf{s}_{t}(\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x))-\mathsf{s}_{t}(\bar{x}_{t})\rangle)\,,$
where  $z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta_{t}I\right)$ ,  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ ,
and  $\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}$  denotes the path function
$\displaystyle\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x)\coloneqq a_{r}x+(1-a_{r})\bar{x}_{t}+b_{r}z\,,$
(13)
$\displaystyle a_{r}\coloneqq\sin(\pi r/2)\,,~~b_{r}\coloneqq\cos(\pi r/2)\,.$
(14)
Let  $\widehat{p}_{1}$  be the law of  $X_{1}$  generated by Algorithm 2. Then
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{TV}}\left(p_{1},\widehat{p}_{1}\right)^{2}\leq 2D^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}\left(p_{1},\widehat{p}_{1}\right)\lesssim D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{T}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{T}\right)+T\delta+\sqrt{d/L_{\delta}}\cdot\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\,.$
Following the reasoning of the preceding subsection, Theorem 4.4 implies a complexity of
$\displaystyle\boxed{\max\{\sqrt{dL_{\delta}\log(d/\delta)},L_{\delta}\log(d/\delta)\}\log\frac{d+\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}}$
to reach  $D_{\mathsf{BL}}\left(\widehat{p}_{1},p_{\mathsf{data}}\right)^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2}+\sqrt{d/L_{\delta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\varepsilon_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}$ .
Note that the dependence on the score error is worse; we do not know if this is fundamental.
4.3 Adaptation to low-dimensional structure
While Theorem 4.4 improves the dependence on the ambient dimension to  $\sqrt{d}$ , ideally, we would like to choose  $\eta$  according to the intrinsic dimension of the data distribution  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  (Li and Yan, 2024) (cf. Definition 4.5).
The key observation is that for  $p=p_{\mathsf{data}}*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I\right)$ , we can write, for  $X_{0}\sim p_{\mathsf{data}}$ ,  $X\sim\mathsf{N}\left(X_{0},\sigma^{2}I\right)$ ,
$\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\log p(x)=-\frac{I}{\sigma^{2}}+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(X_{0}\mid X=x)}{\sigma^{4}}\,,$
(15)
and hence, when  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  has low-dimensional structure, we may expect the shifted Hessian  $\nabla^{2}\log p+\sigma^{-2}I$  to be well-behaved. This motivates adapting Theorem 4.4 by replacing the score function with the shifted score function  $\widetilde{\mathsf{s}}(x)=\mathsf{s}(x)+\sigma^{-2}x$ .
Definition 4.5 (Intrinsic dimension).
Suppose that the data distribution  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  has support  $\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}=\mathrm{supp}(p_{\mathsf{data}})$ . Let  $N_{\mathsf{data}}(r)=N(\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{};r,\|\cdot\|_{2})$  be the covering number of  $\mathcal{X}$  under Euclidean norm, and we define the intrinsic dimension of  $p_{\mathsf{data}}$  as  $\mathsf{d}_{\star}=1+\log N_{\mathsf{data}}(\sigma_{0}/\sqrt{d})$ .
Theorem 4.6.
Suppose that  $\delta\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]$ ,  $B=\Theta(1)$ , and
$\displaystyle\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{\eta_{t}}\gg\mathsf{d}_{\star}\log(d/\delta)+\log^{2}(d/\delta)\,.$
(16)
Consider instantiating the subroutine FORS in Algorithm 2 with the following choices:  $q_{t}=\mathsf{N}\left(\bar{x}_{t}+\bar{\eta}_{t}\mathsf{s}_{t}(\bar{x}_{t}),\bar{\eta}_{t}I\right)$ , where  $\bar{x}_{t}\coloneqq\alpha_{t}^{-1}X_{t+1}$ ,  $\lambda_{t}=\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}$ ,  $1/\bar{\eta}_{t}=1/\eta_{t}+\lambda_{t}$ , and  $\mathcal{W}_{x}^{t}$  is the law of
$\displaystyle\widehat{W}_{r,z,x}\coloneqq\mathsf{Clip}_{B}\bigl(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x),$
$\displaystyle~\mathsf{s}_{t}(\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x))-\mathsf{s}_{t}(\bar{x}_{t})$
$\displaystyle\quad+\lambda_{t}\,(\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}(x)-\bar{x}_{t})\rangle\bigr)\,,$
where  $z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta_{t}I\right)$ ,  $r\sim\mathsf{Unif}([0,1])$ ,
and  $\gamma_{z,r,\bar{x}_{t}}$  is the path function given in (13)–(14).
Let  $\widehat{p}_{1}$  be the law of  $X_{1}$  generated by Algorithm 2. Then
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{1}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{1}\right)\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(p_{T}\,\|\,\widehat{p}_{T}\right)+T\delta+\frac{d}{\mathsf{d}_{\star}}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\,,$

$\displaystyle\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}\vcentcolon=\max_{\eta\in[0,2\eta_{t}]}\En_{X\sim p_{t}*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right)}\|\mathsf{s}_{t}(X_{t})-\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}(X_{t})\|^{2}\,.$
This implies a complexity of
$\displaystyle\boxed{\max\{\mathsf{d}_{\star},\log(d/\delta)\}\log^{2}\Big(\frac{d+\mathsf{M}_{2}^{2}}{\delta^{2}}\Big)}$
to reach  $D_{\mathsf{BL}}\left(p_{\mathsf{data}},\widehat{p}_{1}\right)^{2}\lesssim\delta^{2}+(d/\mathsf{d}_{\star})\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\eta_{t}\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}_{t,\mathsf{score}}^{2}$ .

5 Application to log-concave sampling
In this section, we briefly describe the implications of our results (Theorem 3.3) for the problem of sampling from a log-concave density  $\mu\propto e^{-f}$ .
The key connection lies in the proximal sampler (Lee et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022), which is an instance of the Gibbs sampler in which the target distribution is the augmented density
$\displaystyle\widetilde{\mu}(x,y)\propto\exp\Big(-f(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\,\|y-x\|^{2}\Big)\,.$
See Algorithm 3.
Algorithm 3  Proximal sampler
Input: Gradient  $\nabla f$ , step size  $\eta>0$
for  $n=1,2,3,\dotsc,N$  do
Sample  $Y_{n}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(X_{n},\eta I\right)$ .
Sample  $X_{n+1}\sim\mathsf{RGO}_{f,\eta,Y_{n}}$ .
end for
Output  $X_{N}$ .
Algorithm 3 hinges on being able to implement the restricted Gaussian oracle (RGO):
$\displaystyle\mathsf{RGO}_{f,\eta,y}(x)\propto_{x}\exp\Big(-f(x)-\frac{1}{2\eta}\,\|y-x\|^{2}\Big)\,.$
Convergence of Algorithm 3 was shown in Lee et al. (2021) under log-concavity of  $\pi$ , and in Chen et al. (2022) under weaker isoperimetric assumptions.
On the other hand, note that the RGO is exactly a Gaussian tilt distribution, which we considered in Section 3.
Hence, implementing the RGO step using FORS leads to novel high-accuracy sampling results for log-concave (and isoperimetric) distributions, without assuming access to zeroth-order queries for  $f$ .
Almost all previous approaches (e.g., rejection sampling, Metropolis–Hastings) required zeroth-order queries, whereas algorithms using only first-order queries typically arise as discretizations of diffusion processes and the resulting discretization error degraded their accuracy guarantees.
A notable exception is the result of Lu and Wang (2022) on the zigzag sampler, an example of a PDMP, which achieved a complexity of  $\widetilde{O}(\kappa^{2}d^{3/2}\log^{5/2}(1/\delta))$  evaluations of partial derivatives of  $f$ , under strong-log-concavity and given a warm start, where  $\kappa$  is the condition number.
We only summarize some representative results here, and defer a full presentation to Appendix F.
Let  $\mu_{0}$ ,  $\widehat{\mu}$  denote the initialization and output of Algorithm 3, and in all cases we track the number of queries to a first-order and proximal oracle for  $f$  in expectation.
Suppose that  $f$  is smooth ( $s=1$  in Assumption 3.2).
•
Under a log-Sobolev inequality, we obtain  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  in  $\widetilde{O}(\kappa\,(d^{1/2}\log^{3/2}(\mathcal{R}/\varepsilon^{2})+\log^{2}(\mathcal{R}/\varepsilon^{2})))$  queries, where  $\mathcal{R}\coloneqq\log(1+D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\mu\right))$  and  $\kappa\coloneqq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}\,\beta_{1}$ .
•
Under a Poincaré inequality, we obtain  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  using  $\widetilde{O}(\kappa\,(d^{1/2}\log^{1/2}(1/\varepsilon)+\log(1/\varepsilon))\log(\chi^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}))$  queries, where  $\chi^{2}\coloneqq D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)$  and  $\kappa\coloneqq C_{\mathsf{PI}}\,\beta_{1}$ .
•
Under log-concavity, we obtain  $D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\,\|\,\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  in  $\widetilde{O}(\beta d^{1/2}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu)/\varepsilon^{2})$  queries.
Suppose now that  $f$  is Lipschitz ( $s=0$  in Assumption 3.2).
•
Under a Poincaré inequality, we obtain  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  in  $\widetilde{O}(C_{\mathsf{PI}}\,\beta_{0}^{2}\log(1/\varepsilon)\log(\chi^{2}/\varepsilon^{2}))$  queries.
•
Under log-concavity, we obtain  $D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\,\|\,\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  in  $\widetilde{O}(\beta_{0}^{2}\,W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\pi)/\varepsilon^{2})$  queries.
6 Conclusion
In this work, we have presented high-accuracy algorithms for diffusion sampling which operate under minimal data assumptions (Theorem 4.1), with improved dimension dependence under a Lipschitz score assumption (Theorem 4.4) and low-dimensional data (Theorem 4.6).
With regards to the dependence on the target accuracy, our results improve exponentially over all prior works.
Our framework also has implications for log-concave sampling (Section 5) using only first-order queries.
Although this is a primarily theoretical work, we are working toward implementation and experimental evaluation, which will be left as future work.

Appendix A Discussion
A.1 Concurrent work
In independent and concurrent work, Gatmiry et al. (2026) study an accelerated ODE flow and remarkably obtain a high-accuracy guarantee under a set of structural assumptions. This is highly non-trivial since, as discussed in the introduction, prior works based on higher-order methods incur implicit exponential dependencies on problem parameters and the order of the method. Their results rely on the following conditions:
(A1)
The data distribution satisfies  $p_{\mathsf{data}}=p_{\star}*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I\right)$ , where  $p_{\star}$  is supported on a ball of radius  $R$ .
(A2)
Sub-exponential score error: for some  $\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}}=\widetilde{O}(\delta)$ ,
$\mathbb{P}_{X\sim p_{t}}\!\left(\|\mathsf{s}_{t}(X)-\mathsf{s}^{\star}_{t}(X)\|\geq u\right)\leq\exp(-u/\varepsilon_{\mathsf{score}})\,.$
(A3)
The score estimates  $(\mathsf{s}_{t})_{t\in[T]}$  are Lipschitz.
Under these assumptions, Gatmiry et al. (2026) propose a method with query complexity  $\widetilde{O}\left((R/\sigma)^{2}\log^{2}(1/\delta)\right)$ .
In comparing with our results, we make the following remarks:
•
The query complexity, which scales with  $R/\sigma$ , is incomparable to ours. On one hand, when  $R/\sigma$  is constant, then their guarantee is dimension-free. On the other,  $R/\sigma$  could be large in many settings.
For example, as noted by Gatmiry et al. (2026), (A1) indeed holds in the early stopping regime, i.e., their data distribution actually corresponds to the early stopped distribution  $p_{1}$ . However, for this case, achieving distributional closeness to the real data distribution  $p_{\star}$  typically requires  $\sigma\ll\delta/\sqrt{d}$ , and then we should expect  $\mathrm{poly}(1/\delta)$  dependence, instead of a high-accuracy  $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$  dependence. Further, the bound  $R$  may also scale with  $\sqrt{d}$  in some settings.
•
Assumption (A2) is substantially stronger than our average error condition (Definition 2.1). It is unclear whether standard statistical learning procedures can achieve convergence guarantees expressed in terms of sub-exponential tail bounds.
Finally, while their analysis is specific to accelerated ODE-based diffusion models, our approach applies more broadly to general first-order sampling methods, including log-concave sampling (Section 5).
Appendix B Additional notation and technical tools
For any function  $f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$  such that  $Z_{f}\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{-f(x)}dx<+\infty$ , we define  $\mu_{f}$  to be the distribution over  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  with density  $\mu_{f}(x)=\frac{1}{Z_{f}}e^{-f(x)}$ .
For  $\nu\ll\mu$ , let  $\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}\left(\nu\,\|\,\mu\right)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\lambda-1}\log\En_{\mu}[(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu})^{\lambda}]$  denote the Rényi divergence of order  $\lambda>1$ .
B.1 Functional inequalities
Definition B.1 (PI).
A probability distribution  $\mu$  on  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  satisfies a Poincaré inequality (PI) with constant  $C$  if for all smooth and compactly supported functions  $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ ,
$\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}_{\mu}(\phi)\leq C\En_{\mu}\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}\,.$
We let  $C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)$  to be the smallest constant  $C$  such that  $\mu$  satisfies PI with constant  $C$ .
Definition B.2 (LSI).
A probability distribution  $\mu$  on  $\mathbb{R}^{d}$  satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality (LSI) with constant  $C$  if for all smooth and compactly supported functions  $\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}$ ,
$\displaystyle\En_{\mu}\Big[\phi^{2}\log\frac{\phi^{2}}{\En_{\mu}[\phi^{2}]}\Big]\leq 2C\En_{\mu}\|\nabla\phi\|^{2}\,.$
We let  $C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)$  to be the smallest constant  $C$  such that  $\mu$  satisfies LSI with constant  $C$ .
The following facts are standard, see Bakry et al. (2014) or Chewi (2026, Chapter 2).
Lemma B.3 (Bakry–Émery).
If  $\mu$  is  $\alpha$ -strongly log-concave, then  $C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)\leq\frac{1}{\alpha}$ .
Lemma B.4 (Holley–Stroock perturbation principle).
Let  $\mu$  and  $\nu$  be probability measures such that  $\frac{1}{b}\leq\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\leq b$  for some parameter  $b>0$ . Then  $C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\nu)\leq b^{2}\,C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)$ .

$\displaystyle~\mathrm{const}+\En_{r,z}\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\frac{\|x-x_{0}-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\|^{2}}{2\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}\,,$
$\displaystyle\log\rho^{\star}_{x_{0}}(x)=$
$\displaystyle~\mathrm{const}+\En_{r,z}\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W^{\star}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\frac{\|x-x_{0}-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})\|^{2}}{2\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}\,.$
Decomposition of KL divergence
As an intermediate distribution, we consider the density  $\widetilde{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)$  with
$\displaystyle\log\widetilde{\rho}_{x_{0}}(x)=$
$\displaystyle~\mathrm{const}+\En_{r,z}\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\frac{\|x-x_{0}-\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})\|^{2}}{2\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}\,.$
Note that for  $u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ , it holds that
$\displaystyle\log(1+D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mathsf{N}\left(u,\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}I\right)\;\|\;{}\mathsf{N}\left(v,\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}I\right)\right))=\frac{\|u-v\|^{2}}{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}\,.$
Therefore, using Lemma B.8, Lemma B.9, and the fact that  $\log(1+Ct)\leq C\log(1+t)$  for  $t\geq 0$  and  $C\geq 1$ , we know that for any  $x_{0}$ ,
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\widehat{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\leq 2D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\widetilde{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)+\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}e^{4B}\,\|\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\|^{2}\,.$
Next, we note that we can write  $\widetilde{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\propto\rho^{\star}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,e^{h_{x_{0}}}$ , where
$\displaystyle h_{x_{0}}(x)=\En_{r,z}\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\En_{r,z}\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(W^{\star}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))\in[-2B,2B]\,.$
Then, using Lemma B.10, we can bound
$\displaystyle D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\widetilde{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\leq 4e^{2B}\,\big(D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\rho^{\star}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)+\En_{x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]\big)\,.$
Combining the inequalities above immediately implies
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\widehat{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\leq&~8e^{2B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\rho^{\star}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\\
&~+\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}e^{4B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}\|\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\|^{2}+8e^{2B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p},\,x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]\,.\end{aligned}$
(27)
Further, using  $D^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}\left(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\right)\leq\min\left\{\frac{1}{2}D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\mu_{1}\,\|\,\mu_{2}\right),1\right\}$ , we can upper bound
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot),\widehat{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\leq&~8e^{2B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\rho^{\star}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)\\
&~+e^{4B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}\min\{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\|\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\|^{2},1\}+8e^{2B}\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p},\,x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]\,.\end{aligned}$
(28)
Upper bound on  $h$
We denote
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \phi_{\eta^{\prime}}\vcentcolon=\En_{X\sim p*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta^{\prime}I\right)}\min\left\{G\|\mathsf{s}(X)-\mathsf{s}^{\star}(X)\|^{2},B^{2}\right\}\,,\qquad\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\vcentcolon=\max_{\eta^{\prime}\in[0,2\eta]}\phi_{\eta^{\prime}}\,.\end{aligned}$
(29)
In the following, we will show that  $\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p},\,x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]$  can be upper bounded by  $\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}$ .
We upper bound
$\displaystyle\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p},\,x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]=$
$\displaystyle~\En_{x\sim p,\,x_{0}\sim\mathsf{N}\left(x,\eta I\right)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\max_{r\in[0,1]}\En_{x,x_{0},z}\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(W_{z,r,x_{0}}(x)-W^{\star}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))^{2}$
$\displaystyle\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~\max_{r\in[0,1]}\En_{x,x_{0},z}\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x),\mathsf{s}^{\star}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\mathsf{s}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))\rangle)^{2}$
$\displaystyle~+\max_{r\in[0,1]}\En_{x,x_{0},z}\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x),\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\rangle)^{2}\,,$
where the expectation  $\En_{x,x_{0},z}[\cdot]$  is taken over  $x\sim p$ ,  $x_{0}\sim x+\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right)$ ,  $z\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\eta I\right)$ .
We fix  $r\in[0,1]$  and first analyze  $W_{r}\vcentcolon=\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x)$ ,  $U_{r}=\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x)-x$ .
Note that  $(U_{r},W_{r})$  is jointly Gaussian and independent of  $x\sim p$ , and we can write down the distribution of  $[U_{r};W_{r}]$  as  $[U_{r};W_{r}]\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{U}^{2}I&\sigma_{UW}I\\
\sigma_{UW}I&\sigma_{W}^{2}I\end{bmatrix}\right)$  such that
$\displaystyle\sigma_{U}^{2}=\eta((1-a_{r})^{2}+b_{r}^{2})\,,\quad\sigma_{W}^{2}=(\pi/2)^{2}\eta\,,\quad\sigma_{UW}=-\eta a_{r}^{\prime}\,.$
Applying Lemma E.11 to the function  $g(x)=\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x)-\mathsf{s}(x)$  gives
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x),\mathsf{s}^{\star}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\mathsf{s}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))\rangle)^{2}=$
$\displaystyle~\En\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(\langle W_{r},\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x+U_{r})-\mathsf{s}(x+U_{r})\rangle)^{2}$
$\displaystyle\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~B^{2}\delta+\En\min\left\{G\|\mathsf{s}^{\star}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))-\mathsf{s}(\gamma_{z,r,x_{0}}(x))\|^{2},B^{2}\right\}$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~B^{2}\delta+\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\,,$
where we recall that we denote  $G=\eta+\frac{\eta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(d+\log(1/\delta))$ . Similarly, we can bound
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{2B}(\langle\dot{\gamma}_{z,r,x_{0}}(x),\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\rangle)^{2}\lesssim$
$\displaystyle~B^{2}\delta+\En\min\left\{G\|\mathsf{s}^{\star}(x_{0})-\mathsf{s}(x_{0})\|^{2},B^{2}\right\}\leq B^{2}\delta+\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\,.$
Combining these inequalities, we can conclude that
$\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \En_{x_{0}\sim q,x\sim\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)}[h_{x_{0}}(x)^{2}]\lesssim B^{2}\delta+\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\,,\end{aligned}$
(30)
where  $\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}$  is defined in Eq. 29.
Putting everything together
We first prove Proposition E.4 (1). Combining Eq. 27, Eq. 30 and the definition of  $\phi_{\eta^{\prime}}$  in Eq. 29, we get
$\displaystyle~\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\,\|\,\widehat{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)$
$\displaystyle\lesssim$

where we use  $\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}\leq\eta\leq G$ .

$\displaystyle~\En_{x_{0}\sim\breve{p}}D^{2}_{\mathsf{H}}\left(\nu_{x_{0}}(\cdot),\widehat{\rho}_{x_{0}}(\cdot)\right)$

Lemma E.11.
Suppose that  $p=\overline{p}*\mathsf{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}I\right)$ , and consider  $x\sim p$ ,  $(U,W)\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\Sigma\right)$  is independent of  $x$ , with  $\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_{U}^{2}I&\sigma_{UW}I\\
\sigma_{UW}I&\sigma_{W}^{2}I\end{bmatrix}$ . Then, it holds that for any  $\delta\in(0,1)$ ,
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W,g(x+U)\rangle)^{2}\leq 2B^{2}\delta+4\En\min\Big\{\Big(\sigma_{W}^{2}+\frac{3\sigma_{UW}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(d+\log(1/\delta))\Big)\|g(x+U)\|^{2},\,B^{2}\Big\}\,.$
Proof. We consider the joint distribution of
$\displaystyle\bar{x}\sim\overline{p}\,,\quad x\sim\mathsf{N}\left(\bar{x},\sigma^{2}I\right)\,,\quad[U;W]\sim\mathsf{N}\left(0,\Sigma\right)\,.$
We write  $V=x-\bar{x}+U$ . Then,  $(V,W)$  is independent of  $\bar{x}\sim\overline{p}$  and the conditional distribution  $W\mid V$  can be written as
$\displaystyle W\mid V\sim\mathsf{N}\left(cV,{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}^{2}I\right),$
where  $c=\frac{\sigma_{VW}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{U}^{2}}$ ,  ${\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}^{2}=\sigma_{W}^{2}-\frac{\sigma_{VW}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{U}^{2}}$ .
Now, note that for random vector  $X$  and a fixed vector  $v$ , we can bound
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle X,v\rangle)^{2}=$
$\displaystyle~\En\min\left\{\langle X,v\rangle^{2},B^{2}\right\}$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\min\left\{\En\langle X,v\rangle^{2},B^{2}\right\}=\min\left\{v^{\top}(\En[XX^{\top}])v,B^{2}\right\}\,.$
Then, we can bound
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W-cV,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}=$
$\displaystyle~\En\left[\En\left[\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W-cV,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}\mid\bar{x},V\right]\right]$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~\En\left[\min\left\{g(\bar{x}+V)^{\top}\En\left[(W-cV)(W-cV)^{\top}\mid\bar{x},V\right]g(\bar{x}+V),B^{2}\right\}\right]$
$\displaystyle=$
$\displaystyle~\En\min\left\{{\macc@depth\char 1\relax\macc@set@skewchar\macc@nested@a 111{}}^{2}\|g(\bar{x}+V)\|^{2},B^{2}\right\}\,.$
Further, by Lemma B.5, we know that  $\mathbb{P}\left(\|V\|^{2}\geq(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{U}^{2})(2d+3\log(1/\delta))\right)\leq\delta$ . This implies
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle cV,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}\leq B^{2}\delta+\En\min\left\{c^{2}(\sigma^{2}+\sigma_{U}^{2})(2d+3\log(1/\delta))\|g(\bar{x}+V)\|^{2},B^{2}\right\}\,.$
Combining the inequalities, we can conclude
$\displaystyle\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W,g(x+U)\rangle)^{2}=$
$\displaystyle~\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~2\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle cV,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}+2\En\mathsf{Clip}_{B}(\langle W-cV,g(\bar{x}+V)\rangle)^{2}$
$\displaystyle\leq$
$\displaystyle~2B^{2}\delta+4\En\min\Big\{\Big(\sigma_{W}^{2}+\frac{3\sigma_{UW}^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}(d+\log(1/\delta))\Big)\|g(\bar{x}+V)\|^{2},B^{2}\Big\}\,.$
This gives the desired upper bound.
∎
Appendix F Log-concave sampling
Here, we apply Theorem 3.3 at each step of Algorithm 3 to sample from the RGO distribution.
Note that Theorem 3.3 requires the choice of a point  $x_{+}$  which is close to  $\text{prox}_{\eta f}(x_{0})$ .
For simplicity, we assume that this error is zero, which amounts to assuming that we have access to the proximal oracle for  $f$ .
When  $f$  is  $\beta$ -smooth and  $h\leq 1/(2\beta)$ , then computation of the proximal map is a convex optimization problem, and error analysis can be done as in Altschuler and Chewi (2024).
Otherwise, one could generalize the analysis to consider obtaining a stationary point of the proximal map optimization as in Fan et al. (2023).
We recall the definitions of PI and LSI in Definition B.1 and Definition B.2.
Theorem F.1.
Let  $\lambda\geq 2$  be a fixed constant, and  $\varepsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2}]$ .
Suppose that  $\mu\propto e^{-f}$  and that  $f$  satisfies Assumption 3.2.
Choose
$\displaystyle\eta^{-1}=C\,\big(\beta_{s}^{2}d^{s}\log(1/\varepsilon)+\beta_{s}^{2}d^{-(1-s)}\log^{2}(1/\varepsilon)\big)^{1/(1+s)}$
for a sufficiently large universal constant  $C=C_{\lambda}>0$ .
Let  $\widehat{\mu}$  denote the law of the output of Algorithm 3 initialized at  $\mu_{0}$ , where in each step the RGO is implemented by Algorithm 1 via Theorem 3.3.
Then, the following holds.
1.
Suppose that  $\mu$  satisfies a log-Sobolev inequality with constant  $C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)<\infty$ , which can only hold if  $s=1$  (i.e.,  $f$  is smooth).
Then,  $\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}\left(\widehat{\mu}\,\|\,\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  using at most
$\displaystyle N=\widetilde{O}\Big(\kappa d^{1/2}\log^{3/2}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}\left(\mu_{0}\,\|\,\mu\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\kappa\log^{2}\frac{\mathcal{R}_{\lambda}\left(\mu_{0}\,\|\,\mu\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\qquad\text{first-order queries in expectation}\,,$
where  $\kappa\coloneqq C_{\mathsf{LSI}}(\mu)\,\beta_{1}$  is the condition number.
2.
Suppose that  $\mu$  satisfies a Poincaré inequality with constant  $C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)<\infty$ .
Then,  $D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  using at most
$\displaystyle N=\widetilde{O}\Big(C_{\mathsf{PI}}(\mu)\,\beta_{s}^{2/(1+s)}\,d^{s/(1+s)}\log^{1/(1+s)}\big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\big)\,\Big(1+\frac{\log^{1/(1+s)}(1/\varepsilon)}{d^{(1-s)/s}}\Big)\log\frac{D_{\chi^{2}}\left(\mu_{0}\;\|\;{}\mu\right)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)$
first-order queries in expectation.
3.
Suppose that  $\pi$  is log-concave.
Then,  $D_{\mathsf{KL}}\left(\widehat{\mu}\,\|\,\mu\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}$  using at most
$\displaystyle N=\widetilde{O}\Big(\beta_{s}^{2/(1+s)}\,d^{s/(1+s)}\,\frac{W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{0},\mu)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)\qquad\text{first-order queries in expectation}\,.$
Proof. This follows from tracking the error of the RGO implementation and choosing  $\delta$  appropriately, exactly as in Altschuler and Chewi (2024), and is therefore omitted.
∎
This theorem can be generalized in several directions.
One could assume that  $\pi$  satisfies a Latała–Oleszkiewicz inequality which interpolates between Poincaré and log-Sobolev, as in Chen et al. (2022); Chewi et al. (2024).
One could also consider RGO implementations under more complicated settings, such as composite settings (Fan et al., 2023).
For brevity, we omit such extensions.
