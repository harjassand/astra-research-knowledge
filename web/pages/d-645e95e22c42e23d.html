## 1. Intrinsic degree-nine equations

Let T be the symmetric four-linear polarization with f(x)=T(x,x,x,x). For a direction v put F=f(v) and g(x)=T(v,v,v,x). Define a cubic in x whose coefficients are homogeneous degree-nine polynomials in v:

\[
P(v,x)=F^2T(v,x,x,x)-3F g(x)T(v,v,x,x)+2g(x)^3. \tag{1}
\]

The coefficient ideal I⊂Q[v] is computable directly from expanded f. It has at most binomial(n+2,3) generators, each of degree9. These are fixed-degree polynomial operations.

If F≠0, put ell(x)=g(x)/F and R(v)=I−2v ell. Euler gives ell(v)=1. Taylor expansion yields

\[
f(R(v)x)-f(x)=-8g(x)P(v,x)/F^3. \tag{2}
\]

Thus P(v,·)=0 iff R(v) preserves f. This is the independently reconstructed polar-reflection criterion, matched to Miura–Taki2026. Every involutive reflection has precisely this ell and its minus-one line is [v].

There are no projective excluded points on F=0: there (1) becomes2g(x)^3, so its coefficients force ∇f(v)=0. Every QPD hidden quartic is smooth over C: for active support A, ∇f(v)=0 implies Q_AA(v_A²)=0, contradicted by its nonsingular PD principal block. Hence the projective zero set of I consists exactly of the reflection points and is disjoint from F=0.

The independently gated complex-monomial theorem bounds that set by r≤2n²−n. This fact alone is not the computation bound. The next sections bound the **f-saturation exponent** needed to obtain its low-degree radical ideal.

## 2. The final radical ideal and its bounded regularity

Work in hidden direction coordinates v. The points are the n axes together with permitted pair directions. For pair i,j a reciprocal swap has scale t satisfying

\[
t^4=q_{jj}/q_{ii},\qquad q_{jk}=t^2q_{ik}\quad(k\notin\{i,j\}).
\]

The minus-one line has v_i/v_j=−t. Let p_ij(v_i,v_j) be the homogeneous squarefree polynomial with precisely the permitted nonzero pair roots. Its degree k_ij is0,2 or4: no roots; a quadratic proportional to v_i²−a v_j²; or the quartic q_ii v_i^4−q_jj v_j^4. All coefficients are rational when Q is rational.

Let J be the homogeneous radical ideal of these projective points. Then

\[
J=\left(v_iv_jv_k\ (i,j,k\text{ distinct}),\quad v_iv_jp_{ij}\ (i<j)\right). \tag{3}
\]

Proof: the triple monomials constrain support to at most two. A pair generator then leaves its axes and allowed simple roots. In degree d≥1 the quotient has n independent pure-axis monomials and, for each pair, min(k_ij,d−1) independent mixed monomials. These mixed evaluations form a Vandermonde matrix on the k_ij distinct nonzero roots and vanish on every other pair and on axes. Thus both the displayed ideal and the point ideal have Hilbert function

\[
H(d)=n+\sum_{i<j}\min(k_{ij},d-1). \tag{4}
\]

They agree in every degree, proving (3), radicality and saturation. In particular J is generated in degree≤6, and H(d)=r for every d≥5. This proof covers every allowed missing-pair and symmetry pattern.

## 3. A fixed-six-variable unit ideal for each triple

Fix distinct indices1,2,3. Introduce **independent auxiliary variables** m1,m2,m3,s1,s2,s3; these are proof tools, not supplied inputs. Set the scalar F=1 for this auxiliary system. For the PD principal matrix q=(q_ij) define

\[
\begin{aligned}
A_i&=(1-2m_is_i)(q_{ii}-s_i^2),\\
H_{ij}&=q_{ij}(1-4m_is_i)+s_j(-s_i-2q_{ii}m_i+6m_is_i^2)\quad(i\ne j),\\
B&=3s_1s_2s_3-(q_{23}s_1+q_{13}s_2+q_{12}s_3).
\end{aligned} \tag{5}
\]

Their ideal in six variables is the **unit ideal over C**. Here is a complete zero-exclusion proof. A_i=0 forces s_i≠0 and at least one of two branches: type A has m_i s_i=1/2, while type B has s_i²=q_ii. Overlap may be assigned either type.

For an A index, H_ij=0 gives

\[
q_{ij}/(s_is_j)=\kappa_i:=2-q_{ii}/s_i^2. \tag{6}
\]

For two A indices, symmetry q_ij=q_ji implies their kappas agree.

- AAA: all three normalized pair coefficients equal one kappa; B=0 gives3=3kappa, hence s_i²=q_ii and q_ij=s_i s_j, contradicting |q_ij|<sqrt(q_ii q_jj).
- AAB: the two A indices have equal kappa; (6) also gives that same kappa on their two B edges. Again B=0 gives kappa=1 and the A–A pair violates the strict principal-minor inequality.
- ABB: let r=q_23/(s_2s_3). Since s_2,s_3 are real signed square roots of positive diagonals, r is real with |r|<1. B=0 gives kappa=(3−r)/2∈(1,2). Equation(6) gives s_1²=q_11/(2−kappa)>q_11. Consequently |q_12|=kappa|s_1s_2|>sqrt(q_11q_22), again impossible.
- BBB: all three s_i are real signed square roots of q_ii. Each normalized pair coefficient has absolute value<1, contradicting B=0, which requires their sum to equal3.

No relation s=Qm, no global Euler sum, no n-dependent auxiliary variable and no real-only argument is used in this exclusion. All complex cases are covered; the real square-root statements in the B branches follow from q_ii>0 exactly.

## 4. Effective certificate and integer-power homogenization

All A_i and H_ij in (5) have total degree4; B has degree3. Kollár1988, Theorem1.5 and Corollary1.9, printedp965/PDFp4 of the saved journal scan, give a unit-ideal certificate with total degrees≤4^5·3=3072 because there are six variables, ten polynomials, and no generator of degree2. We conservatively use the even bound D=4096. Remark1.6(i) observes that the linear coefficient system descends to the base field, so for rational q the certificate can be rational.

A,H are even under the simultaneous sign change (m,s)→(−m,−s); B is odd. Average a certificate with its sign-transformed version. Its A/H coefficients then have even degree parity and its B coefficient has odd parity, without increasing the degree bound.

Now undo F=1 formally by substituting m/√F,s/√F. Write

\[
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde A_i&=(F-2m_is_i)(q_{ii}F-s_i^2),\\
\widetilde H_{ij}&=q_{ij}F^2-Fs_j(s_i+2q_{ii}m_i)-4Fq_{ij}m_is_i+6m_is_i^2s_j,\\
\widetilde B&=3s_1s_2s_3-F(q_{23}s_1+q_{13}s_2+q_{12}s_3).
\end{aligned} \tag{7}
\]

For an even-degree coefficient monomial of degree e, the A/H term has denominator F^(e/2+2); for an odd-degree monomial of degree e, the B term has denominator F^((e+3)/2). These exponents are integers and at most D/2. Therefore multiplying by F^K, K=2048, produces a polynomial identity

\[
F^K\in(\widetilde A_i,\widetilde H_{ij},\widetilde B). \tag{8}
\]

Finally substitute m_i=v_i², s_i=(Qv²)_i and F=f(v), including all outside coordinates in the s_i and F. Direct polarization gives the coefficients of (1):

\[
[x_i^3]P=v_i\widetilde A_i,\quad
[x_i^2x_j]P=v_j\widetilde H_{ij},\quad
[x_1x_2x_3]P=4v_1v_2v_3\widetilde B. \tag{9}
\]

Multiply(8) by v1v2v3 and use(9): every term is a polynomial multiple of a coefficient of P. Thus, uniformly in n,Q,

\[
f^K v_iv_jv_k\in I\qquad(i,j,k\text{ distinct}). \tag{10}

Let K3 be the triple-monomial ideal. Equation(10) gives f^K K3⊂I. Hidden rational L transports this containment to the observed coordinates without changing the exponent. L is used only in the proof.

## 5. Pair lifting with five additional f powers

Write G_ij=v_i v_j p, p=q_ii v_i^4−q_jj v_j^4, and C_ijk=v_i v_j c, c=q_ik v_i²−q_jk v_j². All equalities below are taken modulo K3, so multiplying by v_i v_j kills terms involving a third direction coordinate. Put m_i=v_i²,m_j=v_j² and Δ=q_iiq_jj−q_ij²>0. On this pair the diagonal polar cofactors factor exactly:

\[
\widetilde A_i=-\Delta m_j^2p,\qquad
\widetilde A_j=\Delta m_i^2p. \tag{11}
\]

The binary f has degree2 in m_i,m_j, so f² lies in (m_i²,m_j²). Multiplying(11) by v_i v_j proves

\[
f^2G_{ij}\in I+K3. \tag{12}
\]

For an outside index k, the coefficient [x_k²x_i]P, after multiplication by v_j and reduction modulo K3, is v_i v_j f E_i, where E_i=q_ik f−s_i s_k. Similarly obtain v_i v_j f E_j with E_j=q_jk f−s_j s_k. Exact binary algebra gives

\[
E_i=s_i c-q_{ik}p,\quad E_j=-s_j c+q_{jk}p,\quad
m_iE_i-m_jE_j=f c-s_kp. \tag{13}
\]

Thus f²C_ijk−f s_kG_ij∈I+K3. Multiply by f and apply(12) to absorb f²s_kG_ij:

\[
f^3C_{ijk}\in I+K3. \tag{14}
\]

Consequently f^(K+3) annihilates all G and C into I.

For a fixed pair, p_ij is the gcd of p and the homogeneous diagonal quadratics c_k=q_ik v_i²−q_jk v_j², up to a nonzero scalar. We need only the elementary two-variable bound

\[
f_{ij}^2 p_{ij}\in(p,c_k:k\ne i,j). \tag{15}
\]

If all c_k vanish, p_ij=p. If their span has dimension2, it contains v_i² and v_j² and hence contains f_ij. If the span has dimension1, let its nonzero generator be c. Either c divides p, and p_ij=c is already in the ideal, or gcd(c,p)=1. In that last case the binary homogeneous complete intersection of degrees2,4 has quotient Hilbert series (1+t)(1+t+t²+t³), so every degree≥5 binary form belongs to (c,p); f_ij² has degree8. This also handles a pure-square c. These cases prove(15).

Multiply(15) by v_i v_j, lift modulo K3, then multiply by f^(K+3) and use(10),(12),(14). We obtain

\[
f^{K+5}v_iv_jp_{ij}\in I. \tag{16}
\]

Combining(3),(10),(16) gives the central containment

\[
\boxed{f^{2053}J\subset I.} \tag{17}
\]

This controls the raw ideal's embedded origin directly. It is stronger than its final point count or the regularity of J, and it does not require asserting that I itself is radical.
