# Literature and provenance notes

## Prior-art verdict

The core no-go and positive rescue are already inside established causal transportability theory. Pearl and Bareinboim define transportability via explicit cross-population commonality assumptions and selection diagrams; their algorithms determine whether source experiments plus target observations identify a target effect. Standardization by a baseline covariate that renders the conditional causal response invariant is a familiar sufficient case. Invariant causal prediction (ICP) uses response-distribution invariance over observed intervention/environment regimes and has been validated on yeast gene-deletion data, but it does not make an unobserved future domain invariant by decree.

The project contribution is therefore an application-ready interface and attack, not a new general theorem: (i) explicit non-identifiability pair tailored to cell-model-to-recipient transfer, (ii) source-standardization formula with pre-treatment state, (iii) an elementary approximate-invariance error budget that keeps target-mechanism uncertainty visible, and (iv) a matched-randomized validation design. The numerical bound is direct TV bookkeeping and should not be advertised as a novelty result absent a more specific theorem or prior-art sweep.

## Primary sources checked

1. Judea Pearl and Elias Bareinboim, “External Validity: From Do-Calculus to Transportability Across Populations,” *Statistical Science* 29(4), 2014; DOI [10.1214/14-STS486](https://doi.org/10.1214/14-STS486), [arXiv:1503.01603](https://arxiv.org/abs/1503.01603). Primary formal basis: selection diagrams encode assumed cross-domain invariances; do-calculus derives whether/which source experiments and target observations suffice. The paper explicitly says transfer presupposes scientific knowledge about mechanism invariances. Its formulae are broader than the simple conditional standardization lemma in `PROOF_AND_TRANSPORT.md`.
2. Elias Bareinboim and Judea Pearl, “A General Algorithm for Deciding Transportability of Experimental Results,” [arXiv:1312.7485](https://arxiv.org/abs/1312.7485). Their abstract states a necessary-and-sufficient algorithmic criterion for transportability given the supplied causal graph/commonality assumptions and available source/target data. The algorithm does not discover those assumptions from nothing.
3. Jonas Peters, Peter Bühlmann, and Nicolai Meinshausen, “Causal inference by using invariant prediction: identification and confidence intervals,” *JRSS B* 78(5), 2016; DOI [10.1111/rssb.12167](https://doi.org/10.1111/rssb.12167), [paper PDF](https://people.math.ethz.ch/~peterbu/Files/Manuscripts/invariant-causal-prediction.pdf). ICP assumes structural/regression invariance across observed regimes; source itself notes sufficient identifiability conditions are established for certain linear Gaussian SEMs under sufficiently rich interventions. It is evidence for mechanism-invariance methods, not a guarantee for arbitrary new species/tissues.
4. Nicolai Meinshausen et al., “Methods for causal inference from gene perturbation experiments and validation,” *PNAS* 113(27), 2016; DOI [10.1073/pnas.1510493113](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1510493113), [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4941490/). They validated ICP and related methods on held-out yeast gene-deletion perturbations. The paper discusses hidden-confounding limitations, and its validation stresses held-out interventional data as the direct way to check intervention-effect predictions. This supports the design principle that prospective target perturbations are a decisive test.
5. Tzachi Hagai et al., “Gene expression variability across cells and species shapes innate immunity,” *Nature* 563, 197–202 (2018); DOI [10.1038/s41586-018-0657-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-018-0657-2), [open full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6347972/), [data repository E-MTAB accessions](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/biostudies/arrayexpress/studies/E-MTAB-5918). They used low-passage primary dermal fibroblasts from human, macaque, mouse, and rat, same anatomical origin/common culture, untreated and mock-transfection conditions, dsRNA and species-aware IFN-β treatments, plus cross-mammalian IFN for human/mouse. Their reported pattern is mixed: cytokine/chemokine response genes diverge and vary more between cells, while regulatory TF/kinase classes are more conserved. This is in-vitro empirical evidence for heterogeneous invariance, not a natural-tissue or clinical transport license.
6. Mohammad Lotfollahi, F. Alexander Wolf, and Fabian J. Theis, “scGen predicts single-cell perturbation responses,” *Nature Methods* 16, 715–721 (2019); DOI [10.1038/s41592-019-0494-8](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-019-0494-8). This primary paper reports predictions across cell types, studies, and species. It is predictive out-of-sample evidence, not causal identification under unrestricted mechanism changes; its own report frames the goal as statistical/generalization performance.
7. Alexander Bunne et al., “Learning single-cell perturbation responses using neural optimal transport,” *Nature Methods* 20 (2023); DOI [10.1038/s41592-023-01969-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-023-01969-x). CellOT reports predictive generalization on held-out patients and cross-species LPS responses. Again, held-out dataset performance is scoped empirical evidence and does not establish universal intervention invariance.
8. Constantin Ahlmann-Eltze, Wolfgang Huber, and Simon Anders, “Deep-learning-based gene perturbation effect prediction does not yet outperform simple linear baselines,” *Nature Methods* 22, 1657–1661 (2025); DOI [10.1038/s41592-025-02772-6](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02772-6). On the benchmarked unseen perturbation datasets, models did not consistently outperform mean/linear baselines. This is not a universal claim about all contexts or metrics.
9. Tian et al., “A systematic comparison of single-cell perturbation response prediction models,” *Science Advances* (published 11 September 2026); DOI [10.1126/sciadv.aed3414](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aed3414), [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42715312/). The published abstract reports 13 methods/baselines, 25 datasets, 24 metrics; no method consistently generalized across cell types, and performance depended on effect size/evaluation perspective. This directly motivates preregistered effect/delta metrics and equal-information baselines.
10. “Deep learning perturbation models can outperform baselines on calibrated metrics,” *Nature Biotechnology* (2026); DOI [10.1038/s41587-026-03307-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41587-026-03307-w). The primary search result exposed the abstract, which reports that common benchmark metrics can be miscalibrated and calibrated metrics can show deep models outperforming uninformative controls; the full paper was not opened in this pass. Therefore the test should use calibration/positive controls and multiple interpretable metrics; neither this result nor the 2025 benchmark establishes a universal ranking.
