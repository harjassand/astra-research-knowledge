# Exact cadence asymptotics for a diffusion-equivalent burst pair

## 1. Statistical experiment and scope

For mechanism \(b\in\{0,1\}\), independent event channels with marks \(j=1,2,3,4\) have per-volume rates
\[
k^{(0)}=(1,1,4,0),\qquad k^{(1)}=(0,4,1,1).
\]
An event of mark \(j\) adds \(j\) units to one measured product count. On an interval of length \(\Delta\), let \(\mu=V\Delta\) and
\[
Y_b^\mu=\sum_{j=1}^4 jN_j,\qquad N_j\ {\buildrel ind\over\sim}\ {\rm Pois}(\mu k_j^{(b)}).
\]
Only exact cumulative endpoint counts are read; event times and marks are hidden. Thus increments over nonoverlapping intervals are iid. The main result assumes known rates, known \(V,\Delta,T\), an initially known count, no additive background, no state dependence, and exact readout. It is a theorem about this observation model, not a claim that a real assay obeys it.

The rate moments \(M_r^{(b)}=\sum_j k_j^{(b)}j^r\) are
\[
M_0=6,\quad M_1=15,\quad M_2=41,\quad
M_3^{(0)}=117,\quad M_3^{(1)}=123,\quad
M_4=(341,401),\quad M_6=(2981,5081).
\]
The cumulant of order \(r\) of \(Y_b^\mu\) is \(\mu M_r^{(b)}\).

## 2. Common diffusion limit and exact support defect

For the cumulative process \(X_b^V(t)\), the centered characteristic exponent is
\[
Vt\sum_j k_j^{(b)}
\left(e^{iuj/\sqrt V}-1-\frac{iuj}{\sqrt V}\right)
=-\frac{41tu^2}{2}+O(V^{-1/2})
\]
for bounded \(u\). Bounded jumps and independent increments give the functional limit
\[
\frac{X_b^V(t)-15Vt}{\sqrt V}\Rightarrow \sqrt{41}\,W(t)
\]
on a fixed time interval for both \(b\). Their concentration limits are also both \(15t\). The two mechanisms therefore have identical scalar-count diffusion limits.

There is a finite-\(\mu\) support defect. Model 0 can make exactly one mark-1 event and no other event, so
\[
P_0^\mu(Y=1)=\mu e^{-6\mu}>0,
\qquad P_1^\mu(Y=1)=0.
\]
Model 0 has positive mass at every nonnegative integer. Model 1 has positive mass at 0 and at every integer at least 2. Hence \(P_1^\mu\ll P_0^\mu\), \(D(P_1^\mu\Vert P_0^\mu)<\infty\), but
\[
D(P_0^\mu\Vert P_1^\mu)=+\infty
\]
for every \(\mu>0\). In the critical regime \(n/\mu=O(1)\), the chance of seeing the exceptional count under model 0 is at most \(n\mu e^{-6\mu}=o(1)\).

### Relation to the published reaction-SDE obstruction

Interpret mark j as a zero-order reaction channel ∅ → jP with propensity V k_j^(b). The rate difference is
δk = k^(1) − k^(0) = (−1, 3, −3, 1).
It annihilates the zeroth, first, and second mark moments:
Σ_j δk_j = 0,  Σ_j j δk_j = 0,  Σ_j j² δk_j = 0,
but Σ_j j³ δk_j = 6. The two networks therefore have the same total event rate, scalar CLE drift, and diffusion coefficient, while their exact jump laws differ at the third cumulant.

This is an explicit instance of the generator-level obstruction in Faul, Hoessly, and Xia (2026). Their Remark 3.5 and Theorem 3.3 characterize rate identifiability for each source complex by linear independence of vectors (v, vvᵀ), where v is a reaction displacement. Here the four scalar displacements 1, 2, 3, 4 give vectors (j, j²) in R², which are linearly dependent; δk is a null direction. Their paper establishes the CLE/generator collision, not a finite-cadence endpoint-testing law. The present result asks how much of the discarded jump information is recoverable from a charged, coarsened count-only channel.

## 3. Pair-specific one-step Hellinger, chi-square, and KL theorem

Write \(p_b^\mu(m)=P_b^\mu(Y=m)\), \(r_\mu(m)=p_1^\mu(m)/p_0^\mu(m)\) on the support of \(P_0^\mu\), and
\[
\chi_\mu^2=E_0[(r_\mu-1)^2],\qquad
H_\mu^2=\sum_{m\ge0}(\sqrt{p_1^\mu(m)}-\sqrt{p_0^\mu(m)})^2.
\]
Here \(H^2\) is the unnormalized squared Hellinger distance. For this particular pair,
\[
\chi_\mu^2=\frac{6}{41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}),\qquad
H_\mu^2=\frac{3}{2\,41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}),
\]
and
\[
D(P_1^\mu\Vert P_0^\mu)=\frac{3}{41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}).
\]
The affinity \(A_\mu=\sum_m\sqrt{p_0^\mu(m)p_1^\mu(m)}\) is therefore
\[
A_\mu=1-\frac{3}{4\,41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}).
\]

### Central local expansion

The probability generating function is
\[
G_b(z)=\exp\!\left(\mu\sum_j k_j^{(b)}(z^j-1)\right).
\]
Set \(Z=(Y-15\mu)/\sqrt{41\mu}\). The lattice has span one. Local Fourier inversion, or the lattice Edgeworth theorem, gives for each fixed \(0<\alpha<1/24\), uniformly over \(|Z|\le\mu^\alpha\),
\[
p_b^\mu(m)=\frac{\phi(Z)}{\sqrt{41\mu}}\left[
1+\frac{M_3^{(b)}}{6\,41^{3/2}\sqrt\mu}H_3(Z)
+O\!\left(\mu^{-1}(1+|Z|^6)\right)
\right],
\]
with a uniform constant after fixing \(\alpha\) sufficiently below \(1/6\). This follows by expanding the centered log-characteristic function near frequency zero and using that the span-one characteristic function has modulus strictly below one away from zero. The difference of the first Edgeworth coefficients is
\[
\frac{M_3^{(1)}-M_3^{(0)}}{6\,41^{3/2}}=\frac1{41^{3/2}}.
\]
Consequently, on this central window,
\[
r_\mu(m)-1=\frac{H_3(Z)}{41^{3/2}\sqrt\mu}
+O\!\left(\mu^{-1}(1+|Z|^{12})\right).
\]
Since standardized moments of every fixed order are uniformly bounded and \(Z\Rightarrow N(0,1)\),
\[
\sum_{m:\,|Z|\le\mu^\alpha}p_0^\mu(m)(r_\mu(m)-1)^2
=\frac{6}{41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}),
\]
using \(E[H_3(N)^2]=3!=6\).

### Pair-specific \(L^2(P_0)\) tail control

A marginal Chernoff bound alone is insufficient for chi-square: the relevant tail summand is \(p_1(m)^2/p_0(m)\). We control that ratio in three regions.

Define
\[
\Lambda_b(s)=\sum_j k_j^{(b)}(e^{js}-1),\qquad
I_b(x)=\sup_s\{sx-\Lambda_b(s)\},\qquad F(x)=I_0(x)-2I_1(x).
\]
For \(x>0\), let \(s_b(x)\) solve \(\Lambda_b'(s_b)=x\). Then \(F'(x)=s_0(x)-2s_1(x)\). If \(y=e^{s_1(x)}\), the comparison \(\Lambda_0'(2s_1)-\Lambda_1'(s_1)\) is
\[
y^2(12y^4-2y^2-3y-7).
\]
The bracket is strictly negative for \(0<y<1\): \(12y^4<12y\) and \(9y-2y^2-7<0\) there. It is strictly positive for \(y>1\), since its derivative \(48y^3-4y-3\) is positive for \(y\ge1\) and the bracket vanishes at 1. Since \(\Lambda_0'\) is strictly increasing, \(F'\) is positive when \(x<15\) and negative when \(x>15\). Thus \(F\) has the unique maximum \(F(15)=0\); also \(F''(15)=1/41-2/41=-1/41\).

On a compact range \(m/\mu\in[a,B]\subset(0,\infty)\), exponential tilting gives the uniform local saddlepoint estimate
\[
p_b^\mu(m)=\frac{1+o(1)}{\sqrt{2\pi\mu\Lambda_b''(s_b(x))}}\,
e^{-\mu I_b(x)},\qquad x=m/\mu.
\]
This follows by tilting to mean \(m\) and applying the span-one local central limit theorem uniformly over the compact tilt-parameter interval. Hence \(p_1(m)^2/p_0(m)\) has exponential factor \(e^{\mu F(x)}\). Outside \(|m-15\mu|\le\sqrt{41\mu}\,\mu^\alpha\), the unique maximum and negative curvature of \(F\) make the sum over the compact region \(o(\mu^{-1})\).

For the low-count region choose \(a\in(0,1)\) so small that \(2J(a/2)>6\), where
\[
J(c)=6-c+c\log(c/6),\qquad 0<c<6,
\]
is the lower-tail rate for \({\rm Pois}(6\mu)\). The mark-1-only event gives \(p_0^\mu(m)\ge e^{-6\mu}\mu^m/m!\ge e^{-6\mu}\) for all \(m\le a\mu\). Under model 1 every event contributes at least 2, so \(Y\le a\mu\) implies that the total event count is at most \(a\mu/2\). Therefore
\[
\sum_{m\le a\mu}\frac{p_1^\mu(m)^2}{p_0^\mu(m)}
\le O(\mu)\exp\{[6-2J(a/2)]\mu\}=o(\mu^{-1}).
\]

For the high-count region, under model 1 each event contributes at most 4, so \(Y\ge m\) implies \(N\ge m/4\) for \(N\sim{\rm Pois}(6\mu)\). A Poisson Chernoff bound gives, for \(m\ge B\mu\) with \(B>24\),
\[
\log p_1^\mu(m)\le-\mu\left[\frac{x}{4}\log\frac{x}{24}-\frac{x}{4}+6\right],
\qquad x=m/\mu.
\]
Under model 0, write \(m=3q+r\), \(r\in\{0,1,2\}\), and realize the count using \(q\) mark-3 events and, if \(r>0\), one mark-\(r\) event (marks 1 and 2 both have rate 1). Stirling's inequality then gives
\[
\log p_0^\mu(m)\ge-\frac m3\log(m/\mu)-C(m+\mu+\log(m+\mu+2))
\]
for a fixed constant \(C\). Combining these inequalities,
\[
\log\frac{p_1^\mu(m)^2}{p_0^\mu(m)}
\le-\frac m6\log(m/\mu)+C'(m+\mu+\log(m+\mu+2)).
\]
For sufficiently large fixed \(B\), this is at most \(-c\,m\log(m/\mu)\) for some \(c>0\), and its sum over \(m\ge B\mu\) is \(o(\mu^{-1})\).

The remaining tail term in \(E_0[(r-1)^2]\) is bounded by twice the sum of \(p_1^2/p_0\) and twice the \(P_0\) probability of the same tail. The latter is smaller than any inverse power of \(\mu\) by the usual two-sided Chernoff bound after taking the central window to grow as \(\mu^\alpha\). Therefore
\[
\chi_\mu^2=\frac{6}{41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}).
\]
This tail argument is pair-specific; it is not a general theorem for bounded mark supports.

### Convert chi-square to Hellinger and reverse KL

On the central window, \(r_\mu-1=o(1)\) uniformly. There,
\[
(\sqrt{r_\mu}-1)^2=\frac{(r_\mu-1)^2}{(\sqrt{r_\mu}+1)^2}
=\frac14(r_\mu-1)^2(1+o(1)).
\]
Outside, \((\sqrt r-1)^2\le(r-1)^2\), so the tail is \(o(\mu^{-1})\). This proves the stated \(H^2\) coefficient.

For reverse KL, let \(f(r)=r\log r-r+1\), with \(f(0)=1\). The inequality
\[
0\le f(r)\le(r-1)^2
\]
follows from \(\log r\le r-1\). On the central window \(f(r)=\tfrac12(r-1)^2+o((r-1)^2)\), and the tail is bounded by the chi-square tail. Since \(E_0(r-1)=0\),
\[
D(P_1^\mu\Vert P_0^\mu)=E_0 f(r_\mu)
=\tfrac12\chi_\mu^2+o(\mu^{-1})
=\frac{3}{41^3\mu}+o(\mu^{-1}).
\]
The opposite KL direction stays infinite because of the mass at 1.

## 4. Critical binary limit and optimal test

Let \(n=n_\mu\) and \(n/\mu\to\lambda\in[0,\infty)\). Condition the null on \(Y\ne1\), writing \(\bar P_0^\mu=P_0^\mu(\cdot\mid Y\ne1)\). The product total-variation distance from \(P_0^{\otimes n}\) to \((\bar P_0^\mu)^{\otimes n}\) is at most \(n\mu e^{-6\mu}=o(1)\) whenever \(n/\mu\) is bounded. This removes the one-point support defect in the critical experiment without changing asymptotic testing risk.

Under \(\bar P_0^\mu\), put \(U_\mu=dP_1^\mu/d\bar P_0^\mu-1\). The preceding central expansion and tail split imply:
\[
E_{\bar0}U_\mu=0,\qquad
\mu E_{\bar0}U_\mu^2\to\frac6{41^3},
\]
the triangular-array Lindeberg condition for \(U_\mu\), and \(nE_{\bar0}U_\mu^4\to0\) when \(n/\mu\to\lambda<\infty\). To see Lindeberg, on the central window \(\sup |U_\mu|=O(\mu^{-1/2+3\alpha})\to0\); outside it, \(nE_{\bar0}U_\mu^2=o(1)\) by the pair-specific tail bound. The fourth-moment assertion follows from the polynomial central remainder, while the tail contribution to \(\sum_i U_i^2\) vanishes in expectation. Thus
\[
\sum_{i=1}^n U_{\mu,i}\Rightarrow N(0,I),\qquad
\sum_{i=1}^n U_{\mu,i}^2\to I,\qquad
I=\frac{6\lambda}{41^3}.
\]
With probability tending to one every observation lies in the central window, so \(\sum_i\log(1+U_{\mu,i})=\sum_i U_{\mu,i}-\frac12\sum_iU_{\mu,i}^2+o_P(1)\). The regularized log likelihood therefore converges under the null to \(N(-I/2,I)\); Le Cam's third lemma gives \(N(+I/2,I)\) under model 1. The Neyman–Pearson test is asymptotically optimal, and with equal priors its limiting error under either mechanism is
\[
e^*(\lambda)=\Phi(-\sqrt I/2)
=\Phi\!\left(-\sqrt{\frac{3\lambda}{2\,41^3}}\right).
\]
The leading score is the third Hermite statistic \(\sum_i H_3((Y_i-15\mu)/\sqrt{41\mu})\); its midpoint threshold has the same limit.

For fixed physical \(T\), \(n\sim T/\Delta\), so
\[
\lambda_V=\frac n\mu\sim\frac{T}{V\Delta^2}.
\]
Consequently:
- \(T/(V\Delta^2)\to0\) gives total variation tending to 0: no test beats chance asymptotically.
- \(T/(V\Delta^2)\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)\) gives the binary Gaussian limit above.
- \(T/(V\Delta^2)\to\infty\) with \(\mu\to\infty\) makes product affinity tend to zero and total variation tend to 1, so consistent testing is possible.

The finer regime is also distinguishable in this exact channel. If \(\mu\le C\), the test that selects model 0 after any increment equal to 1 has zero type-II error and type-I error
\[
(1-\mu e^{-6\mu})^n\le\exp(-n\mu e^{-6C})\to0,
\]
since \(n\mu\sim VT\to\infty\). Thus the ideal model's nontrivial boundary is \(\Delta\asymp V^{-1/2}\).

Numerically \(I=6\lambda/41^3\). A 5% equal-prior limiting error requires \(I=10.8222\), so \(\lambda\approx124{,}313\), equivalently
\[
\Delta\approx0.002836\sqrt{T/V}.
\]
A 5% sum of type-I and type-II limiting errors requires \(I=15.3658\), \(\lambda\approx176{,}505\), and \(\Delta\approx0.002380\sqrt{T/V}\). These are asymptotic design conversions, not finite-sample certificates.

## 5. General first-differing-cumulant Hellinger law

Let two compound-Poisson laws have finite positive-integer mark supports with the same lattice span \(d\), bounded marks, and positive rates on their stated supports. Their additive semigroups then have the same eventual support on \(d\mathbb Z_{\ge0}\); any finite support mismatch consists of fixed counts whose probabilities are exponentially small in \(\mu\), up to a polynomial factor. The total event rates \(M_0\) may differ. Suppose the positive-order cumulants per unit \(\mu\) match through order \(k\ge2\), their common second cumulant is \(m_2>0\), and
\[
\delta=M_{k+1}^{(1)}-M_{k+1}^{(0)}\ne0.
\]
For \(Z=(Y-\mu M_1)/\sqrt{\mu m_2}\), the first local Edgeworth difference is
\[
r_\mu-1=
\frac{\delta}{(k+1)!\,m_2^{(k+1)/2}}
\mu^{-(k-1)/2}H_{k+1}(Z)
+\text{higher order}.
\]
On the central window this gives the Hermite-square coefficient. Outside the window, use
\[
(\sqrt p-\sqrt q)^2\le p+q
\]
and two-sided Chernoff bounds for each marginal. Choosing a sufficiently wide growing central window makes those tails \(o(\mu^{-(k-1)})\). Finite support-mismatch atoms are exponentially small. Therefore
\[
H_\mu^2\sim
\frac{\delta^2}{4(k+1)!\,m_2^{k+1}}\mu^{-(k-1)},\qquad
1-A_\mu\sim
\frac{\delta^2}{8(k+1)!\,m_2^{k+1}}\mu^{-(k-1)}.
\]
This is a Hellinger/affinity theorem only. For \(n\) increments, product affinity is \(A_\mu^n\). With \(\lambda_k=n/\mu^{k-1}\):
- if \(\lambda_k\to0\), total variation tends to zero;
- if \(\lambda_k\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)\), the affinity tends to \(\exp[-C_k\lambda]\), where \(C_k=\delta^2/[8(k+1)!m_2^{k+1}]\), and the optimal error is bounded using the affinity inequalities below;
- if \(\lambda_k\to\infty\) and \(\mu\to\infty\), total variation tends to one.

For fixed \(T\), this scale is \(n/\mu^{k-1}\sim T/(V^{k-1}\Delta^k)\), giving \(\Delta\asymp V^{-(k-1)/k}\). Equal \(M_0\) is not required: a zero-increment probability \(e^{-M_0\mu}\), or any fixed support-exception probability, is exponentially small and remains negligible after polynomially many bins. A global chi-square or KL conclusion is not part of this general theorem.

The common-span condition matters. For example, rates \((1/2,1/2)\) on marks \(\{2,4\}\) and \((1/8,3/4,1/8)\) on \(\{1,3,5\}\) match \(M_0=1,M_1=3,M_2=10,M_3=36\). The first law is always even. Under the second, the probability of an odd increment is \((1-e^{-2\mu})/2\to1/2\). The total variation therefore does not vanish despite the moment match.

The global chi-square route cannot be generalized merely from bounded marks and a shared lattice. Let \(A\) have rates \((1/3,1/3,1/3)\) on marks \(\{1,2,3\}\). For \(0<\varepsilon<1/45\), let \(B\) have rates
\[
(1/3-10\varepsilon,\;1/3+24\varepsilon,\;1/3-15\varepsilon,\;\varepsilon)
\]
on \(\{1,2,3,7\}\). They match \(M_0=1,M_1=2,M_2=14/3\) and differ by \(120\varepsilon\) at \(M_3\). Along \(m=7r\), \(p_B(m)\) is bounded below by the all-mark-7 event, while \(p_A(m)\) requires at least \(\lceil7r/3\rceil\) events. Stirling and a Poisson tail bound give
\[
\log\frac{p_B(7r)^2}{p_A(7r)}
\ge \frac r3\log r+O(r)\to\infty
\]
as \(r\to\infty\) for every fixed \(\mu>0\). Thus \(\chi^2(P_B^\mu\Vert P_A^\mu)=\infty\). Do not infer general-k LAN or KL from the local Edgeworth term.

For finite testing, if \(A=\sum_m\sqrt{p_0(m)p_1(m)}\), then
\[
1-A^n\le {\rm TV}(P_0^{\otimes n},P_1^{\otimes n})
\le\sqrt{1-A^{2n}},
\]
so equal-prior error obeys
\[
\frac{1-\sqrt{1-A^{2n}}}{2}\le e_n^*\le\frac{A^n}{2}.
\]
These bounds are valid at any \(n,\mu\); a finite affinity computed by the recurrence plus a Chernoff remainder gives a numerical interval. They need not be tight at the critical scale.

## 6. Exact recurrence, finite guarantees, and computation

Differentiating the PGF gives the exact coefficient recurrence
\[
q_{b,0}=1,\qquad
q_{b,m}=\frac{\mu}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{\min(4,m)}j k_j^{(b)}q_{b,m-j},\qquad
p_b^\mu(m)=e^{-6\mu}q_{b,m}.
\]
The common exponential cancels from the likelihood ratio. The Neyman–Pearson statistic is the sum of the exact log ratios; the exact rule immediately selects model 0 if any increment is 1.

A finite-sample score guarantee is also available. Let
\[
S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nH_3\!\left(\frac{Y_i-15\mu}{\sqrt{41\mu}}\right),\qquad
d_\mu=E_1H_3(Z)-E_0H_3(Z)=\frac6{41^{3/2}\sqrt\mu}.
\]
Its one-bin variance is
\[
v_b(\mu)=6+\frac{9M_4^{(b)}}{41^2\mu}
+\frac{9(M_3^{(b)})^2}{41^3\mu}
+\frac{M_6^{(b)}}{41^3\mu^2}.
\]
The midpoint score test has, for every \(n,\mu>0\),
\[
P_0(\text{choose 1})+P_1(\text{choose 0})
\le \min\!\left\{2,\frac{41^3\mu}{9n}\,[v_0(\mu)+v_1(\mu)]\right\}.
\]
This bound is conservative; for the small third-cumulant gap it is not a 5%-power certificate near the asymptotic design target. The exact likelihood is the strongest fixed-model test.

The log-domain recurrence in exact_cp_audit.py evaluates the PMFs, affinity and reverse KL. At \(\mu=1000\), an independent 50-digit recurrence gives
\[
\mu H^2=2.1771260016551\times10^{-5},\qquad
\mu D(P_1\Vert P_0)=4.3542100844120\times10^{-5},
\]
versus limits \(3/(2\,41^3)=2.1764048693432\times10^{-5}\) and \(3/41^3=4.3528097386863\times10^{-5}\). At the much smaller \(\mu=100\), the computed values are \(2.18364\times10^{-5}\) and \(4.36686\times10^{-5}\). At mu=1000 the 19150 cutoff is about 20.5 standard deviations above the mean; the optimized Chernoff omitted-tail bounds are 2.85e-84 and 6.79e-84. These are finite floating-point/arbitrary-precision diagnostics, not interval-certified numerics and not a replacement for the analytic tail split.

The computed affinity also supplies finite error bounds through the preceding display. For example at \(\mu=100\), the truncation-based bounds give equal-prior optimal error between about 0.017 and 0.129 when \(n/\mu\approx124{,}288\); the range is too wide to certify the asymptotic 5% target. The score Chebyshev guarantee is also weak at that operating point. Thus the limiting 5% cadence conversion is a falsifiable asymptotic prediction, not a finite planner guarantee.

## 7. Shared unknown scale and fixed-scale endpoint read noise

The main likelihood theorem takes \(\mu\) known. A useful narrower robustness result uses the same endpoint sample to estimate an unknown common intensity scale. Suppose the two rate shapes \(k^{(b)}\) are known, but a common factor is unknown, so the effective per-bin scale \(\nu\) replaces \(\mu\) in both models. Then \(E_bY=15\nu\), \({\rm Var}_b(Y)=41\nu\), and
\[
\widehat\nu=\frac{1}{15n}\sum_{i=1}^nY_i
\]
satisfies \(\widehat\nu-\nu=O_P(1)\) whenever \(n/\nu\to\lambda\in(0,\infty)\), while \(\widehat\nu/\nu\to1\). The plugin score
\[
\widehat S_n=\sum_iH_3\!\left(\frac{Y_i-15\widehat\nu}{\sqrt{41\widehat\nu}}\right)
\]
differs from the known-\(\nu\) score by \(O_P(1)\), hence by \(o_P(\sqrt n)\). Indeed \(\widehat\nu-\nu=O_P(1)\) shifts each standardized count by a common \(O_P(\nu^{-1/2})\) centering perturbation plus an \(O_P(\nu^{-1})Z_i\) scale perturbation; the first derivative is \(3H_2(Z_i)\), whose sum is \(O_P(\sqrt n)\) because its exact expectation is zero, and the higher Taylor terms sum to \(O_P(1)\). Thus the same standardized score limit, and its asymptotic power, survive an unknown common multiplier. This does not cover unknown mark proportions, arbitrary class-specific rates, or a rate profile that can close the third-cumulant gap. The product \(V\) times a common physical rate multiplier is identifiable, not the two factors separately.

Now suppose endpoint readings are \(\widetilde X_i=X_i+\epsilon_i\), with iid errors independent of chemistry, common to both mechanisms, mean zero and fixed finite sixth moment. Differencing gives \(U_i=Y_i+\eta_i\), \(\eta_i=\epsilon_i-\epsilon_{i-1}\), a 1-dependent read-noise sequence. With \(Z_i=(Y_i-15\nu)/\sqrt{41\nu}\), \(a_i=\eta_i/\sqrt{41\nu}\), the exact identity
\[
H_3(Z_i+a_i)-H_3(Z_i)=3a_iH_2(Z_i)+3a_i^2Z_i+a_i^3
\]
implies \({\rm Var}(\sum_i[H_3(Z_i+a_i)-H_3(Z_i)])=O(n/\nu)\). In the critical regime \(n/\nu\to\lambda\), this is \(O(1)\), so the score difference is \(O_P(1)=o_P(\sqrt n)\). Also \(\widehat\nu=(15n)^{-1}\sum_iU_i\) differs from its clean version only by the telescoping term \((\epsilon_n-\epsilon_0)/(15n)\). Combining both arguments, the same plugin score has the same limiting Gaussian separation and error curve under this fixed-scale endpoint noise. This is a score-test result; it does not supply the exact dependent-noisy likelihood. Noise variance growing like \(\nu\), growing quantization bins, saturation, shared drift, or mechanism-dependent errors can alter the limit.

## 8. Prior art and novelty boundary

Duval and Hoffmann, “Statistical inference across time scales,” *Electronic Journal of Statistics* 5 (2011), 2004–2030, [DOI 10.1214/11-EJS660](https://doi.org/10.1214/11-EJS660), [arXiv:1106.1031](https://arxiv.org/abs/1106.1031), prove LAN/Fisher-information transitions across sampling scales in a one-parameter symmetric-Bernoulli compound-Poisson model (Theorems 2.2, 2.4, 2.5). In §3 they discuss an arbitrary discrete compound law and say analogous results could presumably be obtained if the required asymptotic expansions were available. The article does not state an explicit fixed-pair testing law indexed by the first differing cumulant, nor its \(k\)-dependent exponent. It establishes the broad cadence-information mechanism and blocks any novelty claim for that premise.

Duval, “When is it no longer possible to estimate a compound Poisson process?”, *Electronic Journal of Statistics* 8 (2014), 274–301, [DOI 10.1214/14-EJS885](https://doi.org/10.1214/14-EJS885), studies centered finite-variance compound-Poisson processes under macroscopic sampling as \(T\to\infty\). Its abstract reports a parametric Fisher-information analysis, a lower bound ruling out consistent estimation when \(\Delta_T\) grows faster than \(\sqrt T\), and Gaussian asymptotic-equivalence regimes. Rescaling physical time by \(s=Vt\) sends effective horizon to \(S=VT\) and effective cadence to \(d=V\Delta\); \(d\asymp\sqrt S\) is exactly \(\Delta\asymp\sqrt{T/V}\). The broad \(k=2\) exponent is therefore prior art. The parametric example is not reconstructed theorem-by-theorem here, so exact overlap with its constants and fixed-pair analogue is unresolved.

The local lattice Edgeworth method is classical: Kolassa and McCullagh, “Edgeworth series for lattice distributions,” *Annals of Statistics* 18(2) (1990), 981–985, [DOI 10.1214/aos/1176347637](https://doi.org/10.1214/aos/1176347637); Petrov, “Local Limit Theorems for Non-Identical Lattice Distributions,” *Theory of Probability and its Applications* 7(3) (1962), 333–335, [DOI 10.1137/1107033](https://doi.org/10.1137/1107033). These are method sources, not external validation of the pair-specific saddlepoint tail split.

The motivating reaction-network connection is Faul, Hoessly, and Xia, “Identifiability of SDEs for reaction networks,” *European Journal of Applied Mathematics* (2026), [DOI 10.1017/S0956792526100382](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0956792526100382), [arXiv:2505.07638](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07638). Its drift/covariance identifiability theorem motivates looking beyond the diffusion signature; it does not imply this sampled endpoint theorem or identify a biological implementation.

The strongest defensible outcome is a pair-specific regularized LAN and cadence prediction, a general first-differing-cumulant Hellinger law under common-span assumptions, and a score robustness result for one shared unknown intensity scale plus fixed iid endpoint errors. The \(k=2\) exponent itself is established prior art; priority for the exact constants and application remains unknown. No physical assay, instrument cost, or empirical validation is claimed.
