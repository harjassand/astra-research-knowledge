FINITE-HORIZON ALIASING FOR PASSIVE ACQUISITION OF PREDICTIVE STRUCTURE
Status: proved constructions and bounds; scope is the stated HMM classes.

Question and interface
----------------------
Training data consist of n independent trajectories, each reset to the stated
initial distribution and observed for T steps. The learner receives only the
observation strings unless a supplied-representation comparison is stated.
For probability laws P,Q, TV(P,Q) = sup_A |P(A)-Q(A)|. KL divergences use
natural logarithms. A prediction target is the probability of a specified
future observation from the reset initial condition. The constructions below
are time-homogeneous finite-state HMMs with binary, noisy emissions.

The results are information limits, not computational lower bounds. In
particular, the two-state example is nearly non-ergodic as T grows. A uniform
mixing-time or minimum-transition assumption would exclude that scaling.

1. Exact horizon ambiguity: no amount of length-T data suffices
---------------------------------------------------------------
Fix T >= 1. Use hidden states c_1,...,c_{T+1},a, initial state c_1, and
deterministic transitions c_i -> c_{i+1} for i <= T, c_{T+1} -> a, and
a -> a. At c_1,...,c_T and a, emit an independent fair bit. At c_{T+1},
model H_+ emits Bernoulli(3/4), and model H_- emits Bernoulli(1/4).

The hidden-state path is c_1,...,c_{T+1},a,a,... . Therefore

  Law_{H_+}(X_1,...,X_T) = Law_{H_-}(X_1,...,X_T) = Bernoulli(1/2)^T,

exactly. Yet for every observed prefix x_1:T,

  P_{H_+}(X_{T+1}=1 | x_1:T) = 3/4,
  P_{H_-}(X_{T+1}=1 | x_1:T) = 1/4.

The one-step predictive probabilities differ by 1/2, and the two future-bit
laws have TV distance 1/2. For any n, the joint laws of n length-T trajectories
remain exactly equal. Under equal prior probability on H_+ and H_-, every
classifier has error 1/2; any prediction estimate accurate to strictly less
than 1/4 under both models would classify them, so such accuracy is impossible
from these data. The first passive horizon at which the models can be
distinguished is T+1. Direct access that can reset the hidden process to
c_{T+1} also removes the delay, but the emitted bit remains noisy.

For a general contrast, replace 3/4 and 1/4 by 1/2+delta and 1/2-delta,
where 0 < delta < 1/2. A length-(T+1) trajectory contains exactly one
informative Bernoulli draw; its KL divergence between H_+ and H_- is

  d(delta) = 2 delta log((1+2 delta)/(1-2 delta)) nats.

For n independent trajectories, KL is n d(delta). Le Cam's two-point testing
identity gives optimal equal-prior error (1-TV(P_-^n,P_+^n))/2. Pinsker's
inequality, TV <= sqrt(KL/2), implies that error at most alpha < 1/2 requires

  n >= 2(1-2 alpha)^2 / d(delta).

As delta -> 0, d(delta) = 8 delta^2 + O(delta^4), so this gives
n >= (1-2 alpha)^2/(4 delta^2) (1+O(delta^2)). This is the familiar
inverse-square cost of resolving a noisy Bernoulli contrast, after the
trajectory has reached the informative state. For delta=1/4, d=log(3)/2.
At 90% success, Pinsker gives n >= 2(0.8)^2/(log(3)/2) > 2.33, hence at
least 3 trajectories. The exact optimal test is a majority test on the
informative bits (randomized on ties); its error is 53/512 at n=5 and n=6,
and 289/4096 at n=7, so 7 trajectories are necessary and sufficient for
90% success in this fixed pair. No number of length-T trajectories works.

2. Close finite-horizon laws with only two hidden states
--------------------------------------------------------
The exact clock above uses T+2 states. A second example keeps the number of
hidden states equal to two. Let Z_1=0. State 0 emits Bernoulli(1/4), state 1
emits Bernoulli(3/4), and state 1 is absorbing. Under H_0, state 0 is
absorbing. Under H_1, each transition from 0 goes to 1 with probability
lambda and stays at 0 otherwise. Emissions are conditionally independent
given the hidden states. All observations are noisy in both states.

Couple the models to use the same emissions while H_1 has not switched. The
length-T observation strings are identical in this coupling unless H_1
switches during the first T-1 transitions. Thus

  TV(P_0^(T), P_1^(T)) <= 1-(1-lambda)^(T-1) <= (T-1)lambda.

After h observations, the chance that H_1 has reached state 1 is
r_h = 1-(1-lambda)^(h-1). Consequently

  P_0(X_h=1) = 1/4,
  P_1(X_h=1) = 1/4 + r_h/2.

Set lambda=1/T^2 for T >= 2 and predict at h=T+T^2. Since h-1 >= T^2,

  TV(P_0^(T),P_1^(T)) <= (T-1)/T^2 < 1/T,
  |P_1(X_h=1)-P_0(X_h=1)| >= (1-exp(-1))/2 ~= 0.31606.

Thus an observation law can be O(1/T)-close over the available horizon while
a much later one-step marginal prediction differs by a fixed constant. The
model has two hidden states for every T. This construction uses a rare
transition; the gap accumulates over a time of order 1/lambda.

Noisy-trajectory sample lower bound. For n independent length-T trajectories,
couple all H_1 hidden paths to H_0. The complete observed datasets agree if
none of the n paths switches, an event of probability (1-lambda)^(n(T-1)).
Therefore

  TV(P_0^(T,n),P_1^(T,n)) <= 1-(1-lambda)^(n(T-1)),
  optimal equal-prior testing error >= (1-lambda)^(n(T-1))/2.

To estimate the future probability to error strictly less than half its
between-model gap with failure probability at most alpha under both models
would yield a test of error at most alpha. Necessarily,

  n >= log(1/(2 alpha)) / (-(T-1) log(1-lambda)).

For lambda=1/T^2 this is asymptotic to
T log(1/(2 alpha)); for example, alpha=1/3 requires at least about 0.405 T
trajectories. This is a lower bound from a coupling, so it holds even if the
post-switch emissions made switches perfectly visible. Noisy emissions can
only make discrimination harder. It is not an exact minimax sample count for
the observation-only experiment.

The shortest horizon with any observable difference is 2: at time 2 the
Bernoulli means differ by lambda/2. The horizon needed for a fixed contrast
c in the marginal is determined by (1/2)[1-(1-lambda)^(h-1)] >= c, or

  h >= 1 + log(1-2c)/log(1-lambda),   0 < c < 1/2.

This is order 1/lambda for fixed c. At lambda=T^-2, a length-T sample is
short by order T^2 in prediction horizon. An oracle that reports the hidden
transition row distinguishes lambda=0 from lambda>0 immediately. If access
permits one-step transition trials from state 0 and exposes the next hidden
state, this removes the long rollout delay, but a transition under H_1 still
occurs with probability lambda per trial, so order 1/lambda trials are needed
to witness it with constant probability. Merely resetting to state 1 does not
distinguish the pair because the state-1 emission law is shared.

3. What representation supply changes
--------------------------------------
For the two-state example, supplying the correct state labels tells a learner
what a switch means, but it cannot create a switch in the data. With labeled
states, H_0 and H_1 are distinguishable only if a transition is seen; the
probability no transition occurs in n trajectories is exactly
(1-lambda)^(n(T-1)). The length-T lower bound above therefore remains of order
1/(T lambda), even for this supplied representation. More exactly, under
equal priors the state-labeled experiment has Bayes error exactly
(1/2)(1-lambda)^(n(T-1)): if any transition is labeled, H_1 is known; with no
transition, the data coincide with H_0. Supplying lambda itself, or an oracle
for the transition row, gives away the quantity that determines the answer
and is a different interface. A supplied feature map or state basis alone
does not bypass the horizon/support limitation.

There is a separate structure-search cost when the future predictive location
is unknown. For M >= 2, consider a clock with states c_1,...,c_{T+M},a and
the same deterministic path as above. In model H_j, only c_{T+j} emits
Bernoulli(3/4); all other states emit fair bits. Every length-T law is the
same. The post-T observation window has one biased coordinate, whose unknown
index j is the predictive structure. For j != k, the full window laws have
TV at least 1/4, since their marginal at coordinate j differs by 1/4.

Let Q be the all-fair product law on that window. For each H_j,

  KL(P_j || Q) = d0
    = (3/4)log(3/2) + (1/4)log(1/2)
    ~= 0.130812 nats.

For n independent complete windows and uniform J in {1,...,M}, the mutual
information is at most n d0 (by the KL-to-reference identity). Fano's
inequality therefore gives, for any decoder of J,

  P(error) >= 1 - (n d0 + log 2)/log M,

and error at most alpha requires

  n >= ((1-alpha)log M - log 2)/d0.

This is Omega(log M) noisy trajectories for fixed alpha, even though each
trajectory observes all M candidate coordinates. Supplying the correct
predictive coordinate j removes this index-search term; estimating an
unprovided Bernoulli coefficient at that coordinate still requires samples,
and length-T prefixes still contain none of them. Supplying only the clock
basis, without identifying j, does not remove the Fano bound.

Interpretation and limits
-------------------------
These examples isolate three distinct barriers: (i) exact unidentifiability
under a hard observation cutoff; (ii) a rare-event/sample-horizon tradeoff
even with two hidden states and noisy emissions; and (iii) a log-cardinality
cost to acquiring which future feature matters. They do not show that
teacher-free learning is impossible when trajectories cover the relevant
states, when the HMM has a known mixing bound, or when active state/transition
queries are supplied. Representation-supplied learning can eliminate
structure search, but it cannot infer an unobserved emission or transition
from identical data unless the representation includes that value or adds an
informative query channel.
