## Exact literature interface

For a mass-action reaction (y\to y+\nu_r) with rate (\kappa_r x^y), the deterministic drift and Langevin covariance are

\[
A(x)=\sum_r \kappa_r x^{y_r}\nu_r,\qquad
B(x)=\sum_r \kappa_r x^{y_r}\nu_r\nu_r^\top,
\]

and the diffusion generator is \(\mathcal L f=A\cdot\nabla f+\tfrac12 B:\nabla^2 f\). Thus each source monomial \(x^y\) exposes only the rate-weighted sum of lifted signatures \((\nu_r,\nu_r\nu_r^\top)\).

Faul, Hoessly, and Xia’s 2026 paper proves (Theorem 3.3(1)) that, for a fixed network and positive rate constants, rate identifiability from the full SDE law is equivalent to linear independence of those lifted vectors separately at every source complex. Its Remark 3.5 gives the coefficient interface. The proof proceeds by equating generators, expanding drift and covariance in monomials, and using linear independence of distinct monomials to obtain a separate lifted-vector equation at each source. Linear independence forces every rate difference to vanish; conversely, a nonzero linear dependence can be scaled around a strictly positive rate vector to give two distinct positive rate vectors with the same generator. The hypotheses are mass-action Langevin approximation, the full known species set, and ideal structural information; the definition compares laws for all positive initial states in bounded open subsets of the positive orthant, stopped at the boundary. The paper says partial observation and discrete noisy data remain open in its conclusion.

This is a fixed-network theorem; it supplies the second-order signature map used below. It is not a finite noisy-data sample-complexity result and does not identify an unknown reaction graph from partial observations.

## Reconstructed catalytic example

Let \(C\) denote a preformed enzyme–substrate intermediate containing one enzyme and four substrate units, \(C=E:S_4\). Order state coordinates as \((C,E,S,P)\). Consider four possible product-release channels indexed by product yield \(k\in\{1,2,3,4\}\):

| Yield \(k\) | Reaction | Stoichiometric change \(\nu_k\) | Network A rate | Network B rate |
|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | \(C\to E+3S+P\) | \((-1,1,3,1)\) | 1 | absent |
| 2 | \(C\to E+2S+2P\) | \((-1,1,2,2)\) | 1 | 4 |
| 3 | \(C\to E+S+3P\) | \((-1,1,1,3)\) | 4 | 1 |
| 4 | \(C\to E+4P\) | \((-1,1,0,4)\) | absent | 1 |

Both networks use first-order mass action in \(C\). They preserve enzyme amount \(C+E\) and substrate moiety \(4C+S+P\). They share the same species and the single source complex \(C\), but have different reaction graphs. All displayed rates are positive for reactions present in that network.

Write \(\nu_k=b+ku\), where \(b=(-1,1,4,0)\) and \(u=(0,0,-1,1)\). The signed rate difference A minus B across yields \(k=1,2,3,4\) is \(\alpha=(1,-3,3,-1)\). It annihilates the first three power moments:

\[
\sum_k\alpha_k=0,\quad \sum_k k\alpha_k=0,\quad \sum_k k^2\alpha_k=0,
\qquad \sum_k k^3\alpha_k=-6.
\]

Equivalently, each network has \(M_0=\sum\kappa_k=6\), \(M_1=\sum k\kappa_k=15\), and \(M_2=\sum k^2\kappa_k=41\), while \(M_3\) is 117 for A and 123 for B. Since

\[
\sum_k\kappa_k\nu_k=M_0b+M_1u,
\quad
\sum_k\kappa_k\nu_k\nu_k^\top
=M_0bb^\top+M_1(bu^\top+ub^\top)+M_2uu^\top,
\]

the full drift and covariance agree identically for every state \(x\), not just along the assay trajectory. The exact common fields are \(A(x)=x_C(-6,6,9,15)\) and

\[
B(x)=x_C\begin{pmatrix}
6&-6&-9&-15\\-6&6&9&15\\-9&9&17&19\\-15&15&19&41
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

For an explicit factorization, put \(v_1=\sqrt 6\,b+(15/\sqrt 6)u\) and \(v_2=\sqrt{7/2}\,u\). Then \(B(x)=x_C(v_1v_1^\top+v_2v_2^\top)\). The corresponding Langevin system has noise \(\sqrt{C}(v_1dW_1+v_2dW_2)\); its \(C\)-coordinate is the CIR equation \(dC=-6C\,dt-\sqrt{6C}\,dW_1\), which has a unique nonnegative solution from \(C\ge0\). The remaining coordinates are explicit integrals driven by the same Brownian motions. Thus the full Langevin laws coincide from the assay initialization; more generally they coincide for common initial conditions on the mathematical domain \(C\ge0\). The shared first moment also makes the ODE baseline identical.

The exact CTMC sees more. Its generator is \(Qf(x)=x_C\sum_k\kappa_k[f(x+\nu_k)-f(x)]\). Start one assay at \(C=1,E=S=P=0\). One reaction occurs and releases \(E+(4-k)S+kP\); the resulting state is absorbing. The reachable state space has five states: the initial intermediate and four product outcomes. The total event rate is 6 under either network, so the waiting time is \(\mathrm{Exp}(6)\) for both. Every completed turnover produces at least one \(P\), so the product count alone also signals that the event occurred. The endpoint product yield has distributions

\[
P_A(k=1,2,3,4)=(1,1,4,0)/6,\qquad
P_B(k=1,2,3,4)=(0,4,1,1)/6.
\]

This is a partial-observation separation: measure only the integer product count after turnover; no intermediate trajectory or reaction identity is required. The first and second jump moments agree, while \(\sum_k\kappa_k k^3\) is 117 for A and 123 for B. At the initial state the CTMC generators applied to \(f(C,E,S,P)=P^3\) therefore give 117 and 123. Conditional on a completed turnover, both yield laws have mean \(5/2\) and variance \(7/12\), while their third cumulants are \(-1/2\) for A and \(+1/2\) for B. The holding-time law also agrees. Since every completed event yields \(P>0\), an assay may either stop at product appearance or use a fixed horizon and classify \(P=0\) as right-censored. For five parallel assays, \(\Pr(\text{at least one censored by }T)\le 5e^{-6T}\); choosing \(T\ge \log(500)/6\approx1.036\) time units makes this batch-censoring bound at most 1%. If assays run in parallel to completion, their expected maximum completion time is \(H_5/6=137/360\approx0.381\) time units.

## Finite experiment, cost, and comparator

The protocol is to prepare five independent copies of \(C\), allow one turnover per copy, and record product yield \(k\in\{1,2,3,4\}\). Under the fixed candidate rates above, the exact CTMC likelihood ratio is optimal for these two simple hypotheses. It selects A if any yield 1 occurs; B if any yield 4 occurs; if all yields are 2 or 3, it selects A when the count of 3s exceeds the count of 2s and B when the reverse holds. A tie can be randomized; for five trials, ties are impossible.

Under A, an error requires no yield-1 outcome and at least three yield-2 outcomes. Its exact probability is

\[
\sum_{j=3}^{5}\binom{5}{j}(1/6)^j(4/6)^{5-j}=181/7776\approx 0.02328.
\]

By symmetry the same holds under B, so the sum of the two error probabilities is \(181/3888\approx0.04655\). Exact enumeration gives a minimum combined error of \(65/648\approx0.10031\) at four trials with randomized likelihood ties. Since the likelihood-ratio test minimizes the combined error for the two simple hypotheses and any test with fewer samples can ignore extra samples, no count below five meets the 5% target. This is an exact model-based count, not experimental evidence.

The measurement requirement is integer product-yield resolution from 1 through 4 with negligible misclassification; observing \(P=0\) at a finite horizon means the turnover is censored, not that the reaction produced zero product. Turnover timing alone has no information because both total hazards are 6. The stated five-trial cost assumes five correctly prepared starting complexes; the yield and cost of preparing/purifying \(C\), concentration calibration, background reactions, and detector error are unpriced and must be measured for a real assay. With a calibrated outcome-confusion matrix, replace the probabilities above by their measured push-forward and recompute the likelihood test; the five-trial guarantee does not survive arbitrary readout error.

The strongest equally informed comparator is the exact CTMC likelihood-ratio test on these same outcome labels; the proposed rule is that optimal test, not an advantage over it. Deterministic ODE fitting and the full Langevin likelihood are weaker controls here: both assign identical laws to A and B and therefore have zero discrimination power, even under perfect noiseless data. A decisive failure observation for the closed two-model assay is seeing both a yield-1 and a yield-4 outcome in the same batch, which has probability zero under either candidate; less dramatic yield-frequency departures should be assessed with a predeclared multinomial goodness-of-fit test and a larger replicate count.
