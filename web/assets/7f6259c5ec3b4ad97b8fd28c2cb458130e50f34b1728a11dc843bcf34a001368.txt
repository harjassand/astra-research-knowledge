## 2. Strongest complete theorem: one invariant controls the linear approaches

Let \(P\in\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\) be nonzero. Define its **total derivative space**

\[
W_P=\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb C}
\{\partial^\alpha P:\alpha\in\mathbb N^n\},
\qquad
r(P)=\dim W_P.
\]

Equivalently, \(r(P)\) is the dimension of the quotient of constant-coefficient differential operators by those annihilating \(P\), the associated apolar algebra. This terminology and viewpoint are classical. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.04818)

Set

\[
\ell_i(z)=\log(1+z/i),
\qquad
\ell_i'(z)=\frac1{z+i}.
\]

### Exact realization theorem

The following quantities are equal:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
r(P)
&=\text{Hankel rank of the shuffle lift of }P\\
&=\text{minimum linear weighted-automaton dimension of that lift}\\
&=\text{dimension of the span of the analytic branches of }P(\ell(z))\\
&=\text{minimum homogeneous rational linear ODE order for }P(\ell(z))\\
&=\text{minimum rational linear-system dimension}\\
&\hspace{1.3cm}\text{with a rational \emph{linear} readout representing }P(\ell(z)).
\end{aligned}}
\]

Cyclic realizations, rational changes of basis, and rational linear output coefficients do not circumvent the minimum.

Here is the essential proof.

### A. Monodromy turns analytic continuation into translation

A loop around \(-i\) adds \(2\pi\mathrm i\) to \(\ell_i\) and leaves the other logarithms unchanged. Consequently, continuation produces all functions

\[
P(\ell(z)+2\pi\mathrm i\,k),
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z^n.
\]

Taylor’s formula gives

\[
P(x+a)=
\sum_\alpha \frac{a^\alpha}{\alpha!}\partial^\alpha P(x).
\]

All translates therefore lie in \(W_P\). Conversely, finite interpolation in the translation parameters recovers every derivative from a sufficiently large grid of translates. Thus their span is exactly \(W_P\).

Evaluation at the logarithms is injective: a polynomial relation could be continued around all loops, producing a polynomial vanishing on the entire integer lattice of translations. Such a polynomial must be zero. The argument also accommodates rational functions of \(z\) as coefficients.

The broader independence theory of hyperlogarithms predates this investigation; the required special case is proved directly in the report. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1101.4497)

### B. Every rational linear realization must contain the branch span

An order-\(m\) homogeneous rational differential equation has an \(m\)-dimensional local solution space at an ordinary point. All analytic continuations of a solution remain in that space. Therefore

\[
m\ge r(P).
\]

For an \(m\)-dimensional rational linear system with rational linear readout, all continued outputs likewise lie in the span of \(m\) scalar outputs of a fundamental matrix. The same lower bound follows.

For the matching upper bound, choose a basis \(Q_1,\ldots,Q_r\) of \(W_P\). Constant matrices \(C_i\) satisfy

\[
\partial_i Q=C_iQ.
\]

The vector \(y(z)=Q(\ell(z))\) then obeys

\[
y'(z)=
\left(\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{C_i}{z+i}\right)y(z).
\]

This is an \(r(P)\)-dimensional rational system. Eliminating between its first \(r(P)+1\) derivative rows gives a scalar equation of order at most \(r(P)\). The lower bound makes the minimum exact.

### C. The same invariant appears in the coefficient Hankel matrix

Write \(P=\sum_\alpha c_\alpha x^\alpha\). Define its raw shuffle lift by assigning every word of multidegree \(\alpha\) the coefficient

\[
\alpha!\,c_\alpha.
\]

For prefix and suffix multidegrees \(\beta,\gamma\), the Hankel entry is

\[
(\beta+\gamma)!\,c_{\beta+\gamma}.
\]

After dividing column \(\gamma\) by \(\gamma!\), this is precisely the coefficient of \(x^\gamma\) in \(\partial^\beta P\). Hence the Hankel rank equals \(r(P)\).

Every \(m\)-state linear realization factors that Hankel matrix through an \(m\)-dimensional space. Conversely, \(W_P\), with transitions given by partial differentiation and output given by evaluation at zero, supplies an \(r(P)\)-state realization.

This completes the characterization.

## 3. What this proves—and what it does not prove

For the simple polynomial

\[
P_n=x_1x_2\cdots x_n,
\]

the derivatives are all \(2^n\) squarefree submonomials. Therefore

\[
r(P_n)=2^n.
\]

But \(P_n\) has a formula with only \(2n-1\) gates when leaves and multiplication gates are counted.

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Linear-size commutative formulas can require exponentially large
faithful logarithmic linear realizations.}
}
\]

This rules out a proposed route that first constructs a polynomial-dimensional linear realization for every small commutative circuit and then applies the differential-system multiplicity estimate.

**It does not rule out efficient direct identity testing.** Indeed,

\[
\operatorname{ord}_0\prod_{i=1}^n\ell_i(z)=n,
\]

despite the minimum linear ODE order being \(2^n\).

That distinction is decisive:

\[
\text{large linear-representation dimension}
\quad\not\Rightarrow\quad
\text{large vanishing order}.
\]

Moreover, the logarithms themselves fit into an \((n+1)\)-dimensional rational linear system using the state vector \((1,\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_n)\). The output \(P(\ell)\) is then **nonlinear**. The obstruction is to linear readouts and linear representations—not to storing the logarithms or evaluating the original circuit.

After establishing this obstruction, the campaign shifted to direct vanishing-order bounds rather than attempting progressively smaller linear realizations.

## 4. Adversarial tests of alternative transfers

### Symmetrization remains exponentially expensive under approximation

Consider the normalized symmetric lift

\[
S_n=\frac1{n!}
\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak S_n}
x_{\pi(1)}\cdots x_{\pi(n)}.
\]

Its commutative image is \(x_1\cdots x_n\).

Index Hankel rows by increasing words \(u_A\) on subsets \(A\subseteq[n]\), and columns by increasing words \(v_B\) on complements of subsets \(B\). Then

\[
[u_Av_B]S_n=\frac1{n!}\mathbf 1_{A=B}.
\]

This is a \(2^n\times2^n\) diagonal minor. A subset automaton attains the matching upper bound, so the exact minimum is \(2^n\).

There is also a robust version. At a prefix–suffix cut of lengths \(k,n-k\), the coefficient matrix has

\[
\binom nk
\]

equal nonzero singular values. A rank-\(m\) approximation cannot capture more than \(m\) of these equal singular directions. Consequently, relative Euclidean coefficient error at most \(0\le\varepsilon<1\) requires

\[
\boxed{
m\ge
(1-\varepsilon^2)
\binom n{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}.
}
\]

Constant relative error therefore does not restore polynomial linear-state complexity.

The exact \(2^n\) statement concerns the entire series. The approximation statement concerns degree-\(n\) coefficients and gives the central-binomial bound. These are not lower bounds for arbitrary commutative circuits.

### Sorting words also fails on an exact family

For a fixed order placing all \(x_i\) before all \(y_i\), take

\[
P_n=\prod_{i=1}^n(x_i+y_i).
\]

Sorting each monomial produces

\[
Q_n=
\sum_{A\subseteq[n]}
x_Ay_{[n]\setminus A}.
\]

Its Hankel submatrix indexed by \(x_A\) and \(y_{[n]\setminus B}\) is the \(2^n\)-dimensional identity.

Thus fixed-order sorting is another exponentially costly linearization of a linear-size formula. This does not exclude a different, circuit-dependent asymmetric transformation.

### Rational functions of the logarithms do not automatically remain D-finite

A second complete statement is:

\[
\boxed{
R(\ell_1(z),\ldots,\ell_n(z))
\text{ is D-finite over }\mathbb C(z)
\iff
R\in\mathbb C[x_1,\ldots,x_n],
}
\]

for a reduced rational function \(R\in\mathbb C(x_1,\ldots,x_n)\).

Here *D-finite* means satisfying a nonzero homogeneous linear differential equation with rational coefficients.

The polynomial direction follows from the realization theorem. Conversely, D-finiteness would force the translates \(R(x+2\pi\mathrm i\,k)\) to span a finite-dimensional space. Their poles would then lie in a fixed finite union of hypersurfaces. Integer translations would permute those hypersurfaces, forcing each to be periodic under a nonzero translation in every coordinate. A nonconstant polynomial cannot have those additive periods in characteristic zero.

This is a classification within the chosen logarithmic representation, **not** a general impossibility theorem for rational-function PIT.

The report also proves a restricted obstruction to amplifying contact by ramified rational substitutions while preserving the same logarithmic alphabet. None of these obstructions settles the foundational target.

## 5. A positive theorem was reconstructed—but the novelty check disqualified it

The differential-system estimate needed by this route is the following bound, attributed to Moura:

\[
\operatorname{ord}_0 f
\le
(n-1)\binom m2+m-1
\]

for a nonzero scalar output of an \(m\)-dimensional system whose coefficient matrix has denominator degree \(n\) and numerator degrees at most \(n-1\), regular at the base point. I reconstructed the supplied derivative-row, minor, and Wronskian proof. 

Combining it with the realization theorem gives an explicit scalar generator for polynomials with \(r(P)\le r\). Set

\[
L=(n-1)\binom r2+r,
\qquad
\phi_{i,L}(z)=
\sum_{j=1}^{L-1}
\frac{(-1)^{j-1}z^j}{j\,i^j}.
\]

Then every nonzero polynomial satisfying that derivative-space promise remains nonzero after substitution \(x_i=\phi_{i,L}(z)\). For degree at most \(D\), evaluating at

\[
z=0,1,\ldots,D(L-1)
\]

gives a polynomial-size rational hitting set.

**This is not a new capability from this investigation.** Bhardwaj and Saptharishi’s *Hitting Sets for Polynomials with Small Partial Derivative Spaces*, submitted September 28, 2026, already proves polynomial-size hitting sets for precisely this class, including depth-three powering circuits, using truncated logarithms and a related derivation/Wronskian mechanism. Different pole choices or a sharper displayed truncation constant do not establish foundational novelty. [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34436)

I also checked whether that restricted theorem could be inserted into known PIT bootstrapping results. The relevant tiny-circuit hypotheses include models allowing powers of nonlinear polynomials, not merely powers of linear forms. I did not establish the required stronger hypothesis or a valid bootstrap to general circuits. [ECCC](https://eccc.weizmann.ac.il/report/2017/035/)

## 6. The precise remaining foundational obligation

The surviving route does **not** require a small derivative space.

It asks whether there is an explicit, uniform polynomial \(B(n,s,D)\) such that every nonzero degree-at-most-\(D\) polynomial computed by a commutative circuit of size at most \(s\) satisfies

\[
\boxed{
\operatorname{ord}_0
P\!\left(
\log(1+z),
\log(1+z/2),
\ldots,
\log(1+z/n)
\right)
<
B(n,s,D).
}
\tag{*}
\]

### What would follow from \((*)\)

The reduction is complete.

Use \(L=B(n,s,D)\) in the truncated-logarithm generator above. Truncation preserves the composition modulo \(z^L\), so the resulting univariate polynomial is nonzero. Its degree is at most \(D(L-1)\). Therefore

\[
\left\{
\bigl(\phi_{1,L}(t),\ldots,\phi_{n,L}(t)\bigr):
t=0,\ldots,D(L-1)
\right\}
\]

hits every circuit in the target class.

The set has polynomial cardinality, its rational coordinates have polynomial bit length, and its generation takes polynomial bit time. No matrix-evaluation oracle or infinite-precision logarithm oracle is needed. Exact circuit evaluation or black-box answer costs remain separate from generator construction.

**Neither a proof nor a counterexample to \((*)\) was obtained.** It is a sufficient condition for the selected PIT target, not a demonstrated equivalence with every possible PIT algorithm.

### Why the missing bound cannot ignore circuit complexity

There are

\[
M=\binom{n+D}{D}
\]

coefficients in a general degree-at-most-\(D\) polynomial. Requiring the first \(M-1\) Taylor coefficients of \(P(\ell(z))\) to vanish imposes only \(M-1\) homogeneous linear constraints. Hence some nonzero \(P\) satisfies

\[
\operatorname{ord}_0P(\ell(z))\ge M-1.
\]

Algebraic independence ensures the composition is not identically zero.

Thus a general polynomial bound in \(n,D\) alone is impossible. The unproved possibility is a bound that exploits **small circuit size**.

### Exact experiments did not settle the asymptotic question

The delivered integer fixtures have contact orders

\[
1,\ 3,\ 7,\ 15
\]

for \(n=1,2,3,4\). For example,

\[
P_2=x_1x_2+2x_1-4x_2
\]

has logarithmic contact order \(3\), despite having polynomial degree \(2\).

These examples demonstrate substantial cancellation. They do **not** give a uniform polynomial-size circuit family with superpolynomial contact, and I have not extrapolated such a family from four cases.

The unresolved task is therefore concrete: control contact under circuit operations without expanding the entire derivative closure, or construct an efficiently represented family violating \((*)\).
