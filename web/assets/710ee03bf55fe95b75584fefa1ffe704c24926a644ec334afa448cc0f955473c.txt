T04 CONVEX GEOMETRY — FROZEN PHASE-I NOTE v1
Date: 2026-10-09
Status: internally derived stochastic identities and exact model checks; sharp universal inequality unresolved.

1. Exact target and landscape

For a full-dimensional log-concave probability measure μ on R^n, let

  C_P(μ) = sup_f Var_μ(f) / ∫ |∇f|² dμ,

where f ranges over locally Lipschitz functions with finite positive Dirichlet energy. The covariance-scaled KLS inequality with the proposed sharp constant is

  C_P(μ) ≤ 4 ||Cov(μ)||_op.                                      (1)

It is affine invariant: after sending X to Cov(μ)^(-1/2)(X−E X), (1) is exactly C_P(μ)≤4 for isotropic μ. The number 4 cannot be lowered. Section 2 proves this from a product of one-sided exponentials. The question whether (1) holds for every log-concave μ is unresolved here. A dimension-free KLS theorem, even if established, would not by itself settle the sharp constant.

Live primary-source check, used only to describe claims as reported: Song and Zhang, “An O(1) Bound for the KLS Constant,” arXiv:2610.01447v2, submitted 2026-10-01 and revised 2026-10-04, states a universal Cheeger and Poincaré bound in its abstract. Bizeul, Klartag, and Lehec, “Presenting a proof of the Kannan-Lovász-Simonovits conjecture,” arXiv:2610.05474v1, submitted 2026-10-04, states a proof using Song–Zhang’s high-order tilt-average criterion. Balasubramanian and Kasiviswanathan, “A Dimension-Free Bound on the Poincaré Constant of Isotropic Log-Concave Measures,” arXiv:2610.07728v2, revised 2026-10-08, states C_P(μ)≤25. These are author preprints and source-reported claims; this note does not independently validate them, infer priority, or infer the exact value of the sharp KLS constant. The last paper’s introduction also describes the two concurrent KLS claims. URLs and accessed locations are listed in Section 6.

The standard KLS conjecture asks for some universal constant; it does not specify 4. The bound C_P≤4 is the sharpened conjecture proposed here from the exact exponential-product lower benchmark. The cited primary-source abstracts state larger or unspecified universal constants, not this value. This source check does not establish that no earlier paper proposed the sharp value 4.

2. Sharp lower benchmark and asymmetric one-dimensional check

Let Y have density e^(−y) on [0,∞). Its mean and variance are both 1. For smooth f, the unitary map g(y)=e^(−y/2)f(y) sends L²(e^(−y)dy) to L²(dy), and sends the Dirichlet form to

  ∫_0^∞ |f'|² e^(−y)dy
    = ∫_0^∞ (|g'|² + |g|²/4)dy − |g(0)|²/2.                    (2)

The associated Schrödinger operator is −d²/dy²+1/4 on the half-line with Robin boundary condition g'(0)=−g(0)/2. It has the ground state g_0(y)=e^(−y/2), corresponding to constant f and eigenvalue 0. The continuous spectrum starts at 1/4. For any eigenvalue λ<1/4, an L² solution must be Ce^(−κy), κ=√(1/4−λ); the boundary condition forces κ=1/2, hence λ=0. Thus the first nonzero spectral value is 1/4 and C_P(Y)=4.

The threshold is not attained by a nonconstant L² eigenfunction. To see that it is the spectral gap, take a nonzero smooth φ supported in (0,1), let g_L(y)=L^(−1/2)φ((y−L)/L), and subtract its projection onto g_0. The support is away from 0, the projection is exponentially small, and the Rayleigh quotient in (2) tends to 1/4 because ||g'_L||²/||g_L||²=O(L^(−2)).

The centered variable X=Y−1 has density e^(−(x+1))1{x≥−1}, mean 0, and variance 1. The product μ_n=Law(X)^(⊗n) is isotropic and log-concave. Tensorization gives C_P(μ_n)≤4, while test functions depending on one coordinate and the preceding approximate spectral sequence give C_P(μ_n)≥4. Therefore C_P(μ_n)=4 for every n. This proves the sharpness obstruction for (1), not its upper bound for general measures.

An explicit asymmetric two-sided test does not exceed 4. For a,b>0, take

  p_(a,b)(x) = ab/(a+b) · { exp(−a x), x≥0;
                            exp( b x), x<0 }.

Its mean is 1/a−1/b and its variance is 1/a²+1/b². Write p=Z^(−1)e^(−V). The unitary conjugate of −(d²/dx²−V'd/dx) is the one-dimensional Schrödinger operator

  −d²/dx² + (V')²/4 − V''/2,

whose potential is b²/4 on the negative half-line, a²/4 on the positive half-line, and the point interaction −(a+b)δ_0/2. Below the essential-spectrum threshold min(a²,b²)/4, an L² eigenfunction has the form C exp(k_b x) on x<0 and C exp(−k_a x) on x>0, with k_b=√(b²/4−λ), k_a=√(a²/4−λ). The derivative jump at zero requires k_a+k_b=(a+b)/2. At λ=0 this is the ground state. For 0<λ<min(a²,b²)/4 the left side is strictly smaller, so no positive eigenvalue lies below the threshold. Hence C_P(p_(a,b))=4/min(a,b)².

After isotropic scaling, its Poincaré constant is

  4 / (min(a,b)² (a^(−2)+b^(−2))) ≤ 4.

For a≥b this equals 4/(1+(b/a)²), approaching 4 only in the one-sided exponential limit. The symmetric case a=b has value 2. Products of these independently scaled coordinates tensorize, again with maximum coordinate constant at most 4. This family is a useful asymmetric extremal check; it is not evidence that arbitrary log-concave measures satisfy (1).

3. Gaussian-observation localization: exact identities

The following derivation is for a compactly supported, full-dimensional log-concave μ with mean zero and covariance I, and initially smooth bounded f. Approximation and integrability conditions are separated below.

Let X∼μ and observe Y_t=tX+B_t, where B is standard Brownian motion independent of X. Set F_t=σ(Y_s:0≤s≤t). Bayes’ formula gives the posterior

  μ_t(dx) = Z(t,Y_t)^(−1) exp(Y_t·x − t|x|²/2) μ(dx),
  Z(t,c)   = ∫ exp(c·x − t|x|²/2) μ(dx).

Write ψ(t,c)=log Z(t,c), a_t=E_{μ_t}X=∇_cψ(t,Y_t), A_t=Cov_{μ_t}(X)=D²_cψ(t,Y_t), and H_t=D³_cψ(t,Y_t). Let W_t=Y_t−∫_0^t a_s ds be the innovation Brownian motion. In this filtration dY_t=dW_t+a_tdt.

For u(t,c)=E_{μ_(t,c)}f, direct differentiation gives

  ∇_c u = c_t(f) := Cov_{μ_(t,c)}(f,X),
  ∂_t u = −(1/2)Cov_{μ_(t,c)}(f,|X|²),
  Δ_c u = Cov_{μ_(t,c)}(f,|X|²) − 2a·c_t(f).

Consequently ∂_t u+(1/2)Δ_cu+a·∇_cu=0. Itô’s formula along Y_t yields the exact martingale identity

  dF_t = c_t(f)·dW_t.                                           (3)

Since Y_t/t=X+B_t/t and B_t/t→0, the observation reveals X as t→∞. Martingale convergence and Itô isometry therefore give

  Var_μ(f) = E ∫_0^∞ |c_t(f)|² dt.                              (4)

The same calculation for f(x)=x gives

  da_t = A_t dW_t.                                               (5)

The log partition function obeys

  ∂_tψ = −(1/2)(|∇ψ|²+Δψ).

Differentiating twice and applying Itô along Y_t, with H_{ijk}=E_{μ_t}[(X_i−a_i)(X_j−a_j)(X_k−a_k)], gives

  dA_t = Σ_k H_{k,t} dW_{t,k} − A_t²dt,                         (6)

where (H_k)_{ij}=H_{ijk}. The drift cancellation is exact: the PDE for A is ∂_tA=−A²−(a·∇)A−(1/2)ΔA, while Y has drift a and quadratic variation I. Equations (5)–(6) imply

  d⟨a⟩_t = A_t²dt,
  d tr(A_t) = Σ_k tr(H_{k,t})dW_{t,k} − tr(A_t²)dt.             (7)

As a_t=E[X|F_t] and a_∞=X, the matrix martingale isometry gives

  E∫_0^∞ A_t²dt = Cov(X)=I.                                     (8)

In particular every fixed direction u has E∫|A_tu|²dt=|u|². For f(x)=u·x, c_t(f)=A_tu and (4) reduces exactly to (8). This is a dimension-free identity for each fixed direction. It does not control a direction that adapts to the observation path or to ∇f(X).

The Frobenius trace has a different scale. From (7), A_t→0, and monotone convergence,

  E∫_0^∞ tr(A_t²)dt = tr(A_0)=n.                               (9)

Thus a scalar trace bound loses dimension. Also, Itô applied to tr(A²) yields

  d tr(A_t²) = 2Σ_k tr(A_tH_{k,t})dW_{t,k}
       + [Σ_k tr(H_{k,t}²) − 2tr(A_t³)]dt.                    (10)

The tempting supermartingale condition Σ_k tr(H_k²)≤2tr(A³) fails at time zero for the isotropic one-sided exponential product. There A_0=I and H_{k,0}=2e_ke_kᵀ, so Σ_k tr(H_k²)=4n while 2tr(A³)=2n. The drift in (10) is +2n. The exact model therefore rules out this simple covariance-only Lyapunov function.

4. Tilt-slope and Schur-complement energy

For the same f, define K_t as the Hessian in c of u(t,c) at c=Y_t:

  (K_t)_{ij} = E_{μ_t}[(f−F_t)(X_i−a_i)(X_j−a_j)].

Differentiating the PDE for u once gives

  dc_t(f) = K_t dW_t − A_t c_t(f)dt.                            (11)

For t>0, A_t is positive definite on the affine support. Let

  b_t = A_t^(−1)c_t(f),   (σ_t)_j = A_t^(−1)(K_{t,j}−H_{t,j}b_t),

where K_j and H_j are the jth columns/matrices in the natural tensor contraction. Since b is a function of the natural parameter c, σ_j=∂_{c_j}b. Comparing diffusion terms in c=Ab using (6) and (11), then comparing drifts, gives

  db_t = Σ_j σ_{t,j}dW_{t,j}
           − A_t^(−1)Σ_j H_{t,j}σ_{t,j}dt.                     (12)

The drift is a non-Gaussian correction. In the Gaussian case H=0 and b_t=E_{μ_t}∇f, so b is a martingale. For affine f, K_j=H_jb, σ=0 and b is constant under every μ.

There is a second exact energy identity. The block covariance matrix of (f,X) under μ_t is positive semidefinite, so

  R_t := Var_{μ_t}(f) − c_t(f)ᵀA_t^(−1)c_t(f) ≥ 0.             (13)

The first term has drift −|c_t(f)|². Applying Itô to bᵀAb in (6),(12), the two terms involving bᵀH_jσ_j cancel, leaving drift −|A_tb_t|²+Σ_jσ_{t,j}ᵀA_tσ_{t,j}. Subtracting gives

  dR_t = martingale − Σ_j σ_{t,j}ᵀA_tσ_{t,j}dt
       = martingale − ||A_t^(1/2)D_cb_t||_HS²dt.              (14)

Thus R_t is a nonnegative local supermartingale and its expected finite-variation loss is exactly the slope energy. Since 0≤R_t≤Var_{μ_t}(f) and E Var_{μ_t}(f)=E(f−F_t)²→0 by martingale convergence, E R_t→0. For bounded f, localization on finite time intervals gives

  Var_μ(f)
    = Cov_μ(f,X)ᵀ Cov(X)^(−1) Cov_μ(f,X)
      + E∫_0^∞ ||A_t^(1/2)D_cb_t||_HS²dt.                    (15)

The terminal limit does not require b_t itself to converge: b_tᵀA_tb_t≤Var_{μ_t}(f) is enough.

For isotropic μ the first term is |Cov_μ(f,X)|². It is the variance of the orthogonal linear regression of f onto X. The second term is the residual variance, expressed as energy of the changing regression slope along the Gaussian observation path. This is an exact decomposition, not a bound.

The sharp target (1) would follow from the concrete transport estimate

  |Cov_μ(f,X)|² + E∫_0^∞ ||A_t^(1/2)D_cb_t||_HS²dt
       ≤ 4 E_μ|∇f|².                                           (16)

For nonisotropic μ, replace the first term by Cov(f,X)ᵀΣ^(−1)Cov(f,X), and use a whitened observation metric. The missing part is a dimension-free gradient comparison for the tilt-slope energy in (15). Equation (15) alone does not control either summand by the Dirichlet energy, so it does not establish (1).

5. Gradient comparison attempt and exact obstruction

For any posterior μ_t admitting a symmetric Stein kernel τ_t, meaning

  Cov_{μ_t}(g,X)=E_{μ_t}[τ_t(X)∇g(X)],

we have E_{μ_t}τ_t=A_t and

  b_t−E_{μ_t}∇f
    = A_t^(−1) E_{μ_t}[(τ_t−A_t)∇f].                           (17)

This identifies the precise Stein/score defect between the tilt-regression slope and the conditional mean gradient. For a Gaussian posterior τ_t=A_t, the defect vanishes, b_t=E_t∇f, and (12) is a martingale. For general log-concave μ_t, no dimension-free estimate on the integrated defect, or on the weighted energy in (15), has been derived here. A pointwise bound on ||τ_t||_op is not available even at t=0 for the exponential example: in the centered one-sided exponential, τ_0(x)=x+1 is unbounded. An integrated or gradient-aligned estimate could still hold; this observation only rules out a uniform pointwise Stein-kernel cap.

At t=0 for μ_n=Law(Y−1)^(⊗n), formula (17) becomes

  b_0 = E[(Y_1,…,Y_n) ⊙ ∇f(X)]
      = E∇f(X) + E[X ⊙ ∇f(X)].

For every fixed unit vector v,

  |E Σ_i v_iX_i∂_if|²
    ≤ E Σ_i v_i²X_i² · E Σ_i|∂_if|²
    = E|∇f|²,

so the initial Stein defect itself is dimension-free in this product model. This is compatible with C_P=4, but does not control the pathwise integral in (15). In particular, replacing (15) by an estimate on sup_t||A_t||_op or sup_t||τ_t||_op would discard the direction-by-direction structure and is not justified by (8).

For a Gaussian μ=N(0,I), A_t=(1+t)^(−1)I and H_t=0. Gaussian integration by parts gives b_t=E_{μ_t}∇f, and (15) is the usual Gaussian decomposition into first chaos and higher-order energy. The standard Gaussian has C_P=1, so the proposed sharp constant 4 is loose there. For the one-sided exponential product, the exact sharp value is 4, but no direct estimate of the integral in (15) was obtained. The asymmetric two-sided Laplace calculation in Section 2 approaches 4 only as one tail disappears; it gives no finite-dimensional counterexample to (1).

6. Technical conditions and source record

The stochastic identities above are rigorous first for compactly supported μ with a smooth positive density on a convex support and smooth bounded f with bounded derivatives, on finite time intervals before taking t→∞. The posterior has curvature at least t for t>0, so Brascamp–Lieb gives A_t≼t^(−1)I. The observation reveals X because Y_t/t→X almost surely. Formula (4) then follows by conditional-expectation martingale convergence and Itô isometry.

For (12)–(15), stop at T and at the first time λ_min(A_t)≤ε, so all inverse matrices are bounded. Apply Itô on the stopped interval. For bounded f, 0≤R_t≤Var_{μ_t}(f) and the observation martingale converges in L², so E R_T→0. The nonnegative slope-energy integral then converges by monotone convergence. For a general finite-energy f, bounded smooth truncations are a possible extension route, but the needed convergence of the posterior slope-energy integral has not been proved here. The stochastic derivation is recorded for regular compactly supported μ; extending the process identities to every nonsmooth or unbounded-support log-concave law still needs a stated approximation argument.
