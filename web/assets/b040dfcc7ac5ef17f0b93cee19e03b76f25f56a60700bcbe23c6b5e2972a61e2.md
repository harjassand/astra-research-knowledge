# Primary source notes

This note records the small primary-source set used for the cycle-4 identifiability audit. No controlled raw dataset was downloaded.

1. **Treatment-dependent cfDNA release (2026 in-vitro primary study).** Bartolomucci et al., “Cell-Free DNA Release Kinetics and Fragmentation Reflect Treatment Response and Resistance In Vitro,” PubMed record: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42738856/. The study uses A549, FLO-1 and OE19 lines, cisplatin/5-FU exposure, mutation-specific ddPCR, total cfDNA, fragment sizes and cell-death assays. It reports chemotherapy-associated increases in per-cell release and distinct cisplatin release kinetics for sensitive versus resistant OE19 cells. This directly supports the possibility that q_j(t) depends on treatment and clone sensitivity; it does not calibrate a patient-level transfer factor.

2. **Within-tumour differential shedding (primary SPRINTER study).** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-024-01989-z and open PMC text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11735394/. The authors analyze a new longitudinal primary/metastasis-matched NSCLC dataset with 14,994 cells, use single-cell whole-genome data to infer clone proliferation, and compare clone-specific ctDNA shedding indices to proliferation. They report a positive association in the examined matched case(s). Their own result is evidence that release can carry proliferation-dependent clone bias, not a general calibrated map from plasma VAF to cell abundance.

3. **Serial cfDNA clone reconstruction with matched tissue (primary ovarian study).** “Tracking clonal evolution during treatment in ovarian cancer using cell-free DNA,” Nature 2025, https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09580-0. It reports serial plasma for 18 recurrence-tracked patients (mean 7.9 draws, range 3–13), baseline single-cell tissue, clone-specific structural variants, and a plasma whole-genome validation subset. Only three cases had a second-timepoint tissue sample. It adjusts clone estimates using single-cell-derived cancer cell fractions and normalizes inferred clone frequencies to sum to one per timepoint. The text identifies unobserved clone and spatial/drug-penetration limitations; case 046 had both clones B and E in interval tissue while only clone B was detected in contemporaneous cfDNA, with E appearing later. Processed analyses are listed in Synapse syn66399325; raw sequencing is controlled under dbGaP phs002857.v3.p1. This is not a release-calibration dataset.

4. **Existing direct clone-fitness capability.** “Clonal fitness inferred from timeseries modeling of single cell cancer genomes,” Nature 2021, https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-021-03648-3. The authors use serial single-cell WGS of breast epithelium and PDX time series, infer CNA-defined clone fitness, and compare predictions with experimentally enforced clonal competition. This preempts a broad novelty claim for serial cell-associated clonal-fitness measurement.

5. **Finite-molecule sampling.** Gamisch, “Molecular sampling limits of ctDNA detection in clinical plasma samples,” Journal of Liquid Biopsy 2026, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jlb.2026.100480; PubMed https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42433604/. The analysis integrates 5,238 specimens across three cohorts, reports median input 5,531 genome equivalents (IQR 2,784–13,060), and estimates ideal input-limited detection using Poisson molecule counts. It reports that fewer than 10% of samples meet the listed 95%-detection criteria at 0.01% VAF. This supports molecule-input pricing, not a biological cell-fraction interpretation.

6. **Adaptive therapy bulk information prior art.** Browning et al., “Adaptive therapy under parametric, structural, and measurement uncertainty,” arXiv:2608.18387, https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18387. The prior Astra audit records its two-population result that maintaining resistance at O(f0) on a fixed window gives O(f0^2) Fisher information for total burden. This is inherited prior art, not re-proved or claimed here.
