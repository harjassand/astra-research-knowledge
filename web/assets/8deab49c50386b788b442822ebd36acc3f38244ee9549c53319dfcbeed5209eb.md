# Partial observation in cancer evolution: what bulk data cannot identify, and a paired assay that can

## Main finding

The sharpest removable bottleneck is **cell-level linkage**. Bulk ctDNA gives marginal frequencies of mutations; imaging gives aggregate burden. Neither says whether two resistance-associated alterations occupy the same cells, separate branches, or overlapping subclones. A targeted single-cell DNA measurement directly observes that joint genotype in the biopsied tissue. The exact uncertainty in the bulk data is a Fréchet interval, not merely sequencing noise.

A second, distinct bottleneck remains after linkage is fixed: a change in ctDNA clone odds is the sum of a change in tumor clone odds and a change in clone-specific effective release into plasma. A paired tissue/plasma measurement can estimate that discrepancy, but one biopsy site cannot label it as biological shedding rather than spatial under-sampling. Treat that as a falsifiable calibration experiment, not as a solved whole-body inference problem.

The literature already contains much of the assay stack. CloneSeq-SV (Nature, 2025) uses pretreatment single-cell WGS to assign clone-specific SVs and tracks them in serial duplex cfDNA; it followed 18 HGSOC cases through recurrence. PROSPECT-C combined serial cfDNA, sequential biopsies, imaging, and evolutionary modelling in cetuximab-treated colorectal cancer. SPRINTER (Nature Genetics, 2025) provides a concrete warning for interpreting plasma trajectories: within a matched NSCLC tumor, inferred high-proliferation clones had higher ctDNA shedding. So the defensible research contribution is a **prospective paired-tissue/plasma calibration of the plasma-odds = cell-odds assumption**, plus an explicit lineage-linkage endpoint, not a claim that longitudinal ctDNA or single-cell clonal tracking is new.

## Observation model and identifiability result

For a randomly sampled tumor cell, let `A` and `B` indicate two alterations, with marginal cell prevalences `p_A` and `p_B`; let `theta = P(A and B)`. In a copy-neutral, purity-corrected idealization, infinite-depth bulk sequencing identifies the two marginals but only constrains

`max(0, p_A + p_B - 1) <= theta <= min(p_A, p_B)`.

The four latent cell fractions are `p11=theta`, `p10=p_A-theta`, `p01=p_B-theta`, and `p00=1-p_A-p_B+theta`. Thus for `p_A=p_B=0.20`, the double-positive clone fraction may be anywhere from 0 to 0.20 with exactly the same bulk mutation counts. Both endpoints admit valid branching histories under the infinite-sites assumption: A and B can be sibling branches, or one can be nested in the other. More depth cannot shrink this interval. A scan that reports the same total tumor volume also cannot shrink it, because the total cell count is compatible with every composition in the interval.

A single-cell DNA assay changes the observation from two marginal counts to a four-cell genotype table. If per-locus sensitivity and false-positive rates are `s_A,s_B,f_A,f_B`, use a calibrated call matrix `Q` and fit `n_y ~ Multinomial(N, Q p)` for the four observed call patterns `y`; do not count every missing allele as wild type. Under conditional independence of locus errors, the observed double-positive rate is

`q11 = theta*s_A*s_B + (p_A-theta)*s_A*f_B + (p_B-theta)*f_A*s_B + (1-p_A-p_B+theta)*f_A*f_B`.

This is invertible for `theta` when the call rates are calibrated and `s_l != f_l`; in practice use a constrained multinomial maximum-likelihood estimate and propagate uncertainty in the calibration. Copy-number state, normal-cell contamination, allelic dropout, and doublets have to enter the per-cell likelihood or be filtered with predeclared QC. The cell-level conclusion applies to the assayed tissue; it does not establish that the same fractions hold across unsampled metastases.

As a concrete assay-size calculation, take `p_A=p_B=0.20`, `s_A=s_B=0.98`, `f_A=f_B=0.001`. The expected called double-positive rate is 0.00998 when `theta=0.01`, and 0.04831 when `theta=0.05`. An exact one-sided binomial screen against the 1% value needs 113 usable cells to attain at least 80% power at type-I error below 5% for the 5% alternative under those known error rates. That is only a screening minimum. With 500 usable tumor cells, the binomial standard error around a 5% joint fraction is about 0.97 percentage points (about ±1.9 points at 95%, before calibration uncertainty). A practical design should therefore target at least 500 QC-passing cells per biopsy for a 5% joint-fraction endpoint, and collect extra cells for failed QC. For a paired selection-odds estimate, use roughly 1,000 cells per timepoint when both contrasted clones are at least 20% of the sampled tumor; rarer clones require substantially more cells.

TARGET-seq is a primary assay precedent: its paper reports detection of all four targeted mutations in 98.4% of cells in a clonal cell-line test, while noting a limited target count (up to 12 loci tested in that protocol). Those are platform results, not a guaranteed biopsy sensitivity; validate the patient-specific panel with mixtures and technical controls.

## Longitudinal ctDNA model and the counterexample

For clone `k` at time `t`, let `N_kt` be its cell count in the disease, `eta_kt` its effective release/capture rate into plasma, `a_kt` the marker copy/multiplicity and capture factor, and `L_t` a shared library/sample scale. A simple molecule-count model is

`C_kt ~ Poisson(L_t * eta_kt * a_kt * N_kt)`.

After CN and probe-efficiency correction, for two clones R and S,

`log(C_Rt/C_St) = log(N_Rt/N_St) + log(eta_Rt/eta_St) + noise`.

Over an interval, the ctDNA log-odds slope equals the tumor-cell selection slope plus drift in relative effective release. Imaging can add a noisy total-burden observation such as `I_t = sum_l v_l * sum_k N_klt + error`; it does not identify the clone composition or relative release rates.

**Exact counterexample, even with noiseless assays.** At both visits, MRI reports total burden 100. In world 1, clone fractions move from 50:50 to 2:1 with equal release rates; plasma clone odds move from 1 to 2 because of selection. In world 2, tissue stays 50:50, but the R:S effective release-rate ratio moves from 1 to 2; plasma clone odds and MRI are identical to world 1. Bulk ctDNA plus imaging cannot distinguish the worlds. A contemporaneous single-cell biopsy at visit 2 distinguishes cell composition in the sampled lesion. With only a single-site biopsy, however, a plasma/tissue discrepancy also has an unsampled-metastasis explanation; the estimand must be called an effective plasma-to-biopsy transfer drift unless multiple sites are sampled.

This is not a hypothetical nuisance only: SPRINTER reports a positive relationship between clone S-phase fraction and its ctDNA shedding index in a primary/metastasis-matched NSCLC analysis (the clone-specific ctDNA comparison is from one patient). It supports testing the assumption, but does not establish how often or how fast shedding changes under treatment.

## Constructive paired estimator

For the same patient, collect plasma and fresh tumor tissue at baseline and one early on-treatment timepoint. Identify a trunk and two clone-defining lineages in baseline single-cell DNA/CN/SV data. Track each lineage with multiple stable private SV markers (or validated private SNVs) plus a trunk marker in error-corrected plasma sequencing; use matched white blood cells to filter hematopoietic variants. Reassay the same accessible lesion at the second timepoint, and if feasible sample a second lesion at both times. Record lesion-level volumes and standard whole-patient imaging as burden checks, not as clone-genotype measurements.

Let `x_Rt,x_St` be single-cell counts assigned to the two clones and `m_Rt,m_St` be independent, error-corrected plasma molecules supporting their private markers, with copy-number and marker-efficiency offsets applied. Estimate

`selection_hat = log(x_R1/x_S1) - log(x_R0/x_S0)`

and

`transfer_drift_hat = [log(m_R1/m_S1)-log(m_R0/m_S0)] - selection_hat`.

With independent multinomial cell sampling and Poisson molecule counts, a first-order standard error is

`sqrt(sum_{t=0,1}(1/x_Rt + 1/x_St + 1/m_Rt + 1/m_St))`.

This gives a transparent confidence interval and an assay adequacy check. Example: if R:S is 20:80 at both visits, each biopsy yields 1,000 usable cells (200 and 800 cells), and plasma yields 200 and 800 informative independent marker molecules at each timepoint, the standard error of the transfer-drift contrast is about 0.158 log units, or a 95% half-width of about 0.31. A 0.5-log drift (a 1.65-fold change in relative release) is then a useful prespecified discrepancy threshold. For 5:95 clones, the same counts give about 0.57 log units of 95% half-width; rare-clone calibration is much more expensive. This formula ignores uncertainty in marker copy number, capture efficiency, cell-classification errors, and spatial sampling, so the real interval must be wider.

The ctDNA molecule requirement is a hard feasibility gate. In the ideal no-error sampling model, detecting a marker present in fraction `f` of informative molecules at least once with 95% probability requires `M >= log(0.05)/log(1-f)` independent molecules: about 299 for `f=1%`, 2,995 for `f=0.1%`. Requiring multiple duplex families for specificity, low tumor fraction, and a rare clone raises this requirement. Aggregate several independently validated clone-specific markers where possible; do not substitute raw read depth for independent molecule count. CloneSeq-SV used patient-specific panels and reported a theoretical 10^-5 limit with 1,000 mutations at 1,000x coverage, underscoring that its high sensitivity depends on a large marker panel. The monetary cost is institution- and platform-specific; report assay units and specimen burden rather than inventing a dollar figure.

## Prospective discriminating prediction and falsifier

A small validation study can enroll patients with an accessible lesion and at least two detectable baseline clones, then obtain same-day plasma/tissue before treatment and at a prespecified early treatment time (for example day 14–28), with imaging at baseline and the usual first response assessment. Use within-patient clone comparisons and the stable trunk marker as controls. Predeclare the primary analysis only for pairs with at least 20% tissue prevalence and adequate plasma molecule counts; analyze rare clones separately as underpowered.

Prediction under a stable-transfer model: after CN and marker calibration, the plasma clone-odds change agrees with paired biopsy clone-odds change within ±0.3 log units over the interval, and residual transfer drift has no systematic relationship to cell-cycle state or lesion response. A repeated residual above 0.5 log units across multiple independent markers falsifies use of plasma odds alone as a selection-rate estimator for that interval. A repeated residual associated with the clone's S-phase fraction, after accounting for lesion volume and technical offsets, would support a clone-state-dependent release mechanism; it still would not separate cell death/release from unmeasured metastatic representation without multi-site sampling. The imaging prediction is only that summed lesion volume agrees directionally with the trunk ctDNA burden; it cannot confirm clone fractions.

The result would be useful even if stable transfer is rejected: the single-cell readout identifies selection in the sampled lesion, while the residual quantifies exactly how much the plasma-only estimate is biased. If tissue proportions and plasma ratios both move together across pairs, plasma can be validated as a cheaper follow-up proxy in that measured disease setting.

## Primary literature and scope

- Rodriguez-Meira et al., “Unravelling Intratumoral Heterogeneity through High-Sensitivity Single-Cell Mutational Analysis and Parallel RNA Sequencing,” *Molecular Cell* (2019): [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.molcel.2019.01.009). Four mutation calls in 98.4% of cells in a cell-line benchmark; protocol follow-up states up to 12 targeted regions. This supports technical feasibility, not patient-level sensitivity.
- Zatzman et al., “Tracking clonal evolution during treatment in ovarian cancer using cell-free DNA,” *Nature* (2025): [article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09580-0). CloneSeq-SV combines pretreatment scWGS, clone-specific SVs, and serial duplex cfDNA in HGSOC. There were 18 patients with longitudinal samples and radiographic recurrence; mean 7.9 plasma timepoints, and second-timepoint scWGS was generated for three patients. This is the closest existing assay stack and narrows the novelty claim to systematic paired calibration.
- Lucas et al., “Characterizing the evolutionary dynamics of cancer proliferation in single-cell clones with SPRINTER,” *Nature Genetics* (2025): [article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41588-024-01989-z). Matched NSCLC single-cell analysis linked proliferative clones with ctDNA shedding; the ctDNA clone comparison is limited to one patient.
- Siravegna et al., “Longitudinal Liquid Biopsy and Mathematical Modeling of Clonal Evolution Forecast Time to Treatment Failure in the PROSPECT-C Phase II Colorectal Cancer Clinical Trial,” *Cancer Discovery* (2018): [article](https://aacrjournals.org/cancerdiscovery/article/8/10/1270/6146/Longitudinal-Liquid-Biopsy-and-Mathematical). Prospective precedent for integrating serial plasma, matched sequential tissue biopsies, and imaging in anti-EGFR-treated metastatic CRC.

**Assessment:** The single-cell co-occurrence result is mathematically decisive for the sampled tissue, but it is not a new assay concept. The paired transfer-drift estimator is a useful prospective validation question, with spatial representation and sparse ctDNA molecules as the key remaining failure modes. Do not claim clinical treatment utility or whole-body clone frequencies unless separately validated.
