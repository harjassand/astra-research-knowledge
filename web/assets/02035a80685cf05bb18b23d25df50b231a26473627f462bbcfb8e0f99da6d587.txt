Abstract

    

The global attractor conjecture says that toric dynamical systems
have a globally attracting point (up to linear conservation relations)
— or, equivalently, complex balanced systems have a globally attracting point within each stoichiometric compatibility class.
A proof of this conjecture implies that a large class of nonlinear dynamical systems on the positive orthant have very simple and stable dynamics. The conjecture originates from the 1972 breakthrough work by Fritz Horn and Roy Jackson, and was formulated in its current form by Horn in 1974.

    

Toric dynamical systems can be embedded into toric differential inclusions. We show that each bounded positive solution of a toric differential inclusion is contained in an invariant region that prevents it from approaching the boundary of the positive orthant. We use this result to prove the global attractor conjecture.
In particular, it follows that all detailed balanced mass action systems and all deficiency zero weakly reversible systems have the global attractor property.

The name “toric dynamical system” has been introduced [10] in order to emphasize the remarkable algebraic properties of these systems, but this class of polynomial dynamical systems has been first studied in depth in the 1972 breakthrough paper of Horn and Jackson [16], where they have been called complex balanced mass-action systems. Also in [16] Horn and Jackson have shown that these systems enjoy remarkable stability properties, and in particular they have a unique positive equilibrium within each linear invariant subspace, and this equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable.
Actually, in [16] Horn and Jackson stated that the unique positive equilibrium within each linear invariant subspace is a global attractor99
              9

The conjecture that toric dynamical systems have a global attractor within each linear invariant subspace (or, in the language of Horn, that complex balanced mass-action systems have a global attractor within each reaction simplex) was later called the global attractor conjecture [10].






The global attractor conjecture has resisted efforts for a proof for over five decades, but proofs of many special cases have been obtained during this time, for example [21, 22, 10, 4, 5, 19, 3, 18].






In particular, Craciun, Nazarov and Pantea [3] have proved the three-dimensional case, and Pantea has generalized this result for the case where the dimension of the linear invariant subspaces is at most three [18]. Using a different approach, Anderson has proved the conjecture under the additional hypothesis that the graph  $G$  has a single connected component [5], and this result has been generalized by Gopalkrishnan, Miller, and Shiu for the case where the graph  $G$  is strongly endotactic [15].

2 Definitions and notation





As we mentioned in the previous section, some of the main objects of interest in this paper are non-autonomous polynomial dynamical systems of the form (2) such that the graph  $G$  is weakly reversible, and the nonnegative scalar functions  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)$  are bounded away from zero and infinity. We will refer to this class of dynamical systems as variable- $k$  weakly reversible dynamical systems1919
              19
              
              
              
            Variable- $k$  weakly reversible dynamical systems have also been called
variable- $k$  toric dynamical systems [1], because any such dynamical system can be obtained from a toric dynamical system by using the same graph  $G$ , and replacing the constants  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}$  with time-dependent non-negative functions  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)$  that are bounded away from zero and infinity.
.






More precisely, a variable- $k$  weakly reversible dynamical system is a dynamical system on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  of the form







 $\frac{dx}{dt}=\sum_{y\to y^{\prime}\in G}k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)\,x^{y}(y^{\prime}-y)$ 

(5)




such that  $G$  is weakly reversible, and there exists a fixed positive number  $\varepsilon$  such that  $\varepsilon\leq k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$  for all  $t$  and for all  $y\to y^{\prime}\in G$ .






Note that, under these conditions, solutions of the variable- $k$  weakly reversible dynamical system above are also solutions of the differential inclusion on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  given by







 $\frac{dx}{dt}\in F(x),\mathrm{\ where\ }F(x)=\left\{\sum_{y\to y^{\prime}\in G}k_{y\to y^{\prime}}\,x^{y}(y^{\prime}-y)\ \ |\ \ \varepsilon\leq k_{y\to y^{\prime}}\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right\}.$ 

(6)








We will show that solutions of the differential inclusion (6) are also solutions of a type of differential inclusions with rich geometric properties, called toric differential inclusions.
Later in this section we will define the general notion of toric differential inclusions in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$ .
We first define the class of polar differential inclusions in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ .






2.1 Polar differential inclusions





If the graph  $G$  is reversible, then the equations (5) can be written as







 $\frac{dx}{dt}=\sum_{y\rightleftharpoons y^{\prime}\in G}\left(k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)x^{y}-k_{y^{\prime}\to y}(t)x^{y^{\prime}}\right)(y^{\prime}-y),$ 

(7)




by grouping together terms given by an edge  $y\to y^{\prime}$  and its reverse  $y^{\prime}\to y$ . Then, for each reversible edge  $y\rightleftharpoons y^{\prime}\in G$ , there is now a single term in the sum (7), which can be thought of as a “tug-of-war” between the forward and reverse terms. Indeed, both the forward and the reverse terms are trying to “push” the state  $x(t)$  of the system along the same line2020
              20
              
              
              
            i.e., a line whose direction is given by the vector  $y^{\prime}-y$ .
, but in opposite directions. Then, the domain  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  can be partitioned into three regions: the region where the inequality  $\varepsilon x^{y}\geq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x^{y^{\prime}}$  holds (which implies  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)x^{y}\geq k_{y^{\prime}\to y}(t)x^{y^{\prime}}$ ), the region where the inequality  $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x^{y}\leq\varepsilon x^{y^{\prime}}$  holds (which implies  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)x^{y}\leq k_{y^{\prime}\to y}(t)x^{y^{\prime}}$ ), and an uncertainty region where neither one of these two inequalities are satisfied, and either one of the two terms  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)x^{y}$  and  $k_{y^{\prime}\to y}(t)x^{y^{\prime}}$  may win the tug-of-war, due to the fact that  $k_{y\to y^{\prime}}(t)$  and  $k_{y^{\prime}\to y}(t)$  may take any values between  $\varepsilon$  and  $\frac{1}{\varepsilon}$ .
Some concrete examples can be found in Section 3 of [3]. In particular, note that if one term does win the tug-of-war, then the direction of the sum of the two terms is towards the uncertainty region, and also towards the hypersurface  $x^{y}=x^{y^{\prime}}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$ .






The simplest way to understand how these regions adjoin each other is to look at them in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  instead of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$ , after applying a logarithmic transformation. If we denote  $X=\log x$ , then the hypersurface  $x^{y}=x^{y^{\prime}}$  becomes the hyperplane  $(y^{\prime}-y)\cdot X=0$ . Moreover, the uncertainty region described above becomes just the set of points at distance  $<\delta=\frac{2|\!\log\varepsilon|}{||y^{\prime}-y||}$  from this hyperplane. Note also that the direction given by the vector  $y^{\prime}-y$  is exactly the direction orthogonal to the hyperplane  $(y^{\prime}-y)\cdot X=0$ .






Therefore, if the graph  $G$  consists of a single reversible edge  $y\rightleftharpoons y^{\prime}$ , then the dynamics of the system (7) at a point  $x$  can be described as follows: we know that  $x$  will move along a line of support vector  $(y^{\prime}-y)$ , and we are able to specify one direction or another by mapping  $x$  to  $X=\log x$ , and checking whether the distance between  $X$  and the hyperplane  $(y^{\prime}-y)\cdot X=0$  is  $\geq\delta$ . Moreover, if we can specify this direction, then it is always the direction towards (and not away from) this hyperplane, and orthogonal to it.






This characterization has the advantage that it can be carried over to the more general case, where  $G$  contains several reversible edges. In that case we have several tug-of-wars going on at the same time, but for each one of them we can specify the winning direction (if any) at  $x$  by calculating the distance between  $X=\log x$  and some hyperplane in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . Depending on whether  $X$  falls outside an uncertainty region or not, each reversible edge  $y\rightleftharpoons y^{\prime}$  of  $G$  contributes one or two resultant vectors (if one, then it is either  $y^{\prime}-y$  or  $y-y^{\prime}$ , and if two, then they are  $\pm(y^{\prime}-y)$ ).






If follows that any system (7) can be embedded into a differential inclusion on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  given by a set  $\mathcal{H}$  of hyperplanes in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  and a number  $\delta>0$ , as follows. For each  $x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  we define  $F_{\mathcal{H},\delta}(\log x)$  to be the convex cone generated by vectors orthogonal to the hyperplanes of  $\mathcal{H}$ , in the direction that goes from the point  $X=\log x$  towards each hyperplane, and also the opposite direction if  $X$  is at distance  $<\delta$  from some hyperplane. If  $X$  does not belong to any uncertainty region, then  $F_{\mathcal{H},\delta}(\log x)$  is defined to be exactly the polar cone  $C^{o}$  of a cone  $C$  bounded by a subset of hyperplanes2121
              21
              
              
              
            the cone  $C$  is the largest cone that contains the point  $X$  within the polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})$  determined by the set of hyperplanes  $\mathcal{H}$ . The convex cone generated by the “outer normal” vectors orthogonal to the hyperplane faces of  $C$  is its polar cone  $C^{o}$  [30].
 from  $\mathcal{H}$ . Moreover, if  $X$  does belong to some uncertainty regions, then we can still describe  $F_{\mathcal{H},\delta}(\log x)$  in terms of polar cones, by including not just the polar of the largest cone of  $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})$  that contains  $X$ , but also the polar of each cone of  $\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H})$  that is at distance  $\leq\delta$  from  $X$ .






Of course, not all polyhedral fans are determined by a set of hyperplanes as above. Nevertheless, we can generalize the construction described above to define polar differential inclusions given by a polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , as described in the previous section: we define  $F_{\mathcal{F},\delta}(X)$  to be the convex cone generated by the union of polar cones  $C^{o}$  for all  $C\in\mathcal{F}$  such that  $dist(X,C)<\delta$ .








2.2 Toric differential inclusions





Polar differential inclusions can produce sets of cones that contain the right-hand-side of the vector fields (5) for reversible  $G$ , but they are defined on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , while the system (5) is defined on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$ . To obtain a proper embedding we need to introduce toric differential inclusions, which are obtained from polar differential inclusions by a logarithmic transformation: a toric differential inclusion given by a polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  is a differential inclusion on  $\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$  given by







 $\frac{dx}{dt}\in F_{\mathcal{F},\delta}(\log x)$ 

(8)




where  $F_{\mathcal{F},\delta}$  is a polar differential inclusion as defined above.






Due to the way this definition is related to the system (7), it follows that variable- $k$  weakly reversible dynamical systems (5) that are generated by a reversible graph  $G$  can be embedded into polar differential inclusions.






As we discussed above, for a reversible graph  $G$ , the polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}$  could be chosen simply as the fan generated by the set of hyperplanes that are orthogonal to the vectors  $y^{\prime}-y$  for  $y\to y^{\prime}\in G$ .






We need to remove this strong assumption of reversibility,
in order to be able to address the global attractor conjecture in full generality.
In the next section we explain how to find such a polyhedral fan for any weakly reversible graph  $G$ .

4 Strategy for the proof of the global attractor conjecture: the big picture





In order to prove the global attractor conjecture we will prove that toric dynamical systems are persistent. It has been known for a long time that persistence of toric dynamical systems, together with a simple application of the LaSalle invariance principle would immediately imply global stability [21, 22, 32].






More precisely, we will prove the following stronger fact: for any positive initial condition  $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  of a toric dynamical system there exists a compact forward invariant region  $K_{x_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  such that  $x_{0}\in K_{x_{0}}$ . Of course, this implies that toric dynamical systems are persistent.






In order to accomplish this goal, our main focus will be the construction of a specific type of hypersurfaces in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  called zero-separating hypersurfaces (ZSH), which, when considered together with well-chosen level sets of the Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function, give rise to compact forward invariant regions  $K_{x_{0}}$  like we mentioned above; see Figure 2 for a two-dimensional example (in this example the ZSH is just a curve).






More precisely, our strategy will be to construct polyhedral zero-separating hypersurfaces (polyhedral ZSH).
Our construction of a polyhedral ZSH will rely on a ZSH blueprint. A ZSH blueprint is a structure associated to a given polyhedral fan, which contains inside complete instructions for the construction of an exhaustive family of ZSH. Polyhedral fans are relevant because of the theorem on embedding toric dynamical systems into toric differential inclusions; and a toric differential inclusion (denoted  $\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{F},\delta}$ ) is given by a polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}$  and a positive number  $\delta$ .






In our construction of polyhedral ZSH we will see that the specific number  $\delta$  above will practically become irrelevant, and it will turn out that we will not need to look in much depth at detailed properties of toric differential inclusions; it will be sufficient to focus almost exclusively on the much simpler notion of polyhedral fan.






Therefore, given a polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}$ , we will develop a step-by-step process that allows us to construct some polyhedral hypersurfaces associated to  $\mathcal{F}$ . More precisely, we will show how to construct such families of polyhedral hypersurfaces by taking advantage of a ZSH blueprint, and we will see later that these hypersurfaces are indeed zero-separating hypersurfaces.






In summary, we will proceed according to the following plan2828
            28
            
            
            
          For convenience of notation we assume that we start in  $(n+1)$ -dimensional space; then the fundamentals steps (i.e., Steps 2 and 3) take place in  $n$ -dimensional space.:






Step 0. Given a toric dynamical system  $\mathbf{T}_{n+1}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{>0}$ , we embed it into a toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}_{n+1}$ , generated by a polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}_{n+1}$  and a number  $\delta>0$ .






Step 1. Given the polyhedral fan  $\mathcal{F}_{n+1}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$  we construct the  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n}$ -subdivision  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  of  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  generated by  $\mathcal{F}_{n+1}$ .






Step 2. Given the  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n}$ -subdivision  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  we construct a ZSH blueprint  $\mathcal{B}_{n}$  for  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$ .






Step 3. Given the ZSH blueprint  $\mathcal{B}_{n}$  we construct an exhaustive family of  $n$ -dimensional polyhedral ZSHs denoted  $\mathcal{Z}_{\varepsilon}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n+1}$ .






Step 4. We show that for any  $x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n+1}$  there exists a polyhedral hypersurface  $\mathcal{Z}_{\varepsilon}$  among the ones that we have constructed in step 3, such that  $\mathcal{Z}_{\varepsilon}$  (together with a well-chosen level set of the Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function) determines a compact set  $K_{x_{0}}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n+1}$  such that  $x_{0}\in K_{x_{0}}$  and  $K_{x_{0}}$  is a forward invariant region for the toric dynamical system  $\mathbf{T}_{n+1}$  that we started with in Step 0.






Step 0 has already been done in [2], and we have discussed it in section 3. Step 1 will not be difficult, but we will need to we restrict our attention (without loss of generality) to  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n+1}$ -fans.
Steps 2 and 3 are very much interconnected, and most of our effort will focus on these two steps. Step 4 will be a relatively quick corollary of the previous steps.

4.2 Forward invariant regions and ZSH for toric differential inclusions





We are interested in constructing forward-invariant regions for toric differential inclusions. Let us first define the following notion:






Definition 4.4.




Consider a piecewise smooth surface  $\mathcal{Z}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$ , and a toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  generated by a fan  $\mathcal{F}$  and a number  $\delta>0$ . If  $P$  is a smooth point of  $\mathcal{Z}$ , we say that  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $P$  if the tangent hyperplane of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $P$  is a supporting hyperplane of the cone  $F_{\mathcal{F},\delta}(P)$ . Also, for a set  $\Omega\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$ , we say that  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  within  $\Omega$  if  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  at any smooth point  $P\in\Omega$ .








Let us note the following simple characterization of the condition that  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $P$ :






Remark 4.5.




Consider a piecewise smooth surface  $\mathcal{Z}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$ , and a toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  generated by a fan  $\mathcal{F}$  and a number  $\delta>0$ . Consider a smooth point of  $\mathcal{Z}$  denoted  $P$ . Then  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $P$  if and only if there exists a normal vector  $\vec{n}_{P}$  of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $P$  such that  $\vec{n}_{P}\in\mathcal{C}$  for any cone  $\mathcal{C}\in\mathcal{F}$  that satisfies
the condition







 $\mathrm{dist}(\mathcal{C},\log P)<\delta.$ 











Indeed, this follows immediately from the definition of the cone  $F_{\mathcal{F},\delta}(P)$  in (11), together with the general observation that the cone generated by a union of polar cones equals the polar cone of the intersection of those cones [31].






The key notion in this section is the zero-separating hypersurface, or ZSH for short:






Definition 4.6.

 $(i)$  there exists some  $\eta\in(0,1)$  such that







 $\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\setminus(\eta+\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}),$ 









 $(ii)$   $\mathcal{Z}$  intersects every toric ray3232
                  32

and






 $(iii)$  there exists some  $M\geq 1$  such that the toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}$  cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  within the set  $(0,M)^{n}$ .








Recall that, given a toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}$  in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$ , we want to construct a family of ZSH for  $\mathcal{T}$  denoted  $\mathcal{Z}_{\varepsilon}$  such that each such ZSH (together with a well-chosen level set of the Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function) gives rise to a compact forward invariant set  $K_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$  for the toric dynamical system that we have started with. 6 Proof of the global attractor conjecture in the three-dimensional case





We now discuss the construction of ZSH for three-dimensional toric differential inclusions.






For our construction of ZSH in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{3}$  we will rely very heavily on two-dimensional representations of three-dimensional fans using subdivisions of the sets  $\mathscr{D}_{2}^{P}$  (and/or  $\mathscr{D}_{2}^{H}$ ) described below, and on the corresponding notion of two-dimensional ZSH blueprint.






Recall that in the previous section we have been able to use one-dimensional ZSH blueprints for the construction of one-dimensional ZSH in  $\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{2}$  (i.e., for the construction of polygonal curves that satisfy the ZSH property). Moreover, our construction was “canonical”, in the following sense: once we specified the starting point  $P_{0}$  of coordinates  $(\varepsilon,\varepsilon)$ , everything else was uniquely determined by the following simple conditions:  $(i)$  the edges of the ZSH are in one-to-one correspondence with the tiles of the ZSH blueprint,  $(ii)$  each edge is orthogonal to the basepoint of its corresponding tile, and  $(iii)$  the image of each edge via the map  $\Psi_{2}$  is exactly its corresponding tile.






In the next subsection we will choose an especially convenient fan  $\mathcal{F}_{3}$ , define a ZSH blueprint for it, and devise a construction process for the ZSH which is based on this ZSH blueprint. We will see that we have to adapt and modify some of the rules  $(i)$ - $(iii)$  described above, but in the end we can create such a construction process, and to some extent it can also be seen as “canonical”, under somewhat relaxed restrictions. More precisely, our construction give rise to a polyhedral ZSH such that:  $(i^{\prime})$  the faces of the ZSH are in one-to-one correspondence with the tiles of the ZSH blueprint,  $(ii^{\prime})$  each face is orthogonal to the basepoint of its corresponding tile, and  $(iii^{\prime})$  the image of each edge via the map  $\Psi_{3}$  is arbitrarily close to its corresponding tile (in a sense that will be clarified below).






In a later subsection we will describe the construction of a polyhedral ZSH for a fully general choice of fan  $\mathcal{F}_{3}$ .

7.1 Polyhedral fans and subdivisions in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ 





Recall that by a “fan” we mean a complete and pointed polyhedral fan. We start by defining a special kind of fan, called  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n}$ -fan. The definition proceeds inductively, for all  $n\geq 2$ , as follows.






An  $x_{1}$ - $x_{2}$ -fan  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  is a fan in  $\mathbb{R}^{2}$  that contains both of the 1D cones (i.e., rays) that lie along the  $x_{2}$ -axis.

Also, the set  $\pi_{3}^{-1}(\{0\})$ , i.e., the  $x_{3}$ -axis, must be a union of cones of  $\mathcal{F}_{3}$ , namely the positive ray and the negative ray of the  $x_{3}$ -axis7373

First, note that to any face
 $\Delta_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}$  we can associate a binary word of length  $n$  denoted







 $\alpha(\Delta_{0})=\overline{\alpha_{1}a_{2}...a_{n}}\in\{0,1\}^{n},$ 





as follows.
Consider the face  $\pi_{n}(\Delta_{0})$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n-1}$ ; if we have







 $\dim(\pi_{n}(\Delta_{0}))=\dim(\Delta_{0})$ 





then we define  $\alpha_{n}=0$ , and if







 $\dim(\pi_{n}(\Delta_{0}))=\dim(\Delta_{0})-1$ 





then we define  $\alpha_{n}=1$ .
Similarly, we define







 $\alpha_{n-1}=\dim(\pi_{n}(\Delta_{0}))-\dim(\pi_{n-1}(\pi_{n}(\Delta_{0}))),$ 





and so on. In other words, as we consider the successive projections  $\pi_{n}$ ,  $\pi_{n-1}$ ,  $\pi_{n-2},...,\pi_{1}$  of  $\Delta_{0}$ , the binary letter  $\alpha_{j}$  tells us if the  $j^{\mathrm{th}}$  projection  $\pi_{j}$  did or did not cause its dimension to decrease by one unit. Equivalently, but in the opposite order, we can think about  $\Delta_{0}$  as having been “grown” by starting from a single point  $0\in\mathbb{R}^{0}$ , and then its dimension went up by one unit at every stage  $j$  that has  $\alpha_{j}=1$ .








7.3 ZSH pre-blueprints of an  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n}$ -subdivision  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$

We first define one-dimensional ZSH pre-blueprints; these are sets of intervals with disjoint interiors that cover  $\mathbb{R}^{1}$  and have a strong relationship with a given  $x_{1}$ - $x_{1}$ -subdivision. Consider an  $x_{1}$ - $x_{1}$ -subdivision  $\mathcal{S}_{1}$ , as shown in Figure 12. In general,  $\mathcal{S}_{1}$  consists of a finite set of points (i.e., 0-faces) and a finite set of closed intervals (i.e., 1-faces) whose endpoints are those 0-faces.

 $\mathit{nbhd}(\sigma_{0})=\sigma_{0}+[-\varepsilon(0),\varepsilon(0)]$

 $\mathit{nbhd}(\sigma_{1})=\sigma_{1}\setminus\displaystyle\bigcup_{\sigma_{0}\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}\mathit{nbhd}(\sigma_{0})$

7.4 From ZSH pre-blueprints to faithful ZSH blueprints






In the previous section we have discussed the construction of ZSH pre-blueprints of an  $x_{1}$ - $x_{n}$ -subdivision  $\mathcal{S}_{n}$  for any  $n$ . 9 Appendix: some general results about fans, toric differential inclusions, and ZSH





In this section we discuss some technical results that we used in previous sections. For some of these results, for the sake of a simpler notation, we formulate and prove them in 3D instead of nD; the proof in nD is analogous.






9.1 Finer and coarser fans, embeddings of toric differential inclusions, forward invariant regions





The following definition clarifies what we mean by “the fan  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  is coarser than the fan  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$ ”, or equivalently, “the fan  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  is finer than the fan  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$ ”.






Definition 9.1.




Given two fans  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  and  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , we say that  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  is finer than  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  (or  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  is coarser than  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$ ) if any cone in  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  can be written as a union of cones in  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$ .








We now want to show that forward invariant regions of toric differential inclusions generated by the finer fan are also forward invariant regions of toric differential inclusions generated by the coarser fan; for this we need the notion of “embedding” for differential inclusions.






Definition 9.2.




Given two differential inclusions  $\frac{d{\mathbf{x}}}{dt}\in F_{1}({\mathbf{x}})$  and  $\frac{d{\mathbf{x}}}{dt}\in F_{2}({\mathbf{x}})$ , we say that the first one is embedded in the second one if  $F_{1}({\mathbf{x}})\subseteq F_{2}({\mathbf{x}})$  for all  ${\mathbf{x}}$ .








The following lemma and its corollary allow us to replace a toric differential inclusion  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  with a more convenient one (denoted  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$ ) and argue that ZSH for the new toric differential inclusion are actually also ZSH for the old toric differential inclusion we started with.






Lemma 9.3.




Consider a number  $\delta>0$  and two fans  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  and  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  such that  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  is finer than  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$ . If  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  is the toric differential inclusion generated by  $(\mathcal{F}_{1},\delta)$ , and  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$  is the toric differential inclusion generated by  $(\mathcal{F}_{2},\delta)$ , then  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  is embedded in  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$ .








Proof.




Consider some  ${\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{>0}$ . For each cone  $K_{1}\in\mathcal{F}_{1}$  at distance  $<\delta$  from the point  $\log({\bf x})$ , there exists some cone  $K_{2}\in\mathcal{F}_{2}$  that is also at distance  $<\delta$  from the point  $\log({\bf x})$ , such that  $K_{2}\subseteq K_{1}$ . But this implies that the polars of these two cones satisfy  $K_{1}^{o}\subseteq K_{2}^{o}$ , so we obtain







 $F_{\mathcal{F}_{1},\delta}(\log({\bf x}))\subseteq F_{\mathcal{F}_{2},\delta}(\log({\bf x})),$ 





i.e.,  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  is embedded in  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$ .
∎








Lemma 9.3 immediately implies the following result.






Corollary 9.4.




Consider a number  $\delta>0$  and two fans  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$  and  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  in  $\mathbb{R}^{n}$  such that  $\mathcal{F}_{2}$  is finer than  $\mathcal{F}_{1}$ . Denote by  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  the toric differential inclusion generated by  $(\mathcal{F}_{1},\delta)$ , and by  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$  the toric differential inclusion generated by  $(\mathcal{F}_{2},\delta)$ . Assume that  $\mathcal{T}_{2}$  cannot cross some surface  $\mathcal{Z}$  within a set  $\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}$ . Then  $\mathcal{T}_{1}$  also cannot cross  $\mathcal{Z}$  within  $\Omega$ .










9.2 Necessary and sufficient conditions for ZSH





Consider a ZSH denoted  $\mathcal{Z}$  inside the unit cube  $(0,1)^{3}$ , and recall that we can assume that the normal line at any smooth point of  $\mathcal{Z}$  is contained in the union of the (closed) positive orthant and the (closed) negative orthant.
The outer normal of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at a point  $\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{Z}$  is defined as the normal direction of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $\mathbf{x}$  pointing into the (closed) negative orthant. Then we have:






Lemma 9.5.

Proof.




Since  $\mathcal{Z}$  is a zero-separating surface for  $\mathcal{T}_{3}$  and  $\log(\mathbf{x})\in\mathcal{C}$ , it follows that the tangent plane to  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $\mathbf{x}$  is a supporting plane of the polar cone  $\mathcal{C}^{o}$  (otherwise the cone  $\mathcal{C}^{o}$  would provide a way for trajectories of  $\mathcal{T}_{3}$  to “cross through” the zero-separating surface  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $\mathbf{x}$ ). Therefore, the outer normal of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $\mathbf{x}$  must belong to the polar cone of  $\mathcal{C}^{o}$ . In conclusion, the outer normal of  $\mathcal{Z}$  at  $\mathbf{x}$  belongs to the polar of the polar of the cone  $\mathcal{C}$ , which is simply  $\mathcal{C}$ .
∎

(iv) If  $v=(0,-1,1)$ , then for any  $\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}>0$  there exists a neighborhood  $V_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}$  of the origin such that if  $(t^{X},t^{Y},t)\in V_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}$  and if  $Y<1+\varepsilon_{1}$  and  $X>1+2\varepsilon_{1}$  then the (signed) angle between the vector  $w_{v}(t^{X},t^{Y},t)$  and the direction of the  $X$ -axis belongs to the interval  $[\tan^{-1}\frac{Y+1}{X}-\varepsilon_{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]$ .








Proof.




Using the fact that  $\log(t)<0$  we can write the Jacobian matrix of  $\Psi_{3}$  in the form







 $\left|\frac{\log t}{t}\right|\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}-t^{1-X}&0&X\\
0&-t^{1-Y}&Y\end{array}\right),$ 

(24)




Then we multiply it by the vector  $v$  to obtain an explicit formula for the vector  $w_{v}(t^{X},t^{Y},t)$ :







 $w_{v}(t^{X},t^{Y},t)=\left|\frac{\log t}{t}\right|\left(-v_{1}t^{1-X}+v_{3}X,-v_{2}t^{1-Y}+v_{3}Y\right).$ 





Then, for example, in case  $(i)$  we want to show that







 $-v_{1}t^{1-X}+v_{3}X\gg|-v_{2}t^{1-Y}+v_{3}Y|.$ 





This follows if we take into account that, for small a enough neighborhood  $V_{\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}}$  (i.e., if  $t$  is small enough), the hypotheses  $X-Y>\varepsilon_{1},Y-1>\varepsilon_{1}$  imply  $t^{1-X}\gg t^{1-Y}$ ,  $t^{1-X}\gg X$ ,  $t^{1-Y}\gg Y$ , which imply







 $-v_{1}t^{1-X}\gg|-v_{2}t^{1-Y}+v_{3}Y|+|v_{3}X|.$ 









The other cases are similar.
∎










9.4 Asymptotic level sets of the Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function





Consider a Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function  $V:\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}$  given by







 $V(x_{1},...,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}\log\frac{x_{i}}{x_{i}^{0}}-x_{i}+x_{i}^{0}),$ 





for some fixed  $(x_{1}^{0},...,x_{n}^{0})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}$ . Then we have:






Lemma 9.11.




For large values of  $M$ , the level set of the Horn-Jackson Lyapunov function given by the equation







 $V(x_{1},...,x_{n})=M,$ 





is contained between two simplicial sets of the form







 $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=\alpha\left(1\pm\frac{C}{\log M}\right)$ 





for some value  $\alpha\sim M/log(M)$  and for some for some constant  $C>0$  that depends only on  $n$ .








Proof.




The proof of this lemma follows from elementary considerations.
For example we can calculate the minimum of  $V$  and the maximum of  $V$  along an arbitrary simplex of the form
 $\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=\alpha.$ 
for large values of  $\alpha$ .
(The minimum is attained at a point  $x_{min}$  that is proportional with the vector  $x_{0}$  that shows up in the expression of  $V$ , and the maximum is attained at a point  $x_{max}$  where all but one of the coordinates are zero; the nonzero coordinate of  $x_{max}$  is the one where  $x_{0}$  has its smallest coordinate.)
∎
