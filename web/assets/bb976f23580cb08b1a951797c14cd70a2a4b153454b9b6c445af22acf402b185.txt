N21 — Climate response under hidden ocean and feedback states
Phase II bounded record, v1; 2026-10-09

Claim status: internally derived candidate theorem and finite-tested synthetic example. No empirical response estimate, model validation, novelty/priority claim, or claim of resolving Earth-system equilibrium sensitivity.

TARGET

Can energy conservation and causal forcing experiments identify forced climate response without inferring it from the climate system's passive fluctuations, even when slow ocean/cloud states are hidden? Can such identification extend to equilibrium response without assigning values to unknown feedbacks?

Answer: the response kernel over the excited and observed time scales is identifiable by exogenous intervention, independently of natural-variability/FDT assumptions and without reconstructing hidden states. Combining its measured temperature and total-heat transfer functions with the global energy budget yields an exact, forcing-path-specific dynamic restoring operator. This gives transient predictions for the same forcing channel and regime by convolution. It does not identify the infinite-time/zero-frequency gain from a finite campaign unless a tail/stability bound is supplied. A stable energy-balanced hidden-feedback countermodel makes the latter limit quantitative.

1. DEFINITIONS AND SCOPE

Use anomalies relative to a specified reference climate. Let u(t) be an externally assigned radiative-forcing perturbation in W m^-2, with its spatial/vertical pattern and time history specified. The forcing channel must be the one whose response is sought. An effective-radiative-forcing scalar may be used only when its rapid adjustments are included consistently; otherwise use a vector u of prescribed forcing modes. Let T(t) be global-mean surface temperature anomaly. Let H(t) be the full Earth-system heat inventory anomaly per unit Earth area, in J m^-2, including full-depth ocean, atmosphere, land, and cryosphere/latent heat terms. Let N(t) be globally averaged downward net TOA radiative flux anomaly, W m^-2. With other boundary energy inputs either measured and included or removed as a baseline, conservation is

    dH/dt = N.                                                   (1)

The system can have arbitrary unobserved ocean, cloud, ice, circulation, and internal-noise states. No equilibrium distribution, fluctuation-dissipation law, radiative-feedback coefficient, heat-uptake efficacy, or low-dimensional state model is assumed for the identification result below.

For the simplest exact theorem, the response to the chosen forcing channel is locally linear and time invariant around the reference ensemble over the tested amplitude range, and the response is causal:

    Y_t = sum_{j=0}^p G_j U_{t-j} + eta_t,                       (2)

where Y_t may be T_t, H_t, or N_t, and eta is the zero-input internal variability plus measurement error. The intervention sequence U is independent of the complete pre-intervention state and eta, has mean zero and nonzero variance, and is generated independently of all future eta. This is an assignment condition, not a claim about observational forcing records. Discrete time avoids distribution-valued white noise; continuous kernels follow by refining the interval. For nonlinear dynamics, replace (2) by the causal response functional defined in §3 and use amplitude ladders; the covariance formula is then only the best linear projection unless local linearity is verified.

2. INTERVENTIONAL RESPONSE IDENTIFICATION

Theorem (randomized forcing identifies the operational climate response). Under (2), for every tested lag j,

    G_j = Cov(Y_{t+j}, U_t) / Var(U_t).                          (3)

For m simultaneously assigned forcing modes with full-rank covariance matrix Sigma_U,

    G_j = Cov(Y_{t+j}, U_t) Sigma_U^{-1}.                       (4)

Proof. Expand Y_{t+j}=sum_k G_k U_{t+j-k}+eta_{t+j}. For a mean-zero white intervention sequence, Cov(U_{t+j-k},U_t)=0 unless k=j, when it equals Var(U_t). Randomization gives Cov(eta_{t+j},U_t)=0, including when eta contains arbitrary hidden-state evolution driven by earlier variability. This proves (3). The same covariance expansion in vector form proves (4). No internal state is estimated. A mode that is not both excited by U and visible in Y does not affect the target transfer; any hidden mode that does affect Y after the intervention appears in G_j.

If U is colored, the equivalent stationary frequency-domain identity is

    S_YU(omega) = G(omega) S_UU(omega),
    G(omega) = S_YU(omega) S_UU(omega)^{-1},                    (5)

at frequencies where S_UU is full rank. This is causal system identification by assigned input, not FDT: the cross-spectrum is between the treatment and response, rather than an autocorrelation of unforced fluctuations.

For an observational natural event Z (eruption indicator, measured solar anomaly, or another source proxy) rather than randomized U, the scalar IV analogue is G_j=Cov(Y_{t+j},Z_t)/Cov(U_t,Z_t), provided Cov(U_t,Z_t) is nonzero and the event is independent of background disturbances conditional on the design, has no direct pathway to Y outside the included forcing vector U, and no event-induced selection or anticipation contaminates the comparison. These are strong exogeneity/exclusion conditions. A lone eruption does not itself supply replicated treatment assignment or broad frequency excitation.

More explicitly, if event e has forcing waveform F_{e,q}=a_e p_q with a common profile p and an exogenous amplitude instrument Z_e, its lag-j response obeys Y_{e,j}=a_e sum_{q=0}^j G_{j-q}p_q+eta_{e,j}. The IV ratio Cov(Y_{e,j},Z_e)/Cov(a_e,Z_e) identifies (p*G)_j, the response to that event profile. It does not by itself identify the impulse kernel G. Recovering G needs a collection of event profiles whose convolution design has adequate rank over the target lags/frequencies. This is the precise sense in which volcanic events can inform transient response while sparse eruptions cannot guarantee century-scale equilibrium identification.

Nonlinear version. For a pre-event state distribution mu and a fixed forcing pattern, define

    R_j(a;mu) = [E_mu(Y_{t+j} | do(U_t=+a))
                 - E_mu(Y_{t+j} | do(U_t=-a))] / (2a).           (6)

This is an exact finite-amplitude causal contrast even with hidden states and nonstationary/noisy dynamics, provided assignment is randomized and the same baseline distribution is used. If the conditional mean response m_j(a) is differentiable, (6) tends to m'_j(0) as a tends to zero. If |m'''_j(a)|<=M_3 on [-a,a], its central-difference bias is at most M_3 a^2/6. Without such a smoothness bound, report the finite-amplitude contrast and its dependence on a; do not label it an infinitesimal feedback.

3. CONSERVATION-CONSTRAINED MULTISCALE RESPONSE

Apply (3) jointly to T, H, and N. Equation (1) implies, for randomized perturbations and their ensemble-mean causal responses,

    dG_H(t,s)/dt = G_N(t,s),                                    (7)

or, in discrete time, the corresponding finite difference of the identified heat-inventory response equals the net-flux response, up to the chosen sampling/averaging operator. This identity is a stringent closure diagnostic for forcing calibration, TOA measurements, heat inventory, and time alignment. It does not turn H into a proxy for T: flux/heat closure alone leaves temperature feedbacks and the distribution among hidden reservoirs unresolved.

If the response is stationary and linear, write the causal impulse kernel as g_T(t), so that for an input history f,

    Delta T(t) = integral_0^infinity g_T(s) f(t-s) ds.         (8)

The conservation result also gives a dynamic, data-defined feedback quantity. With F the imposed ERF channel, let G_T(s)=Delta T(s)/F(s) and G_H(s)=Delta H(s)/F(s) be the transfer functions. The restoring radiative response is R=F-N, so its transfer is G_R=1-G_N=1-sG_H by (1). Wherever G_T(s) is nonzero, define

    Lambda_F(s) = G_R(s)/G_T(s) = [1-sG_H(s)]/G_T(s).         (9)

This has units W m^-2 K^-1 and is exactly the frequency-dependent restoring response per unit surface-temperature response along this forcing path. It is not assumed constant, instantaneous, or universal across forcing patterns. If several hidden states make R depend on state history rather than T alone, Lambda_F remains an identified ratio for this input/output path; it does not imply a universal feedback law for interventions in other directions. The ratio should only be reported on excited frequencies where G_T is distinguishable from zero. At equilibrium, if the system is stable and G_T(0) is finite, G_N(0)=0 and Lambda_F(0)=1/G_T(0). Thus finite-frequency restoring and heat-uptake contributions can be separated from observations, while the DC value retains the same infinite-tail requirement.

Thus a unit step f(t)=F0 1_{t>=0} gives Delta T(t)=F0 integral_0^t g_T(s) ds. For an arbitrary ramp/pulse/scenario, convolution with the experimentally identified kernel gives its linear response without a fitted feedback value. If g_T is integrable and the same regime remains stable under the perturbation, the equilibrium gain is

    chi_eq = integral_0^infinity g_T(s) ds = G_T(0),
    ECS(pattern) = F_2xCO2 chi_eq.                             (10)

The forcing pattern qualifier matters: equal global-mean ERF does not imply equal response for spatially distinct forcing patterns. For a twofold-CO2 metric, the imposed CO2 concentration/forcing pathway itself is the cleanest target; a volcanic aerosol response is not automatically interchangeable with it.

For a log-linear CO2 forcing ramp reaching F_2x at t_d, the temperature at t_d is

    T(t_d) = (F_2x/t_d) integral_0^{t_d} (t_d-s) g_T(s) ds
            = (F_2x/t_d) integral_0^{t_d} S_T(v) dv,           (11)

where S_T(v)=integral_0^v g_T(s)ds is the unit-step response. A prescribed averaging window around t_d is handled by averaging (8). This defines TCR-like/transient metrics from the response kernel and does not identify (9) unless the unobserved tail is controlled.

4. CONSTRUCTIVE OPTIMAL OBSERVATION PROTOCOL

For a scalar target forcing mode, a model ensemble or genuinely repeatable experimental ensemble can use M independent, matched baseline realizations. Randomly assign half to +a and half to -a in a single forcing pulse; measure T, TOA N, and total heat inventory H over the full desired lag window. Pair common initial conditions across the +a/-a branches where available, but retain independent randomization. For each lag,

    Ghat_j = (mean(Y^+_j)-mean(Y^-_j))/(2a).                    (12)

Under local linearity and independent homoscedastic branch errors of variance sigma_j^2, Var(Ghat_j)=sigma_j^2/(M a^2) for M total equally split branches: each arm has M/2 members, so the variance of its mean difference is 4 sigma_j^2/M, then division by (2a)^2 gives the formula. Under |U|<=a and the same noise model, symmetric endpoint assignment maximizes Var(U)<=a^2 and hence Fisher information M Var(U)/sigma_j^2 for the slope; it attains the minimum variance sigma_j^2/(M a^2). With paired common initial conditions, replace sigma_j^2 by the variance of the paired difference divided by two according to the actual pairing covariance; estimate that covariance rather than assuming cancellation. Run several amplitudes to expose nonlinear response and/or estimate the central-difference bias. A pulse returns all lags from one assignment; this is more efficient than separate one-lag experiments.

For multiple spatial forcing patterns, use orthogonal randomized sign sequences (or random-phase multisines) with an input design matrix whose Gram matrix U^T U is well conditioned. In the homoscedastic linear regression Y=UG+epsilon with fixed total input energy, an isotropic Gram matrix maximizes det(U^T U): if the nonnegative eigenvalues of U^T U have fixed sum, their product is maximized when they are equal (AM-GM). This also minimizes the largest coefficient variance and prevents hidden forcing directions from being mistaken for zero climate response. Select and report the lowest excited frequency from the slowest target process: no design can estimate a century-scale gain from only interannual power. A pilot ensemble can estimate internal-noise spectra; allocate repeat runs to the frequencies that dominate the target scenario and uncertainty.

Suggested model protocol: at a fixed control climate, randomize a small forcing code around baseline in paired initial-condition branches, using the exact forcing mode of interest (for example CO2 concentration or a diagnosed ERF field). Record global and regional temperature, CERES-equivalent TOA flux, full-depth OHC plus atmosphere/land/ice storage, and forcing diagnostics on one time base. Use (3)/(5), estimate the kernel nonparametrically first, and test (7). Only then fit a compact EBM if it predicts held-out forcing waveforms. A real-Earth natural-event analogue would use several exogenous event episodes and event-resolved forcing estimates with a predeclared baseline/control window; it must retain event-specific forcing uncertainty and initial-state spread.

5. SHARP FINITE-HORIZON LIMIT: AN ENERGY-BALANCED SLOW-FEEDBACK COUNTERMODEL

Use surface temperature T_s, an unobserved deep-ocean temperature T_d, and a slow radiative-feedback state z (e.g. a cloud/albedo feedback memory). Forcing F is in W m^-2; temperatures are K; heat capacities C_s,C_d are W yr m^-2 K^-1; lambda_0,kappa,b are W m^-2 K^-1; tau is years:

    C_s Tdot_s = F - lambda_0 T_s - kappa(T_s-T_d) + b z,
    C_d Tdot_d = kappa(T_s-T_d),
    tau zdot = T_s-z.                                           (13)

With t measured in years and C_s,C_d in W yr m^-2 K^-1, H=C_s T_s+C_d T_d is stored energy in W yr m^-2 (multiply by seconds per year to express J m^-2); its derivative with respect to years has units W m^-2. Summing the first two equations gives

    Hdot = N = F - lambda_0 T_s + b z.                          (14)

So this countermodel obeys global first-law closure exactly; z is a slow radiative feedback state, not an uncounted heat reservoir. At a stable equilibrium under a constant F, T_s=T_d=z and

    chi_eq = 1/(lambda_0-b),   ECS = F_2x/(lambda_0-b),          (15)

provided 0<=b<lambda_0. The hidden ocean and feedback rates are not observable from T_s alone without intervention, but the randomized input/output experiment identifies their combined kernel.

For this same system, the dynamic restoring identity in (9) gives Lambda_F(s)=lambda_0-b/(1+tau s), since z(s)/T_s(s)=1/(1+tau s) and F-N=lambda_0 T_s-bz. The high-frequency limit is lambda_0; the zero-frequency value is lambda_0-b. In the b=0.8 example these are 1.3 and 0.5 W m^-2 K^-1. This is the multiscale consequence: high-frequency perturbations can determine the fast restoring response while missing the slow feedback that sets equilibrium gain.

For an illustrative calculation, set the standard two-layer values C_s=8, C_d=100, lambda_0=1.3, kappa=0.7 from Geoffroy et al.'s analytical CMIP5 EBM illustration, and choose (for demonstration only) tau=300 years. Compare b=0 with b=0.8; both are asymptotically stable, with lambda_0-b=0.5 in the latter. Drive the system by the idealized log-linear forcing ramp F(t)=3.9 t/t_d W m^-2, where t_d=ln(2)/ln(1.01)=69.66 years is the doubling time for a 1% annual CO2 increase and forcing is proportional to ln(CO2). Compute the 20-y mean of T_s over [t_d-10,t_d+10]. The script gives:

    b=0:   ECS=3.0 K;     20-y mean=1.958 K
    b=0.8: ECS=7.8 K;     20-y mean=2.033 K

The equilibrium response changes by 160% while this transient diagnostic changes by about 0.075 K (3.8%). The slowest eigen-timescale in the b=0.8 system is about 952 years. This is an internally computed counterexample, not a fit to Earth or an empirical claim about plausible cloud feedbacks. It shows why accurately identifying a 70-y ramp response plus energy closure cannot, by itself, identify a multi-century equilibrium tail. As b approaches lambda_0 from below, ECS diverges while every fixed finite-time response remains finite and converges to a finite marginal-limit trajectory; the zero-frequency limit is nonuniform.

More generally, an added stable response mode g_slow(t)=(a/tau) exp(-t/tau), t>=0, has exactly a extra DC gain, but its unit-step contribution by horizon T is a(1-exp(-T/tau))<=aT/tau. For tau >> T it is nearly invisible in every finite-window temperature response while its equilibrium gain is still a. This response-mode formula is an algebraic illustration; (13)-(15) supply the energy-balanced feedback/ocean realization for the reported finite experiment. First-law closure is not a tail bound: Hdot is the measured N, but early N can remain close to F while hidden inertia/feedback postpones temperature adjustment.

6. FAILURE MODES, ALTERNATIVES, AND BOUNDARIES

Passive natural variability does not identify an arbitrary forcing-response map. In a stable state-space model x_{t+1}=Ax_t+w_t, y_t=Cx_t, the unforced distribution/covariance depends on A,C and the noise law, but not on the unassigned forcing vector B in x_{t+1}=Ax_t+B u_t+w_t. Two systems with identical A,C,w and different B have exactly the same natural variability and different G_j=CA^{j-1}B. Thus no statistic of passive y alone can distinguish those forced responses without a structural relation between variability and forcing. If internal stochastic radiative noise is known to enter through exactly the same B as the target intervention, a model-specific FDT-like shortcut may work; that shared-channel premise is the information intervention removes.

FDT alternatives were considered. Leith's climate FDT theorem is valid for a restricted dynamical setting; generalized nonlinear FDT formulas still require assumptions about the stochastic dynamics/reference density. These may be valuable when verified for a target model, but they do not provide the intervention-independent identification guarantee in (3). Early-warning alternatives based only on increased variance/autocorrelation also mix changes in restoring dynamics with changes in noise, forcing, or observation error. The intervention kernel can test whether the causal decay rate has changed, but only for modes excited and seen in the chosen outputs.

What the protocol cannot establish: a globally valid nonlinear response for a forcing far outside the tested amplitude; response to a different forcing pattern; stationary/time-invariant kernel across evolving background climates; the unmeasured tail of g_T; a stable equilibrium; or the actual Earth value of ECS from a model ensemble. If the control is drifting, use a time-indexed causal response R(t,s) and baseline stratification; do not pool into a stationary kernel. If forcing has multiple pathways, measure/assign all important channels or report the net effect of the composite event, not a CO2-specific effect.

For a real natural experiment, volcanic eruption timing can be a plausibly exogenous source of variation but the aerosol forcing amplitude, spatial pattern, stratospheric heating/chemistry, and the initial ENSO/ocean state create uncertainty. Use a vector forcing input or state the exclusion assumption; do not treat an eruption dummy as a calibrated scalar forcing by itself. The observational record does not contain a deliberately randomized century-long CO2 pulse. A climate-model intervention is causal within that model, not empirical confirmation that its feedback law is Earth's.

7. PRIMARY COMPARISON AND OBSERVATIONAL STATUS

Leith (1975), “Climate Response and Fluctuation Dissipation,” Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences 32, 2022–2026, DOI 10.1175/1520-0469(1975)032<2022:CRAFD>2.0.CO;2. The primary abstract states that the theorem proven there is for a system with two quadratic integrals such as 2-D/geostrophic turbulence and discusses climate use. This is a passive-fluctuation route under special structure, not the assignment identity (3).

Nicolis & Nicolis (2015), “The Fluctuation–Dissipation Theorem Revisited: Beyond the Gaussian Approximation,” JAS 72, 2642–2656, DOI 10.1175/JAS-D-14-0391.1. Their nonlinear climate-dynamics response derivation assumes intrinsic variability can be modeled by Gaussian Markov noise and derives response using a Fokker–Planck/generalized-potential construction; the paper explicitly says typical responses do not reduce to simple state autocorrelation. This sharpens the passive-response route but still depends on a dynamical/noise model not needed in randomized (3).

Geoffroy et al. (2013), “Transient Climate Response in a Two-Layer Energy-Balance Model. Part I,” J Climate 26, 1841–1857, DOI 10.1175/JCLI-D-12-00195.1. The primary article gives an analytical two-layer EBM, calibrates it using abrupt-4xCO2 CMIP5 experiments, and reports good within-model reproduction of step and linear forcing temperature responses; it also identifies transient TOA imbalance representation as a weakness addressed with an efficacy term in Part II. This demonstrates established value of forced experiments and slow-ocean modes. The present result instead gives a nonparametric intervention estimand and exact heat-budget closure, then explicitly refuses finite-horizon ECS extrapolation without a tail bound. This is a synthesis/candidate identification theorem, not a claim that climate impulse responses or abrupt CO2 experiments are new.

Zanchettin et al. (2019), “Clarifying the Relative Role of Forcing Uncertainties and Initial-Condition Unknowns in Spreading the Climate Response to Volcanic Eruptions,” GRL 46, 1602–1611, DOI 10.1029/2018GL081018. The primary study compares ensembles with common initial conditions but differing realistic volcanic forcing and explicitly quantifies forcing versus initial-condition contributions. It supports the caution that volcanic episodes need both forcing uncertainty and state spread, and that event response is not a clean scalar treatment without design.

Marti et al. (2024), “Monitoring global ocean heat content from space geodetic observations to estimate the Earth energy imbalance,” State Planet 4-osr8, DOI 10.5194/sp-4-osr8-3-2024. This primary observation-method paper compares full-depth/geodetic OHC-derived EEI with CERES TOA flux. It reports a 1993–2022 geodetic ocean heat uptake trend of 0.75 [0.61,1.04] W m^-2 and, over common 2000–2020, EEI trends of 0.37 W m^-2/decade from its geodetic product versus 0.44 [0.34,0.55] W m^-2/decade from CERES; it notes coverage and deep-ocean/systematic uncertainty limits. This establishes that energy-budget observables exist and can cross-check trends, not that their past covariation has identified a forced response kernel.

8. EXACT REMAINING INFERENCE AND CLAIM LEDGER

To infer a local forcing response from data, the remaining empirical requirements are: assigned/exogenous forcing with known waveform and full-rank excitation; repeated draws or a justified stationary IV sequence; stable measurement/coverage for T, N and total H; and amplitude/regime checks for local linearity. To infer scenario response, also require input pattern match and linear superposition/time invariance across that trajectory. To infer ECS, additionally require convergence/stability and a quantitative bound on the unobserved low-frequency response tail, or observe the system long enough that a defensible tail bound follows. No value of this tail is obtained here.

Claim ledger:
* Source-reported: classical restricted climate FDT; nonlinear Markov-noise FDT development; forced CMIP5 abrupt-CO2 EBM calibration; volcanic forcing/initial-condition spread; observational OHC/EEI estimates. Citations above.
* Internally derived: (3)–(5), the energy-budget response identity (7), the scenario and dynamic-feedback formulas (8)–(11), the symmetric paired estimator (12) and its variance, passive-coupling counterexample, and finite-horizon slow-tail construction.
* Finite-tested: script below computes the three-state energy-balanced example and random-input recovery on a synthetic hidden-state linear system.
* Externally established: none for the candidate theorem as a climate-research contribution; the algebra is internally proved and standard randomized system identification is related prior art.
* Empirically confirmed for Earth: none by this work.
* Unresolved: whether the required treatment pattern can be inferred precisely from actual volcanic events; whether local response is invariant across observed climate drift; and any empirical bound on century-to-millennial feedback tails.
