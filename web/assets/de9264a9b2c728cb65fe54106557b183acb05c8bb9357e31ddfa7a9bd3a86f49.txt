UNKNOWN PREDICTIVE-DICTIONARY ACQUISITION: AN OBSERVATIONAL IMPOSSIBILITY
Date: 2026-10-08
Status: exact no-go theorem for dictionary identity under full trajectory access; constructive rational counterexample; no claim that prediction itself is impossible.

RESULT

Removing the supplied feature dictionary changes the problem qualitatively. A trajectory law can identify observable predictions and, under finite-rank conditions, a predictive function space; it need not identify which hidden basis the environment designer called “the dictionary.” I give two distinct rational orthonormal dictionaries, separated by a constant after sign/permutation matching, each with coherence zero and a two-sparse coefficient vector with a fixed nonzero margin. Both represent exactly the same conditional mean and induce exactly the same complete data law. Therefore every passive full-data algorithm and every adaptive scalar-trajectory-query algorithm has identical transcript law in both worlds. Under an equal prior, its probability of recovering the intended dictionary is at most 1/2, even with unlimited exact observations and unlimited precision.

This is stronger than a finite sample lower bound: source data contain zero information about the dictionary label. A later task can make the label operational. If its target is the first atom of the unknown dictionary, a source-only transferred predictor has mean squared error at least 1/65 under the uniform two-world prior. Target-task data can resolve the label; source-task validation cannot. The result does not rule out learning the observable conditional mean, a canonical basis chosen by the learner, or the predictive subspace. Those are different objectives.

1. LEGAL ORACLE AND UNKNOWN OBJECT

Fix N>=2. On each independent time step the environment emits a context

  X=(R_1,...,R_N),    R_i iid uniform on {-1,+1},

and a scalar future target Y=R_1. This is a one-state, finite-alphabet, memoryless process with 2^N possible context/target tuples, since Y is redundant given X. A raw-trajectory oracle returns iid samples or finite strings from this fixed law. A legal scalar query may name any bounded function g of a finite observed trajectory and receives either an independent sample g(path), a finite-precision empirical mean from fresh paths, or even the exact population mean E[g]. Query choices may adapt to prior answers. The oracle exposes no dictionary label, hidden state, or teacher answer. This is a deliberately generous observation interface; the full sample path is at least as informative as any scalar function of it.

The unknown object is an intended predictive dictionary D and a coefficient vector theta satisfying

  E[Y | X] = sum_j theta_j phi_j(X).

The task asks for the environment's intended atom functions (up to the usual signed permutation equivalence), not merely any representation that predicts Y. If the task instead accepts any functionally equivalent basis, the impossibility below is irrelevant by definition.

2. TWO DICTIONARIES WITH THE SAME FULL DATA LAW

Define two orthonormal bases on the first two coordinates:

  D_A:
    phi_1 = (3 R_1 + 4 R_2)/5,
    phi_2 = (-4 R_1 + 3 R_2)/5;

  D_B:
    psi_1 = (5 R_1 + 12 R_2)/13,
    psi_2 = (-12 R_1 + 5 R_2)/13.

For j=3,...,N, both dictionaries contain R_j. Because the R_i are independent, centered, and unit variance, each dictionary is orthonormal in L2(P_X); its pairwise coherence is zero and its condition number is one. Each atom is bounded in absolute value by 7/5 (D_A) or 17/13 (D_B). The two dictionaries are not related by column signs or permutations. Their minimum Frobenius distance after the best signed column matching is sqrt(8/65), a positive constant.

The exact same target Y=R_1 is two-sparse in both dictionaries:

  Y = (3/5) phi_1 - (4/5) phi_2
    = (5/13) psi_1 - (12/13) psi_2.

Thus both worlds have exactly s=2 active atoms, with every active coefficient magnitude at least 5/13. They have strong within-dictionary incoherence, perfect conditioning, bounded atoms, and a constant coefficient margin. Yet the environment emits exactly the same (X,Y) law in both worlds. The dictionary label A/B is a convention in the latent description and has no effect on any source observation.

The identity behind the example is an orthogonal change of coordinates in the active two-dimensional predictive subspace. For any invertible basis change T, an identical function vector admits the transformed coefficients T^(-1)theta. Sparsity can vary with the basis; in this example both choices happen to remain exactly two-sparse. A one-target dataset cannot decide which basis was intended. Incoherence and nonzero coefficient separation alone do not fix this gauge.

3. IMPOSSIBILITY THEOREM FOR ADAPTIVE SCALAR AND FULL-DATA LEARNERS

Theorem. Let W be uniform on {A,B}, selecting the intended dictionary above, and let the legal source oracle expose only samples or scalar functionals of the source trajectory law. For every adaptive learner, every query budget q, every response precision, and every decoder Dhat,

  Pr[Dhat = D_W modulo signed permutation] <= 1/2.

Equivalently, the transcript mutual information I(W; transcript)=0. The same conclusion holds for a learner given the complete infinite source trajectory, since its law is P under both W=A and W=B.

Proof. Couple the two worlds to use the same infinite source sequence (X_t,Y_t). They have the same law, and the dictionary label does not change the sampled sequence. Give the learner the same private randomness in both coupled worlds. Its first query is identical; the scalar response is identical under the coupling (including if it is an exact population expectation). Inductively, after any identical transcript, its next adaptive query and response remain identical. The final output is therefore the same in both worlds. Since D_A and D_B are not signed permutations of one another, the output cannot equal both. Under the equal prior the success probability is at most one half. The transcript is independent of W, giving I(W;transcript)=0.

This bound matches passive against full-data access in the strongest possible sense: neither receives any information about W. Adaptivity, unlimited query count, unlimited observation precision, and access to all raw source coordinates cannot change the result. If the learner is asked only to return a predictor for the source conditional mean, it can output R_1 and attain zero error; the theorem concerns recovery of a particular latent dictionary representation.

4. LATER-TASK CONSEQUENCE

Make the dictionary operational by specifying a later task whose target is its first atom:

  Y^(2) = phi_1(X) if W=A, and Y^(2) = psi_1(X) if W=B,

with X still uniform on {-1,+1}^N. This task rule is shared across worlds (“predict atom 1 of the same latent dictionary”), but task 1 never exposes W. The first atoms differ in L2(P_X):

  E[(phi_1-psi_1)^2] = 4/65.

For any source-only prediction rule f(X), averaging squared error over W=A/B yields

  (1/2) E[(f-phi_1)^2 + (f-psi_1)^2]
      = E[(f-(phi_1+psi_1)/2)^2] + (1/4)E[(phi_1-psi_1)^2]
      >= 1/65.

So no amount of source-task scalar sensing or source holdout validation can guarantee later-task MSE below 1/65 over this two-world family. If later task semantics are basis-invariant and the target is the same observable function, D_A and D_B are equivalent for use and no dictionary recovery is needed. If later behavior depends on atom identity, new task data or an external dictionary convention is necessary.

Under a concrete noisy validation model, suppose the later task supplies iid X and observes Y^(2)+N(0,sigma^2). The KL divergence per labeled example between the two worlds is

  E[(phi_1-psi_1)^2]/(2 sigma^2) = 2/(65 sigma^2).

Hence any test deciding W with error <=delta under each world requires

  n_val >= (65 sigma^2/2) kl(1-delta || delta)

by the data-processing inequality. This lower bound is for acquiring the task-specific latent atom choice from target-task labels. It does not include feature-evaluation or model-coding costs. In the noiseless real-valued version, one discriminating labeled context suffices; that new label is precisely the information absent from task 1.

5. COST LEDGER: WHAT CAN AND CANNOT BE CHARGED

Dictionary coding. The two examples have compact rational descriptions: four small numerator/denominator pairs plus the shared coordinate rule. If the two-world candidate set is public, identifying the intended basis requires one side-information bit. Source transcripts convey zero bits about that choice. For a general finite dictionary codebook of size M whose members induce the same process law, exact identity requires log2 M bits of external label information in the worst case, or under a uniform prior; no source-sample or scalar-query code can replace it. If feature functions are only specified by an arbitrary program, charge that program length L(D), coefficient coding, and the signed-permutation convention. A canonical basis selected from the data is a different code/object from the environment's unknown intended basis.

Sensing. One source example costs N Rademacher context bits plus the target symbol (or N context features and one response if the context has another encoding). A length-T trajectory costs its full T-symbol payload and reset/collection time. A scalar query also costs its query-description bits, response precision bits, and any fresh samples used to estimate its expectation. In this theorem every such source response has zero mutual information about W, so spending more sensing cost does not improve exact dictionary identification. Raw features can help learn source prediction: here R_1 is observed directly, so a one-feature predictor is immediate. That is useful prediction, not recovery of D_A or D_B.

Validation. Any held-out source sample or source-law statistic has the same distribution in both worlds. It can certify source prediction error for R_1, but cannot certify which basis was intended. Validation of later atom-1 use requires labeled examples from task 2 or side information tying the feature index to a public definition; its sample cost is additional. A pass on source held-out data is not evidence of dictionary identity.

Storage and use. Store the selected basis code, coefficient vector, feature-evaluation program, precision, and provenance only after the dictionary is identified or a canonicalization rule is declared. For source prediction, the compact invariant model Y=R_1 costs O(log N) bits (or one feature identifier under a shared feature schema) and has constant-time prediction once R_1 is read. This dominates storing an arbitrary two-atom factorization for this source task. For later tasks keyed to atom identity, store the dictionary plus its code convention and revalidate on target data. The estimated MSE floor 1/65 is a task-shift risk if that validation/data are absent.

A complete experiment would log source and target transitions, reset costs, query encodings and replies, feature-evaluation operations, dictionary/program bits, coefficient bits, validation examples, and downstream risk. An adaptive algorithm cannot claim acquisition advantage against a passive/full-data baseline on this instance: both have zero information about the latent label; the fair and feasible source-task comparator simply returns the invariant predictive function R_1.

6. WHAT CONDITIONS COULD CHANGE THE CONCLUSION

At minimum, exact dictionary identity needs an identifiable generative contract beyond single-task predictive fit. Possible repairs are: (i) quotient the answer by function-space equivalence and signed permutations, or impose a canonical basis convention; (ii) observe multiple tasks/outputs whose coefficient vectors are jointly sparse and sufficiently diverse, with a fixed normalization and a uniqueness/separation condition; or (iii) provide interventions or target-specific measurements whose laws differ between candidate dictionaries. Each changes the interface or target.

Classical dictionary-learning guarantees are relevant but do not prove the missing predictive result. Spielman, Wang and Wright study vectors Y=A X from many samples with a fixed square dictionary and a random sparse coefficient matrix; their identifiability and ER-SpUD theorem uses that multi-sample coefficient-generation structure. Arora, Ge and Moitra handle incoherent overcomplete dictionaries under stochastic assumptions on sparse codes. A single conditional-mean function with unknown basis is not their observation model. The counterexample shows that atom incoherence, basis conditioning, separation between dictionary hypotheses, and a nonzero active-coefficient margin are not sufficient by themselves; one needs restrictions that make the cross-sample/tasks coefficient distribution informative about the basis or a canonicalization.

For predictive processes, a safer learnable object is often the observable law or its finite-dimensional predictive subspace. Hankel/linear realization methods can recover a realization from prefix probabilities under a supplied witness-horizon/rank and conditioning promise, but minimal realization coordinates remain basis-dependent. The related unknown-rank/witness-horizon leaf in this programme gives such a conditional subspace route; it does not recover the generating latent observable dictionary.

Claim status:

* Exact two-dictionary construction: internally proved by direct algebra; no simulation is needed.
* Zero-information adaptive/passive impossibility: exact consequence of equality of the complete observed process law.
* Cross-task MSE floor and Gaussian validation KL: direct derivations shown above.
* Established prior art: dictionary-learning identifiability under multi-sample sparse-code assumptions; minimal state/linear realizations modulo coordinate changes.
* Unknown: any positive teacher-free algorithm for discovering a noncanonical predictive-observable dictionary from a general trajectory oracle; no novelty or external correctness review is claimed.
