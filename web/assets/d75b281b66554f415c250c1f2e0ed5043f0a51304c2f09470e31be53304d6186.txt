Status: the finite critical-radius endpoint/saturation conversion is valid
for all sufficiently large n, with constants depending only on c0,c1. The
two lower-bound regimes meet without an epsilon gap. Exact saturation at
Q=n+1 matches an Omega(n) lower bound. For the unknown-radius corollary,
the exact transition scales contain c1 and are suppressed in the displayed

c1/sqrt(n), add the constant-branch endpoint range described below.

1. Normalize the accuracy parameter

Write kappa=r sqrt(n) in [c0,c1], x=n epsilon, and

  H = 1 + log(r^2/epsilon) = 1 + log(kappa^2/x).

The fine-accuracy law is stated on 0<epsilon<r^2, so H>1. The target order is

  F = min{n+1, sqrt(n H)}.

Let C_T=8192/3, c_T=exp(-2 c1^2/3)/(384 c1), and

  c_* = c_T exp[-9 C_T/(4 c0^2)],  K=log(c_T/c_*)=9 C_T/(4 c0^2)>0.

These are fixed positive constants once c0,c1 are fixed. In fact c_*<c0^2.
To check this, put z=c0^2 and use c1>=c0:

  c_*/c0^2 <= exp[-(2/3)z-6144/z]/(384 z^(3/2)) < 1.

For 0<z<=1 the right side is increasing and is below 1 at z=1; for z>=1,
discarding exp(-6144/z) leaves a bound below 1. Thus x<c_* implies
epsilon<r^2 throughout the critical interval, and the fine-accuracy split
at x=c_* is inside the claimed fine-accuracy domain.

2. Tail-converse inversion below x=c_*

For epsilon<c_*/n, (5.2) rules out every successful branch budget
Q<=Q0=ceil(1/r). For sufficiently large n, Q0<=n/128, so Q>Q0 puts every
remaining candidate in the lower-bound setup of (5.1) whenever Q<=n/128.
If such a code has error at most epsilon, then

  epsilon >= (c_T/n) exp(-C_T Q^2/n),
  Q >= sqrt[(n/C_T) log(c_T/x)].

If instead Q>n/128, it already has a linear-in-n lower bound. Combining the
two cases gives the valid real-valued bound

  Q >= min{n/128, sqrt[(n/C_T) A]},   A=log(c_T/x).

The strict inequality Q>n/128 in the second case only strengthens this
display. The inversion is legal because x<c_*<c_T, hence A>K>0.

To compare its logarithm to the claimed one uniformly in kappa, note

  A = H + delta(kappa),   delta(kappa)=log(c_T/kappa^2)-1.

Let D=max_{kappa in [c0,c1]} |delta(kappa)|, which is finite. Since H>=1,
A<=H+D<=(1+D)H. Also A>=K, and H<=A+D<=A(1+D/K). Therefore A and H
are comparable above and below by positive constants depending only on
c0,c1. This proves

  Q = Omega_{c0,c1}( min{n+1, sqrt(n H)} )

on x<c_*. For the last comparison, n/128 is a fixed fraction of n+1, and
replacing n by n+1 in the cap changes the order by at most a factor 2 for
n>=1. This argument handles arbitrarily small epsilon: as H grows past
order n, the square-root term is capped and the lower bound remains
Omega(n).

3. The fixed-Q theorem covers c_*<=x<kappa^2

For c_*<=x<kappa^2, equivalently c_*/n<=epsilon<r^2, the fixed-Q
consequence (1.2) says for any successful code

  Q >= min{C r/epsilon, 1/(32r)}.

Because epsilon<r^2, r/epsilon>1/r. Thus

  Q >= min{C,1/32}/r >= [min{C,1/32}/c1] sqrt(n).

Meanwhile

  1 <= H <= 1+log(c1^2/c_*),

so H is bounded by a constant depending only on c0,c1. For sufficiently
large n the cap is inactive and F=Theta_{c0,c1}(sqrt(n)). The upper bound
e<=C_up r/Q from the same code family reaches error epsilon with
Q=O(r/epsilon)=O(sqrt(n)) because epsilon>=c_*/n and kappa<=c1. Hence this
middle interval also has Q=Theta_{c0,c1}(F). It joins the tail interval at
x=c_*; no open endpoint is omitted.

4. Transition at epsilon=r^2 and saturation

At epsilon=r^2, H=1 and the fine expression tends to sqrt(n), up to its
inactive cap for large n. The coarser accuracy inversion from (1.2) and
e<=C_up r/Q gives Q=Theta(r/epsilon) for r^2<=epsilon<=r: indeed
y=r/epsilon lies in [1,1/r], so both terms in
min{C y,1/(32r)} are at least a fixed constant times y, and the upper
construction uses Q=O(y). At the shared endpoint, r/epsilon=1/r, which is
Theta(sqrt(n)) uniformly because kappa is in [c0,c1]. Thus the coarse and
fine laws cover the transition with constants depending only on c0,c1.

The displayed fine formula reaches its cap when

  1+log(r^2/epsilon) >= (n+1)^2/n,

or equivalently log(r^2/epsilon)>=n+1+1/n. This is an exponentially small
epsilon scale. The tail converse supplies Omega(n) once the logarithm is
order n (via either its square-root branch or Q>n/128), and the exact
Q=n+1 archive supplies the matching upper bound for every epsilon>0.
There is no additional transition to prove at the saturation point. The
constants in the crossover between the square-root branch and the linear
alternative need not match: both are Theta(n) in that constant-times-n
window, which is all the claimed order law requires.

5. Uniformity and asymptotic qualifications

All constants used above are uniform over the actual radius parameter
kappa in the compact interval [c0,c1]. They can be very small or large as
functions of c0,c1; no absolute numerical constant uniform over arbitrary
intervals is claimed. The phrases “sufficiently large n” are material:
n must be large enough that r<=1/2, Q0<=n/128, and the bounded-H middle
interval is below the linear cap. Since c0,c1 are fixed, one n0(c0,c1)
suffices. No assumption that epsilon is polynomially small is used; the
tail inversion and exact-recovery cap cover all 0<epsilon<r^2.

6. Unknown-radius ball wording in Section 7

For the ball ||b||<=c1/sqrt(n), the radius-blind upper code is controlled
by its largest allowed radius R=c1/sqrt(n). Restricting any decoder to the
boundary sphere ||b||=R gives the matching lower bound (apply the fixed
radius theorem with kappa=c1). Thus the exact transition formulas are

  Q=Theta(c1/(sqrt(n) epsilon)),  c1^2/n<=epsilon<=c1/sqrt(n);

  Q=Theta(min{n+1, sqrt[n(1+log(c1^2/(n epsilon)))]}),
             0<epsilon<c1^2/n.

Suppressing fixed c1 factors yields the canonical n^-1 and n^-1/2 scales
printed in Section 7; the lower transition neighborhood is still
Theta(sqrt(n)) because its logarithm is bounded there. If Section 7 is
intended to state the law over the full original accuracy domain
0<epsilon<=R, then for c1>1 the interval
n^-1/2<epsilon<=c1 n^-1/2 lies beyond the printed coarse interval. It is
not a substantive gap: there Q=Theta(1), by the coarse law and Q>=1, but
it should be stated or explicitly included in “up to envelope-dependent
constants at the transition.” If only epsilon<=n^-1/2 is intended, the
printed regimes cover that restricted domain. The displayed log
log(1/(n epsilon)) should be read only in its printed fine regime
epsilon<n^-1; outside it may be nonpositive, while the exact boundary-scale
formula above remains well-defined.
