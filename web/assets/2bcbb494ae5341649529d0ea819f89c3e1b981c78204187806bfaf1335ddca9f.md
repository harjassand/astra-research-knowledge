# Circuit reliability under a hard molecular throughput cap

## Verdict

I reconstructed a correct stochastic-ordering theorem for a finite-cap protein birth-death model. Its practical reading is crisp: if the design objective is to keep one protein above a copy-number threshold, then at a fixed *peak* translation-completion capacity and fixed protein-loss rate, constitutive expression at the full capacity is the strongest possible count-distribution baseline. Any causal feedback policy with the same cap has at least as much stationary low-copy probability.

This is **not a defensible novelty claim**. The main coupling is already present in Hemberg and Barahona’s gene-regulatory-network perfect-sampling paper: bounded Hill/Monod production propensities are dominated by a first-order immigration-death process. The finite-cap tail formula and its interpretation as a reliability resource floor are a useful corollary, but the argument is an elementary specialization of known process domination. I recommend retaining it only as a scoped design bound or as a rejection of overbroad claims about feedback.

## Exact model and theorem

There is one measured protein count $N_t\in\{0,\ldots,M\}$. The circuit may contain hidden promoter, mRNA, controller, or resource states. A protein birth increments $N$ by exactly one, has predictable conditional intensity $\lambda_t$, and satisfies $0\leq\lambda_t\leq B$ at every time and history. At $N=M$, the accepted birth intensity is zero. Each extant protein is lost independently at constant rate $\gamma>0$. The feedback controller is causal; it can depend on the full past and on hidden molecular states. There are no other upward jumps in $N$, no burst jumps, and feedback does not lower the protein-loss hazard.

Let $X_t$ be the capped immigration-death chain with birth rate $B$ whenever $X_t<M$, and death rate $\gamma X_t$. Couple $N_0=X_0$, use common candidate protein births from a rate-$B$ Poisson random measure and thin them to implement the controlled intensity $\lambda_t$, and couple the deaths of molecules present in both processes. Extra deaths in the larger process preserve the order. This gives

$$
N_t\leq X_t \quad\text{for every }t\geq0\quad\text{almost surely}.
$$

Consequently, for every threshold $h\in\{1,\ldots,M\}$,

$$
\Pr(N_t<h)\geq\Pr(X_t<h).
$$

The comparator is an irreducible finite birth-death chain when $B>0$. Its stationary distribution is

$$
\pi_B(n)=\frac{q^n/n!}{Z_M(q)},\qquad
q=\frac B\gamma,\qquad
Z_M(q)=\sum_{j=0}^{M}\frac{q^j}{j!}.
$$

Since $X_t$ converges to $\pi_B$ from every initial condition, the controlled circuit obeys the long-run marginal floor

$$
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\Pr(N_t<h)
\geq
\frac{\sum_{n=0}^{h-1}q^n/n!}{Z_M(q)}.
$$

For output presence ($h=1$),

$$
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\Pr(N_t=0)\geq Z_M(q)^{-1}\geq e^{-q}.
$$

If $M=\infty$, the comparator is ordinary immigration-death and the tail is the corresponding Poisson tail $e^{-q}\sum_{n<h}q^n/n!$. A target dropout probability $\varepsilon$ for $h=1$ therefore requires $Z_M(B/\gamma)\geq1/\varepsilon$; without a hard cap it requires $B/\gamma\geq\log(1/\varepsilon)$.

### Proof details and proof boundary

Represent controlled births by a Poisson random measure on time and a mark coordinate; accept a candidate at time $t$ iff its mark is below the predictable intensity $\lambda_t$. The comparator accepts every candidate while $X<M$. If $N=X<M$, every controlled birth is also a comparator birth, and the comparator can have extra births. If $N<X$, a controlled birth cannot cross above $X$, including when $X=M$. Couple each death of a molecule counted in both chains; when the counts differ, extra deaths in the larger chain can only shrink the gap. Thus the order is invariant. Detailed balance for the comparator gives $\pi_B(n)B=\pi_B(n+1)\gamma(n+1)$, yielding the truncated-Poisson formula.

The predictable-intensity version allows non-Markov feedback on the protein count, but it does not cover anticipative control, multi-protein burst jumps, feedback that reduces $\gamma$, or other production paths not charged as individual protein completions. If feedback controllers alter growth/dilution, $\gamma$ must be replaced by a verified uniform lower bound on per-protein loss; if no positive lower bound is available, this conclusion does not follow. The cap $M$ and throughput bound $B$ are supplied model premises, not values inferred by the theorem.

## Resource interpretation and strongest matched baseline

If at most $R$ ribosomes can translate this output at once, and each completes proteins at rate at most $\kappa$, then one may set $B=\kappa R$, provided all relevant protein completions are included and the per-ribosome completion hazard is actually bounded by $\kappa$. The design cost is the *peak* throughput capacity $\kappa R$, the loss rate $\gamma$, and the copy ceiling $M$. At this cap, the strongest matched comparator is a constitutive promoter that drives translation at the full allowed completion rate whenever $N<M$. The theorem says feedback cannot improve any low-copy threshold probability against this comparator.

This comparison does not hold fixed average ATP expenditure. The constant-cap comparator can translate more often on average than a controller that suppresses production at high count. It also does not charge the DNA/promoter cost of the controller, free-ribosome sequestration, transcript dynamics, or growth burden. A promoter-level controller with bursts is covered only if its *individual completed protein births* have predictable aggregate intensity at most $B$; bounding transcription initiation alone is insufficient.

## Cost-matched counterexample that limits the claim

For $M=10$, compare two exact finite chains with loss rate $\gamma=1$. A state-dependent process with birth rate $\lambda_n=10-n$ and death rate $n$ has stationary law $\operatorname{Binomial}(10,1/2)$, mean 5, variance 2.5, dropout $1/1024$, and $\Pr(N<5)=193/512\approx0.37695$. A constant-rate process with the same cap and loss has a truncated-Poisson law. Choose its attempted birth rate so its stationary mean is also 5; this is $b\approx5.1041217944$, with the same mean accepted protein production (5 per time unit), dropout $\approx0.00616841$, and $\Pr(N<5)\approx0.42915$. Thus a regulating policy can improve the low tail at the same *average* production cost by using higher peak capacity. It does not contradict the theorem: the regulated policy has peak rate 10, whereas the average-cost-matched constant source peaks at about 5.104. This calculation prevents presenting the theorem as a feedback-versus-constitutive result independent of the chosen resource metric. Reproduce it with [cost_matched_counterexample.py](cost_matched_counterexample.py).

## Prior-art attack and novelty boundary

The decisive prior-art result is Hemberg and Barahona, “Perfect Sampling of the Master Equation for Gene Regulatory Networks,” *Biophysical Journal* 93(2), 401–410 (2007), DOI [10.1529/biophysj.106.099390](https://doi.org/10.1529/biophysj.106.099390). In §2 they require an order-preserving Markov CME and a reversible dominating process with known stationary distribution. In §3, after the Hill and Monod CME equations (their Eqs. 10 and 12), they explicitly use the first-order linear immigration-death process as the dominating process because those birth rates are bounded above by $k$. In §7 they apply the construction to a toggle switch and repressilator. That already establishes the core bounded-rate stochastic domination in a directly relevant gene-circuit setting. The source’s contract is a Markov jump model with order-compatible propensities; it does not state a theorem for arbitrary history-dependent control. Extending the coupling by thinning to predictable intensities is mathematically straightforward, so this extension alone is not a credible priority claim.

Other relevant primary results constrain the interpretation:

- Lee and Lee, “Quantitative analysis of a transient dynamics of a gene regulatory network,” *Physical Review E* 98, 062404 (2018), DOI [10.1103/PhysRevE.98.062404](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevE.98.062404), analyze positive-feedback protein extinction and show how basal production ameliorates it. This is directly aligned with dropout as a reliability event.
- Yong and Gyorgy, “Stability and Robustness of Unbalanced Genetic Toggle Switches in the Presence of Scarce Resources,” *Life* 11(4), 271 (2021), DOI [10.3390/life11040271](https://doi.org/10.3390/life11040271), analyze how transcriptional/translational resource competition lowers toggle-switch potential barriers and raises switching susceptibility. Their potential-based toggle model is not the exact finite-chain theorem above.
- Mugler et al., “Noise Expands the Response Range of the *Bacillus subtilis* Competence Circuit,” *PLOS Computational Biology* 12(3), e1004793 (2016), DOI [10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004793](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1004793), use stochastic modeling and fluorescence measurements to show a case where noise supports dynamic response. Reliability is not always the right objective.
- A recent primary study by Ham, Coomer, Öcal, Grima, and Stumpf, “A stochastic vs deterministic perspective on the timing of cellular events,” *Nature Communications* 15 (2024), DOI [10.1038/s41467-024-49624-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-49624-z), compares first-passage predictions in birth-death, telegraph, and autoregulatory models. It reinforces that a copy-number stationary floor is not a switching-time theorem.

No literature search can prove historical novelty. The direct 2007 overlap is enough to reject a strong novelty claim for the coupling. What remains potentially reusable is a finite-copy threshold formula, an explicit peak-throughput interpretation, and the cost-matched counterexample. These are an internally reconstructed model result, not a biological law.

## Falsifier and next gate

For the abstract theorem, a counterexample would need a causal single-molecule birth process with per-history accepted intensity $\leq B$, fixed independent per-molecule loss $\gamma$, and a hard cap $M$, yet a long-run low-count probability below the capped immigration-death tail. The coupling proof rules this out under the written assumptions. Violations in a proposed biological mapping are experimentally checkable: bursts can exceed the individual-birth intensity cap; product half-life can depend on state; growth/dilution can shift; and translation capacity can fluctuate or be shared with host demand.

A discriminating biological test would require single-cell protein counts, active ribosome/translation-throughput measurements, protein-loss measurements, and a calibrated hard count range for one specified circuit. Compare a feedback construct with a constitutive construct matched for $B$, $\gamma$, and $M$, then separately compare them at equal mean translation cost. If the first comparison shows lower feedback dropout under a verified pointwise cap, the model assumptions or coupling implementation are wrong. If only the equal-average-cost comparison favors feedback, that is consistent with the theorem and is exactly the tradeoff in the finite counterexample. No biological data were collected here.

The theorem and counterexample are **internally reconstructed model results**; the counterexample values are finite computations from exact stationary-law formulas.
