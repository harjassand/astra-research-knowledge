1  Introduction

In an expander graph, the edge boundary of a set of vertices controls its distance from the empty set
or the whole vertex set. Coboundary expansion extends this condition to sets of faces in a simplicial
complex. Over F2, the coboundary of a set of i-faces consists of the (i + 1)-faces containing an
odd number of them. We construct complexes of increasing size for which this boundary controls
distance from the space of coboundaries, uniformly in the size and with a bounded number of
top-dimensional faces at each vertex.

    The distinction between cocycles and coboundaries is essential.  If f ∈ Zi(Y )\Bi(Y ), its
coboundary vanishes while its distance from Bi(Y ) is positive. Thus (1.2)    requires the vanishing
of Hi(Y ;F2) = Zi(Y )/Bi(Y ), with reduced cohomology understood in degree zero. A uniform
inequality againstZi(Y ) alone allows nonzero cohomology. Our proof supplies both that quantitative
inequality and the required vanishing for the same complexes.

Prior work.  The higher-dimensional coisoperimetric viewpoint developed through the random-
complex work of Linial and Meshulam [16] and Meshulam and Wallach [20]. Their models retain a
complete lower skeleton and study the disappearance of cohomology as top-dimensional faces are
added. Gromov connected cohomological filling inequalities with topological overlap and asked for
arbitrarily large complexes with bounded vertex incidence and uniform filling bounds [9, Sections 2.3–
2.5 and 2.14]. The full coboundary expansion problem also demands vanishing cohomology; a recent
formulation is [5, Problem 1.3].
    There are two different degree conditions in this history. Lubotzky and Meshulam constructed
random Latin-square coboundary expanders in dimension two [18]; Lubotzky, Luria, and Rosenthal
constructed random Steiner-system coboundary expanders in every dimension [17]. These results
bound the number of top-dimensional faces through a codimension-one face. The Latin-square
complexes have a complete tripartite 1-skeleton, and the Steiner-system complexes have a complete
(d− 1)-skeleton. In dimensions at least two, vertex degrees grow with the size. The degree bound in
Theorem 1.1is at vertices.
    For bounded vertex degree, Kaufman, Kazhdan, and Lubotzky established cosystolic and
topological expansion for two-dimensional skeleta of Ramanujan complexes [11]. Evra and Kaufman
obtained bounded-degree cosystolic expanders in every dimension through a local-to-global theorem
[7]. The associated expansion methods include the building-like complexes of Lubotzky, Meshulam,
and Mozes [19], the strongly symmetric complexes of Kaufman and Oppenheim [13], the coefficient-
independent methods of Kaufman and Mass [12], and the quantitative local-to-global results of
Dikstein and Dinur [6]. We use only the distance-to-cocycles part of cosystolic expansion, stated
precisely in (5.1).
    Chapman and Lubotzky proved the existence of bounded-degree two-dimensional coboundary
expanders over F2 [5, Theorem 1.9]. In higher ambient dimensions, Kaufman, Oppenheim, and
Weinberger [15], and Oppenheim and Valentiner-Branth [24], proved degree-one coboundary expan-
sion for coset complexes with suitable non-Abelian coefficients. Those degree-one results and the
cosystolic constructions above do not give the vanishing in every degree required byTheorem 1.1.
Together with the two-dimensional result and the graph case, our theorem gives bounded-degree F2
coboundary expanders in every positive dimension.
    Our complexes belong to an established algebraic construction program. Kaufman and Oppen-
heim developed locally spectral expanding coset geometries [14]; O’Donnell and Pratt used Chevalley
groups [22]; and Grave de Peralta and Valentiner-Branth developed Kac–Moody–Steinberg group
constructions [8]. Oppenheim and Valentiner-Branth proved the cosystolic expansion theorem for
these KMS complexes that supplies our quantitative input [23]. Their affine-type-A polynomial
matrix construction [23, Example 4.13] already uses the simple root groups appearing here, with
quotients by irreducible polynomials. Powers of t also occur, with different local groups, in the
degree-one construction of [15, Section 2.5 and Theorem 6.3]. For the local groups used here, we
pass to the tower of quotients modulo tm and prove the cohomology vanishing needed to obtain
coboundary expansion in all degrees below d.

Proof strategy.  Fix d and put n = 2d and N = 2d + 1. Over a sufficiently large fixed odd prime
field k, let U≤ SLN(k[t]) be the inverse image of the upper unitriangular group under evaluation
at zero. We construct an infinite n-dimensional coset complex L for U and its finite congruence
quotients Tm. The desired Xm is the d-skeleton of Tm. The local groups are fixed finite groups,
which gives uniform degree bounds; the congruence quotients grow with m.
    The first step is geometric. We identify L with the subcomplex opposite a chamber in an affine
twin building. A filtration by codistance expresses the relative chain groups of each attachment
in terms of a spherical opposition complex. The opposition-complex input of [23] makes these
attachments acyclic in the required range. Since the whole affine building is contractible, L has
vanishing reduced homology below degree n− 1. Its simplex stabilizers have odd order, so its chain
complex gives a partial projective resolution over F2[U]. The orbit complex U\L is a single simplex,
which yields the corresponding homology vanishing for U.
    The second step passes from U to each congruence subgroup.  Morrow’s pro Tor-unitality
theorem [21] and Iwasa’s homology pro-stability theorem [10] apply to the idealstmk[t]. Their initial
conclusion concerns an inverse system, whereas we need a statement at every level. Odd-index
transfer makes the homology maps towards shallower levels surjective over F2.  The cokernels
of stabilization are therefore both pro-zero and connected by surjective transition maps, so each
cokernel vanishes. Two stabilization steps then show that ambient conjugation acts trivially in
degrees 1,...,d− 1. Averaging over the finite odd-order quotient transfers the vanishing from U to
the congruence subgroup, and hence to Tm.
    Finally, the KMS theorem gives a uniform inequality against cocycles on the (n− 1)-skeleton of
Tm. Its weighted norms agree with the corresponding norms on Tm because every panel lies in the
same number of chambers. Passing to the d-skeleton requires a further comparison: the number
of chambers through a d-face is bounded above and below, and the normalization contributes the
same factor in two successive cochain degrees. This factor cancels in the expansion inequality. The
cohomology vanishing replaces cocycles by coboundaries and completes the proof.

Organization.  Section2defines the congruence complexes. Section3proves the local group
identities and the acyclicity of L. Section4proves the cohomology vanishing of the finite quotients.
Section5checks the KMS hypotheses, compares skeleton weights, and provesTheorem 1.1.

3.2  The twin-building model

complex in the classical affine twin building of SLN(k[t,t−1]). We use the lattice construction of

and [2]. Its two halves are the affine buildings at 0 and at infinity, both of type ~An and dimension
n. Their chambers have one vertex of each type in S. The stabilizers of opposite base chambers are

                              B+ ={g∈ SLN(k[t]) :g(0) is upper triangular},

and the analogous group B− over k[t−1] with lower triangular reduction at infinity. They satisfy
B+ =U ⋊T and B+∩B− =T, where T is the constant diagonal torus.
    Fix the positive base chamber c+.  A chamber c in the negative building has a codistance
δ∗(c) =δ∗(c+,c)∈W, where (W,S) is the affine Coxeter system. For two chambers in the same half,
their Weyl distance is the element ofW represented by the types of a minimal gallery between them.
Write ℓ for word length with respect to S, and WJ =⟨J⟩ for J⊆S. The chambers of codistance

4.3  Trivial conjugation and vanishing cohomology
Stabilization lets us represent every homology class on any chosen set of N− 2 coordinates. The two
remaining coordinates will make each elementary conjugation act trivially. This is the finite-level
argument corresponding to the stability-to-conjugation principle in [10, Corollary 5.14].
Lemma 4.4. For 1≤j≤d− 1, conjugation by SLN(R) acts trivially on Hj(ΓN(m);F2), for every
m≥ 1.
Proof. Our choice of N gives N− 2  =  2(d− 1) + 1.  By two applications ofLemma 4.3, the
subgroup ΓN−2(m) on the firstN− 2 coordinates surjects ontoHj(ΓN(m);F2) in the stated degrees.

since each such matrix normalizes ΓN(m) and therefore induces an automorphism of its homology.
    Every elementary matrix eab(f) centralizes the congruence subgroup on the coordinates comple-
mentary to a,b. Its conjugation action therefore fixes the image of a surjection onto Hj(ΓN(m);F2),
so that action is trivial. Elementary matrices generate SLN(R) because R is Euclidean, proving the
lemma.

5  Coboundary expansion

The cohomology calculation has removed the obstruction to coboundary expansion. We now obtain
a uniform cosystolic inequality from the KMS construction, verify bounded degree and growth, and
compare the weights on the two skeleta.

5.1  The quantitative input and its hypotheses
We recall the Kac–Moody–Steinberg (KMS) construction in the case needed here. The affine diagram
~An has type set S, and each proper subdiagram is a disjoint union of paths of type A. Its spherical
local groups are the corresponding unitriangular groups UJ, J ⊊S, with their standard inclusions.
Their universal completion is the group obtained by taking the free product of these groups and
identifying each UJ with its image in UK whenever J⊆K. This is the KMS group of type ~An [23,
Definition 4.6].
    A finite quotient of the completion defines a KMS complex if its map is injective on every spherical
local group and preserves their pairwise intersections; the complex is formed from the images of the
maximal local groups by the common-intersection coset construction [23, Definitions 4.8 and 4.11].
A diagram on n + 1 types is n-classical when every proper subdiagram has irreducible components
of classical type Ar, Br, Cr or Dr [23, Definition 4.2]. Thus   ~An is n-classical.
    We use the following part of the theorem of Oppenheim–Valentiner-Branth [23, Theorem 1.2].
