## 1. Main theorem: exact rational-point preservation under smoothing

Theorem. Let \\(f\in\mathbb Q[X_1,\ldots,X_n]\\) be any nonconstant polynomial of degree \\(d\ge1\\). Define the affine hypersurface

\\[ \boxed{ H_f:\quad U^3\bigl(1+f(X_1,\ldots,X_n)^2\bigr)=1. } \\]

Then:

1. \\(H_f\\) is smooth over \\(\overline{\mathbb Q}\\).
2. \\(H_f\\) is geometrically integral.
3.

# Its rational points are exactly \\[ \boxed{ H_f(\mathbb Q)

{(\mathbf x,1):f(\mathbf x)=0,\ \mathbf x\in\mathbb Q^n}. } \\]

1. Its defining polynomial has total degree \\(2d+3\\).
2. The transformation \\(f\mapsto H_f\\) is deterministic polynomial-time in the standard explicit polynomial representation.

Consequently, every affine rational hypersurface can have its entire rational-point set represented, without introducing or losing a single point, inside a smooth geometrically integral affine hypersurface.

This holds even when the original hypersurface is singular, reducible or has no rational points.

### Proof

#### A. The arithmetic mechanism

Consider the elliptic curve

\\[ E:\quad Y^2=X^3-1. \\]

This is Cremona curve 144a1, with Mordell–Weil rank zero and torsion group \\(\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\\). Its complete rational-point set is

\\[ E(\mathbb Q)=\\{O,(1,0)\\}. \\]

The classification is recorded in the LMFDB.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.lmfdb.org\&sz=32)

lmfdb.org



Suppose \\((\mathbf x,u)\in H_f(\mathbb Q)\\). The defining equation implies \\(u\ne0\\), and

\\[ \frac1{u^3}=1+f(\mathbf x)^2. \\]

Set

\\[ X=\frac1u,\qquad Y=f(\mathbf x). \\]

Then

\\[ Y^2=X^3-1. \\]

Because \\(X,Y\in\mathbb Q\\), this is an affine rational point of \\(E\\). The only possibility is \\((X,Y)=(1,0)\\).

Therefore

\\[ u=1,\qquad f(\mathbf x)=0. \\]

Conversely, every rational zero \\(\mathbf x\\) of \\(f\\) gives the rational point \\((\mathbf x,1)\in H_f\\).

This establishes the exact rational-point identity.

#### B. Smoothness

Write

\\[ F(\mathbf X,U)=U^3(1+f(\mathbf X)^2)-1. \\]

Its partial derivative with respect to \\(U\\) is

\\[ \frac{\partial F}{\partial U} =3U^2(1+f(\mathbf X)^2). \\]

At any geometric point satisfying \\(F=0\\),

\\[ U^3(1+f^2)=1, \\]

so

\\[ \boxed{\frac{\partial F}{\partial U}=\frac3U\ne0.} \\]

The Jacobian criterion therefore proves that \\(H_f\\) is smooth over \\(\overline{\mathbb Q}\\).

Crucially, no condition on the critical points of \\(f\\) is required.

#### C. Geometric integrality

Put \\(k=\overline{\mathbb Q}\\) and \\(K=k(X_1,\ldots,X_n)\\).

We show that

\\[ F=U^3(1+f^2)-1 \\]

is irreducible in \\(K[U]\\).

Since this is a cubic in \\(U\\), reducibility would imply the existence of a root \\(r\in K\\). Such a root would satisfy

\\[ r^3=\frac1{1+f^2}. \\]

Setting \\(h=1/r\\), we obtain

\\[ h^3=1+f^2, \\]

or

\\[ f^2=h^3-1. \\]

Thus the rational functions \\(h\\) and \\(f\\) define a rational map

\\[ \mathbb A_k^n\dashrightarrow E_k,\qquad \mathbf x\longmapsto(h(\mathbf x),f(\mathbf x)). \\]

Because \\(f\\) is nonconstant, we may restrict to a suitable affine line on which \\(f\\) remains nonconstant and the rational functions are defined generically. This produces a nonconstant rational map

\\[ \mathbb P_k^1\dashrightarrow E_k. \\]

Since \\(E_k\\) is smooth and projective, that map extends to a morphism. The Riemann–Hurwitz formula gives

\\[ 2g(\mathbb P^1)-2 = \deg(\phi)\bigl(2g(E)-2\bigr)+R, \\]

with \\(R\ge0\\). As \\(g(\mathbb P^1)=0\\) and \\(g(E)=1\\), it would require

\\[ -2=R\ge0, \\]

a contradiction.

Hence \\(F\\) is irreducible over \\(K\\). It is primitive as a polynomial in \\(U\\) over \\(k[\mathbf X]\\), since its constant coefficient is \\(-1\\). Gauss's lemma therefore proves that \\(F\\) is irreducible in \\(k[\mathbf X,U]\\).

Together with smoothness, this establishes geometric integrality.

Finally,

\\[ \deg F=2d+3. \\]

Squaring an explicitly represented polynomial, adding one, and multiplying by \\(U^3\\) are polynomial-time operations in its encoded input length.

The theorem follows. \\(\square\\)

## 2. The stronger structural interpretation

There is more information in the proof than merely preservation of rational solvability.

Let

\\[ B_f=\mathbb A^n\setminus V(1+f^2). \\]

Projection onto the original variables defines

\\[ \pi:H_f\longrightarrow B_f. \\]

Over \\(B_f\\), the polynomial \\(1+f^2\\) is invertible, and

\\[ U^3=(1+f^2)^{-1}. \\]

The derivative in \\(U\\) is invertible everywhere on this cover. Consequently, \\(\pi\\) is a finite étale cover of degree three, indeed a \\(\mu_3\\)-torsor.

Thus the construction establishes a more specialized theorem:

> Arbitrary affine rational hypersurface zero sets can be represented exactly as the rational points of smooth, geometrically integral affine hypersurfaces obtained from degree-three étale covers of principal open subsets of affine space.

In particular, the construction preserves the exact number of rational points, whether zero, finite or infinite.

This is the strongest unconditional connecting theorem I obtained in this investigation. Its proof does not depend on any OpenAI-generated mathematical claim.

## 3. Consequence of OpenAI family 004: bounded-degree smooth rational-point undecidability

OpenAI's manuscript Hilbert's tenth problem over the rational numbers claims that rational solvability of arbitrary integer-coefficient polynomials is undecidable.

Its Section 7 derives two additional statements: the rational-solvability problem has Turing degree \\(0'\\), and its restriction to polynomials of degree at most four retains that degree. The reduction to degree four is a standard arithmetic-circuit construction; the undecidability itself depends on the manuscript's much deeper preceding argument.

Source: OpenAI family 004, original Section 7, pinned revision.

Applying the theorem above to a polynomial of degree at most four gives

\\[ \deg H_f\le2(4)+3=11. \\]

We therefore obtain the following precise conditional consequence.

Corollary, conditional on the correctness of OpenAI family 004. No algorithm decides whether an arbitrary smooth, geometrically integral affine hypersurface over \\(\mathbb Q\\), of total degree at most eleven and unrestricted dimension, has a rational point.

The proof is an effective many-one reduction:

\\[ \mathrm{H10}(\mathbb Q) \ \le_m\ \mathrm{H10}\_{\deg\le4}(\mathbb Q) \ \le_m\ \mathrm{SmoothIntegralAffine}\_{\deg\le11}(\mathbb Q). \\]

The first reduction is the arithmetic-circuit normalization. The second is \\(f\mapsto H_f\\). Constant inputs can be handled separately by fixed smooth geometrically integral hypersurfaces with a rational point or without one.

Under the same assumption, the target decision problem has Turing degree \\(0'\\): the reduction gives hardness, while enumeration of rational tuples gives the upper bound.

The distinction matters: this does not establish a many-one reduction of the halting problem to \\(\mathrm{H10}(\mathbb Q)\\). OpenAI's Section 7 explicitly distinguishes its Turing reduction from that stronger assertion.

### A further implication already supplied by published mathematics

Bjorn Poonen proved in 2009 that an algorithm deciding rational-point existence on smooth projective geometrically integral varieties over a number field would also decide rational-point existence on arbitrary varieties.

Consequently, if OpenAI family 004 is correct, rational-point existence is also undecidable for smooth projective geometrically integral varieties over \\(\mathbb Q\\).&#x20;

That is a historically consequential implication, but its logical transfer was already established in the literature; it is not a newly discovered bridge.

Nor does it establish the corresponding result for smooth projective hypersurfaces of bounded degree. Projective completion introduces rational points at infinity, and the affine construction above does not prevent them.

## 4. An unconditional undecidability result requiring no OpenAI premise

There is also a separate consequence of the classical Davis–Putnam–Robinson–Matiyasevich theorem.

Theorem. Deciding the existence of an integer point on a smooth, geometrically integral affine hypersurface over \\(\mathbb Q\\) of total degree at most five is undecidable. As a computably enumerable decision problem, it is many-one complete.

### Proof

Given an arbitrary integer polynomial \\(f\\), introduce arithmetic-circuit variables. Its equation \\(f=0\\) becomes a finite system

\\[ q_1(\mathbf Z)=\cdots=q_s(\mathbf Z)=0, \qquad \deg q_i\le2, \\]

with integer solutions equivalent to those of the original equation.

Now construct the single hypersurface

\\[ \boxed{ U\left(1+\sum\_{i=1}^s q_i(\mathbf Z)^2\right)=1. } \\]

For integer inputs,

\\[ 1+\sum_i q_i(\mathbf Z)^2 \\]

is a positive integer. Its product with the integer \\(U\\) equals one exactly when \\(U=1\\) and all \\(q_i=0\\).

Thus integer solvability is preserved.

Let \\(G=1+\sum_iq_i^2\\). The coordinate ring is

\\[ \mathbb Q[\mathbf Z,U]/(UG-1) \cong \mathbb Q[\mathbf Z,G^{-1}]. \\]

It is the coordinate ring of a nonempty principal open subset of affine space. Hence the hypersurface is smooth and geometrically integral. Its degree is at most five.

The reduction is effective. DPRM makes ordinary integer polynomial solvability many-one complete among computably enumerable sets, and integer solutions can be enumerated. The same classification follows for these quintic hypersurfaces. \\(\square\\)

This establishes an exact restricted undecidability theorem without relying on any claim from the October 2026 release.

Its historical novelty is another matter.

The strongest independent mathematical result of this run is the parameter-free cubic étale rational-point detector:

\\[ \boxed{ f(\mathbf x)=0 \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad U^3(1+f(\mathbf x)^2)=1 \quad\text{over }\mathbb Q, } \\]
