# Plasticity and reversible drug tolerance: when bulk response cannot predict schedule control

**Status:** exact counterexample established for a two-state linear model; experimentally measurable discriminator specified; no biological fit or clinical inference. The weak point is narrower than “plasticity matters”: a bulk curve can identify a drug-response spectrum while failing to identify whether its modes are reversible cell states or fixed clones. Those mechanisms can agree exactly on baseline growth and every uninterrupted monotherapy count, yet disagree sharply on an equal-exposure split schedule.

## Bottleneck

Many treatment rules are computed from total burden and an assumed map from drug exposure to drug-sensitive and drug-tolerant fractions. The control decision needs the *transition rates under both drug and withdrawal*, because a holiday can restore sensitivity if cells revert and cannot do so if the resistant fraction is fixed. A one-dose growth curve supplies no such distinction when the transition mechanism is latent.

This is a model-identifiability statement, not a claim that a particular patient’s persisters switch at the rates below. It complements a fixed-clone pulse calculation: if clone-specific drug responses are known and fixed, the aggregate response can recover a clone fraction; when states switch and those transition rates are unknown, fitting the same aggregate curve does not establish the model that should be used for the next schedule.

## Exact counterexample

Let `x=(S,T)^T` be expected counts in sensitive and tolerant states, with continuous-time dynamics `dx/dt = A_u x`, where `u=0` is drug withdrawal and `u=1` is a fixed drug concentration. All rates below are per day and are deliberately illustrative.

In reversible-state model A, the drug-free net growth rate is `g=0.05` in both states. The spontaneous transition rates are `S→T: μ0=0.10` and `T→S: ν0=0.90`. Under drug, sensitive and tolerant net growth rates are `−2` and `0`, while transition rates are `μ1=ν1=0.10`. With column-vector counts,

\[
A_0=\begin{pmatrix}g-\mu_0&\nu_0\\\mu_0&g-\nu_0\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-0.05&0.90\\0.10&-0.85\end{pmatrix},\qquad
A_1=\begin{pmatrix}-2-\mu_1&\nu_1\\\mu_1&-\nu_1\end{pmatrix}
=\begin{pmatrix}-2.10&0.10\\0.10&-0.10\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Start at the drug-free equilibrium `x(0)=(0.9,0.1)^T`, so total untreated abundance is `N(t)=e^{0.05t}N(0)`. For continuous drug exposure, `A1` has eigenvalues `λf≈−2.10498756` and `λs≈−0.09501244`. The total response is

\[
N_A(t)/N_A(0)=0.84826302e^{\lambda_f t}+0.15173698e^{\lambda_s t}.
\]

Now construct model B: two *fixed, non-switching* clones with drug net growth rates `λf, λs`, untreated net growth `g=0.05` for both, and starting proportions `0.84826302, 0.15173698`. Then model B has the same untreated total `e^{0.05t}` and exactly the same total for every duration `t` of uninterrupted drug exposure. The equality is exact up to the displayed rounding: it follows by diagonalizing `A1` and using the two positive modal coefficients as the initial clone counts. No bulk count accuracy or denser time sampling during these two protocols can distinguish A from B.

The hidden compositions are already different. Model A starts with 90% sensitive cells; model B starts with about 84.8% of the fast-drug-decaying clone. After one drug day, model A has sensitive fraction 0.478. A three-day withdrawal raises that fraction to 0.879; model B’s fast-clone fraction after the first drug day is 0.428 and stays there during withdrawal. Yet both models predict the same total through that first on/off pair.

The schedule-level consequence is large. Compare two five-day regimens at the same concentration and total drug exposure of two days:

| Regimen | Days 0–1 | Days 1–2 | Days 2–4 | Days 4–5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Block | Drug | Drug | Withdrawal | Withdrawal |
| Split | Drug | Withdrawal | Withdrawal | Drug |

Both finish at day 5, and both have three withdrawal days. In model A, the block regimen gives `N(5)/N(0)=e^(0.15) N_A(2)/N_A(0)≈0.160416`; the split regimen gives `≈0.072290`. Thus, under these parameters, the split schedule leaves **54.9% fewer viable cells** than the block schedule. In model B, equal clone-specific off-drug growth makes the on/off operators commute, so both schedules give exactly `≈0.160416`. The fixed-clone model predicts no schedule benefit. This is an in-vitro discriminating prediction, not a clinical recommendation.

The reversions themselves are directly measurable. In model A, a T cell starting the three-day withdrawal has probability

\[
P(T\to S;3)=\frac{\nu_0}{\mu_0+\nu_0}\left(1-e^{-(\mu_0+\nu_0)3}\right)
=0.9(1-e^{-3})\approx0.8552,
\]

of occupying S at day 3. A starting S cell has probability `P(S→T;3)=0.1(1−e^(−3))≈0.0950` of occupying T. The static-clone model predicts no within-lineage state conversion. The schedule endpoint is the stronger control test; observed state conversion is the mechanism test.

### General argument

For a two-dimensional linear system under one constant input, if the total output has two real modes,

\[
\mathbf 1^T e^{A_1t}x_0=c_1e^{\lambda_1t}+c_2e^{\lambda_2t},
\]

and `c1,c2≥0`, a diagonal no-switching model with initial clone counts `(c1,c2)` and growth rates `(λ1,λ2)` gives the same output for every `t`. If, off drug, both mechanisms have common net growth `g` and the switching generator conserves cell number, their total untreated curves also coincide. A switched schedule probes the ordered matrix product `e^{A_1t_2}e^{A_0h}e^{A_1t_1}`; a fixed-clone model instead multiplies scalar clone-specific factors. Those products need not produce the same output. The matrices above instantiate a strict separation, so bulk single-pulse fitting is insufficient for a general schedule forecast.

The theorem concerns deterministic expected counts, two states, constant rates within each phase, and a single well-mixed population. The constructed rates do not come from a fitted cell line. It proves non-identifiability under the stated observation interface; it does not prove that all switching models are unidentifiable when phenotype composition, lineage histories, multiple drug concentrations, or state-specific measurements are available.

## Removable discriminator and matched protocol

Use a cancer line or organoid where a live, non-destructive marker has first been shown to track *functional drug sensitivity*, not expression alone. Split one barcoded parental pool at a common density and randomly assign matched lineages to the block and split schedules above. Keep dose concentration, total on-time/AUC, calendar endpoint, vehicle, media exchange, seeding density, passage, imaging, and handling fixed. Include a matched vehicle-only arm to estimate untreated growth and medium-change effects. Count viable cells at days 0, 1, 2, 4, and 5; record the marker and each lineage’s divisions, deaths, and state transitions continuously or at intervals short enough to resolve them. Verify that neither arm acquired stable resistance by rechallenging descendants after a longer drug-free expansion and checking the relevant resistance genotype.

The decisive predictions are pre-registered: (i) among day-1 tolerant-state lineages, the reversible model predicts the observed state fraction returns toward 0.9 during the three-day withdrawal, with the pure-T transition probability `0.855`; the fixed-clone model predicts stable lineage identity; and (ii) the split/block day-5 burden ratio is `0.4506` for model A and `1.0000` for model B. A measured ratio near one rejects the schedule advantage at this parameterization even if markers fluctuate. A ratio near 0.45 together with within-barcode reversion rejects the fixed-clone explanation. A value far from both indicates that the two-state constant-rate models are inadequate or their rates were misspecified.

**Observation bill.** To test the schedule difference alone, the minimum comparison is two matched schedule arms plus the vehicle arm, sampled at a common day-5 endpoint; use at least three independent biological replicates per arm as a practical floor, with variance-based power analysis before the definitive run. To estimate the rates rather than just test the endpoint, direct observation needs state-resolved lineage trajectories in both conditions (drug and withdrawal), plus division/death tracking so selection is not mistaken for switching. A 100-independent-lineage cohort initialized in the tolerant state gives an illustrative precision check: if there are zero T→S conversions in 100 tracked lineages, the one-sided 95% binomial upper bound is about 3%, far below the model-A prediction 85.5%; the probability of zero conversions under model A is about `0.145^100`. Also start a cohort in S to estimate the opposite rate. This is a lower-bound design calculation, not a universal sample-size guarantee; clustering, marker error, and death censoring increase the required count.

If no validated live reporter exists, use barcodes plus state-resolved single-cell RNA profiling on parallel aliquots before treatment, after the first pulse, after withdrawal, and after rechallenge. That is a costlier, destructive substitute: it estimates distributions across sister descendants rather than watching the same cell switch. Report the number of lineages shared across conditions, state-classification error, sequencing depth, cells assayed, and lineages lost to death or low abundance. Costs should be stated in cell-lineage observations, time-lapse hours, aliquots, and single-cell libraries; dollar cost cannot be responsibly specified without a facility/platform quote.

## Falsifier and limits

For this fixed numerical model, the direct falsifier is a blinded, matched estimate whose 95% interval excludes the predicted off-drug conversion probabilities after state-dependent death and division are accounted for. The control claim is separately falsified if the split/block burden ratio does not approach 0.4506 under the measured rates, or if it fails to improve on a matched schedule baseline. Failure of the binary-state marker to predict rechallenge sensitivity falsifies the model’s state definition before the schedule claim is evaluated.

The counterexample omits continuous state variables, multiple persister subtypes, density dependence, cell-cell/stromal interactions, immune effects, spatial structure, mutation or stable conversion to genetic resistance, pharmacokinetics, and treatment toxicity. The model result gives no safe dose, tumor-eradication guarantee, or clinical benefit. A schedule that improves cell-line burden at matched AUC may be unsafe or ineffective in vivo.

## Primary literature and prior-art boundary

- **Reversible tolerance is experimentally established in particular systems.** Sharma et al. reported a drug-tolerant state that can arise after single-cell cloning and reverses after drug withdrawal, with slow interconversion among cancer-cell states: [Cell (2010), “A chromatin-mediated reversible drug-tolerant state in cancer cell subpopulations”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2010.02.027). Shaffer et al. showed rare transcriptional variability in melanoma predicts resistance and that drug exposure can reprogram transient states toward stable resistance: [Nature (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature22794). These support possibility, not the numerical rates used here.
- **Lineage-resolved state changes are measurable.** Harmange et al.’s scMemorySeq study barcoded melanoma cells, expanded them about 7–8 doublings, split matched lineages among conditions, and profiled barcode plus RNA after five days. It reported 19,740 lineages (minimum three cells each), with 49% present in all four conditions; PI3K inhibition shifted lineages out of a primed state and pretreatment reduced resistant colonies in that cell model. The authors explicitly note that population fractions alone confound switching with differential growth or death. [Nature Communications (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-023-41811-8). This is relevant assay precedent, not validation of the proposed schedule.
- **The control dependence on transition mechanism is already prior art.** Gunnarsson, Magnússon & Foo analyze a sensitive/tolerant switching model and prove optimal strategies differ depending on whether drug raises S→T, suppresses T→S, or changes both; they identify joint estimation of drug effects and both transition rates as a needed experiment/model link. Their paper also assumes two direct states, exponential growth without competition, and ignores PK and toxicity. [npj Systems Biology and Applications (published 25 Aug 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41540-025-00571-5). The present contribution is the explicit bulk-observation counterexample and equal-AUC schedule divergence, not the general claim that treatment choice depends on plasticity.
- **Single-cell fate timing may not reduce to a two-state birth/death model.** Iyer et al. combine time-lapse microscopy and population measurements in HCT116 and U2OS cells; their model attributes lineage-correlated fate outcomes to states inherited before and during cisplatin treatment and reports those states can persist for 2–3 cell generations. This warns that instantaneous Markov switching is only a testable simplification. [PLOS Computational Biology (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1013446).
- **Current in-vivo lineage work finds several persister programs.** Li et al. used barcode, single-cell transcriptomic, and spatial assays in melanoma PDX models under BRAF/MEK inhibition, reporting four adaptive transcriptional programs. This is peer-reviewed 2026 evidence of model-specific state diversity, so a two-state fit should be treated as local and falsifiable. [Molecular Cancer (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12943-026-02622-9).
- **Persistence can feed genetic evolution.** Russo et al. used a two-step fluctuation assay in colorectal cancer cell models and estimated a 7–50-fold higher mutation rate in persisters during drug exposure. Therefore even successful short-term state resetting cannot be equated with preventing permanent resistance. [Nature Genetics (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41588-022-01105-z).

**Verdict:** this line yields a useful, exact measurement-design result, not a novel therapy or clinical claim. Bulk response curves—even noiseless, arbitrarily dense monotherapy curves—can fail to identify whether the fitted modes switch; the decisive missing observation is lineage-linked state composition during withdrawal/rechallenge. The numerical schedule separation is large enough to make a clean in-vitro falsification worthwhile if the target system has a validated live state marker. Whether those rates transport across tumor types or improve an in-vivo patient outcome remains `UNKNOWN`.
