# Hostile audit: site-specific accessibility of engineered allostery

## Verdict

The most relevant distinct result is an **experimental counterexample to using insertion tolerance, baseline catalytic activity, or apparent thermal stability as a proxy for single-mutation access to allostery**. Dinan et al. compare two DHFR–LOV2 insertions five residues apart: the sector-connected DL121 has light-dependent regulation and many detected allostery-tuning single mutants; the non-sector-connected DL116 has similar baseline DHFR activity but no single mutants passing the paper's multiple-testing threshold. The same preprint reports that ProDomino insertion-tolerance scores explain 17–19% of growth-rate variation but only 0.6% of the allostery variation in this DHFR setting. This is a design-relevant *objective mismatch*: a model that identifies insert-tolerant sites need not identify sites whose allosteric response can be tuned by mutation.

This is not a new general law of protein evolvability or a theorem that sectors cause evolvability. The latest source is a **2026 bioRxiv preprint, not peer reviewed**; it studies one host, one photoswitch, and two insertion sites. The evidence supports a useful hypothesis and a concrete algorithmic benchmark. It does not establish that DL116 is an evolutionary dead end, that sector connectivity is the causal variable, or that stability generally suppresses allostery.

## Exact source interface and reported comparison

The comparison uses *E. coli* DHFR with the same *Avena sativa* LOV2 sensor inserted at positions 115/116 (DL116) or 120/121 (DL121). The host enzyme's catalytic output is selected in a DHFR-deficient *E. coli* strain. The scan measures nearly all 3,180 possible single amino-acid substitutions across the DHFR portion of each chimera under light and dark selection. After downsampling, the initial timepoint included 3,091/3,180 DL116 variants and 3,047/3,180 DL121 variants. The source reports 1,253 viable DL116 variants and 1,632 viable DL121 variants after its growth-rate floor and read-quality filters.

The operational allostery-tuning statistic is the light-minus-dark change in a mutant's **relative growth-rate slope**. Because the input frequency is the mutant-to-reference ratio, this is a genotype-by-light contrast against the unmutated chimera, not simply the absolute light-minus-dark growth of an unrelated background. The published method uses Welch tests on three light and three dark vials per mutant; it reports 47/1,253 nominally significant DL116 mutants versus 148/1,632 DL121 mutants, and after its SGoF correction zero versus 52. The pooled-label permutation enrichment is reported as `p = 4 × 10^-9`. This is evidence about detectable changes to this particular growth-response phenotype under this assay and filtering rule.

DL121's insertion is at the reported sector connection; DL116's is not. The insertion points differ by five residues, but their predicted LOV2–DHFR interfaces also differ substantially: the source's AlphaFold2 models estimate buried areas of about 1,095 Å² for DL121 and 1,890 Å² for DL116, with different contact sets. DL116's CD signal changed little from 20°C to 80°C and it remained in solution after the melt, whereas DL121 unfolded and aggregated. These are source-reported observations; CD resistance to a thermal ramp is not a measured thermodynamic unfolding free energy, and the structural interfaces are predicted models rather than matched experimental structures.

## Mathematical limits on the stronger interpretations

### No adjusted-significance result is not a no-path theorem

The scan covers one-substitution neighbors only. `p > cutoff` is failure to detect a nonzero effect at the available replicate count; it is not an equivalence result bounding every effect by a useful design margin. DL116 had 47 nominal `p < .05` candidates before correction, so even within the reported analysis its allostery signal is not identically zero. More importantly, no double or higher mutant is assayed. The data therefore establish **no DL116 single mutant passed this threshold**, not that no viable sequence path or multi-residue solution exists.

A minimal equilibrium counterexample makes the missing quantifier explicit. Let `u ∈ {0,1}` denote sensor dark/light, `y ∈ {0,1}` an output-active state, and `g_1,g_2 ∈ {0,1}` two substitutions. Suppose the conditional state energy is

\[
E(y\mid u,g_1,g_2)=-y(h+\kappa u g_1g_2),\qquad h\in\mathbb R,\;\kappa>0.
\]

Then

\[
\Pr(y=1\mid u,g_1,g_2)=\sigma(h+\kappa u g_1g_2),
\quad \sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}.
\]

The light-minus-dark output is exactly zero for the parent and either single mutant, while the double mutant has response `σ(h+κ) − σ(h) > 0`. Both intermediate single mutants retain the same dark-state output `σ(h)`, so they can be viable and neutral for the measured base activity. A two-residue contact that couples a sensor motion to an output state is one physical reading of the four-factor term `u g_1 g_2 y`. This is a **constructed model, not a claim about DL116**. It proves that an exhaustive single-mutant scan cannot logically rule out a viable neutral-intermediate route requiring a jointly formed allosteric bridge. A claim of no *strictly beneficial one-mutation-at-a-time* path is narrower and may be compatible with this example; a claim of no evolutionary route is not.

For a useful “no accessible single mutant” claim, predeclare an effect floor `δ` and estimate the viable-neighbor fraction

\[
\theta_+(\delta)=\Pr[\Delta A\geq\delta\mid\text{viable one-step mutant}]
\]

with simultaneous confidence bounds or equivalence tests. A family of nonsignificant tests does not certify `theta_+(δ)=0`. Even a certified bound on one-step neighbors says nothing about two-step bridges without a separate combination screen or mechanistic constraint.

### The sector mechanism is not identified by this two-site contrast

The observed constructs change sector connection and insertion site together. They also change interdomain buried area, contact geometry, initial allostery, and apparent stability. Let `S` be a sector-connection indicator and `J` summarize the alternative interface/dynamic constraint. For the two observed constructs, if `S` and `J` co-vary with the insertion site, both models `response = a S` and `response = b J` can fit the same DL116/DL121 contrast. Repeating the same two constructs or collecting more sequencing reads cannot distinguish these causal explanations. The authors themselves propose the larger stabilizing DL116 interface as a possible constraint on switching.

Thus the reported association is informative, but the specific causal claim “pre-existing sector pathways make the site more evolvable” remains confounded with site-specific mechanical coupling. The model result that ProDomino scores explain 0.6% of allostery variation is also task-specific: ProDomino was trained to predict insertion tolerance, not mutation-conditioned allosteric dynamic range. It is evidence that **tolerance alone does not solve the target objective here**, not that ProDomino fails its stated task.

## Strongest fair baselines and decisive test

A fair algorithmic comparison should use the same host sequence, sensor sequence, allowed insertion positions, mutation budget, and available structural/evolutionary data for every method. It should compare, at minimum, ProDomino's insertion-tolerance score; a structure/contact/interface score; a sector or coevolution score; and an allostery-trained sequence model. Freeze predictions before measuring held-out insertion sites. Score the intended outcome as the fraction of viable variants with a predeclared allostery gain `δ`, plus the magnitude and direction of the gain. Report baseline catalytic activity, sensor photochemistry, abundance/stability, and the dynamic response separately. ProDomino's published switch-design success remains a strong baseline for identifying tolerated/working sites; the question here is whether it can rank *mutational tuning access*.

The discriminating experiment is a crossed design across multiple insertion sites, ideally including within-site interface edits that preserve insertion tolerance while disrupting or restoring a proposed LOV2-to-sector contact. Match or adjust for baseline enzyme activity, LOV2 photochemistry, variant viability, sequencing depth, and assay variance. Use enough independent selection replicates to power a specified `δ`; validate nominated single mutants with direct enzyme assays under matched light/dark conditions. Where the scan finds no useful singles, test a small, preregistered set of plausible bridge-residue double mutants and compensatory interface pairs.

The sector-specific design hypothesis is falsified for the held-out panel if sector/coevolution features add no predictive lift over matched structure, interface, and sequence baselines, or if sector-disconnected sites repeatedly yield comparable `theta_+(δ)` once interface geometry is matched. The stronger “dead end” interpretation is falsified by a viable DL116 single mutant with a reproducible effect above `δ`, or by a viable neutral-intermediate double mutant that creates the predicted light-coupled output. Conversely, a failure to find such mutations is informative only relative to the tested library, effect floor, and power.

## Prior-art boundary

- **Direct recent source:** Dinan, McCormick, Soni, Thompson & Reynolds, “Protein Surface Site Determines the Evolutionary Accessibility of Allosteric Regulation,” bioRxiv v2 (2026), DOI [10.64898/2026.07.02.735819](https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.735819). This is the specific DL116/DL121 result and has not yet been peer reviewed.
- **Insertion-site ML prior art:** Wolf et al., “Rational engineering of allosteric protein switches by in silico prediction of domain insertion sites,” *Nature Methods* 22 (2025), DOI [10.1038/s41592-025-02741-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41592-025-02741-z). ProDomino predicts insertion tolerance and enabled experimental switch engineering; it preempts claims that sequence-based insertion-site prediction or model-guided allosteric fusion design is new.
- **Allostery/evolvability prior art:** Raman, White & Ranganathan, “Origins of Allostery and Evolvability in Proteins: A Case Study,” *Cell* 166 (2016), DOI [10.1016/j.cell.2016.05.047](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2016.05.047). PDZ-domain class-bridging intermediates already showed distal sector mutations with conditional neutrality and allosteric remodeling. A new result cannot claim the general discovery that sectors/allostery can support adaptation.
- **Allosteric specificity and epistasis prior art:** McCormick et al., “Epistasis shapes the fitness landscape of an allosteric specificity switch,” *Nature Communications* 12 (2021), DOI [10.1038/s41467-021-25826-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-021-25826-7). This preempts a broad claim that ligand-specificity switching in allosteric proteins involves multiple distinct epistatic fitness landscapes.

The potentially distinct contribution is narrower: a nearly complete single-mutant comparison of two closely spaced, otherwise related insertion sites, coupled to an insertion-tolerance model test, exposes a gap between **“will the fusion function?”** and **“can point mutations tune its allostery?”** Any new claim should build on, rather than rediscover, this result.
