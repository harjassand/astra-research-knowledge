CAUSAL INTERVENTIONS FOR TEACHER-FREE ACQUISITION OF PREDICTIVE STRUCTURE
Research leaf | 2026-10-08

Finding
--------
An explicit action-setting interface can identify action-specific latent transition kernels from partially observed data in a finite, well-conditioned model. The result is exact at the population-law level and has a direct finite-sample bound below. The identifying force comes from guaranteed action support plus a known invertible observation channel and a known full-support reset law. It is not a general method for discovering hidden states or learning an arbitrary HMM from scratch.

The fair passive comparison matters. A single uncontrolled HMM identifies only its predictive law under that uncontrolled process. If passive data already contain action labels from an exogenous randomized policy with full support, then the same action-specific kernels are identifiable from the same action-tagged samples. Interventions give control over support and sample allocation; they do not add information once the passive data contain the same randomized experiments. Selection, reset, and measurement costs must be charged.

1. Legal interface and target
-----------------------------
Take a finite hidden state S_t in {0,1}, action A_t in A={0,1}, and binary observation Y_t in {0,1}. Each query is a fresh one-transition episode:

  query(a): reset S_0 ~ mu; set A_0 := a by intervention; draw
  S_1 ~ T_a(. | S_0); return Y_0,Y_1.

The reset law mu is known and has full support, mu(0),mu(1)>0. The emissions are conditionally independent given the hidden states and are generated by a known binary symmetric channel with error eta<1/2:

  P(Y_t=y | S_t=s) = O[y,s],
  O = [[1-eta, eta], [eta, 1-eta]].

The unknown target is the pair of stochastic matrices T_0,T_1, including the state-conditioned intervention effects. No hidden state labels or transition labels are returned by the interface. The action value is recorded because the learner chose it. “Teacher-free” here means the learner is not supplied with T_0,T_1 or state labels; the finite state count, reset law, emission channel, and intervention semantics are supplied. They are substantive model knowledge, not acquired results.

The restriction eta<1/2 is precisely invertibility of O. The reset condition is needed to expose both rows of each transition matrix. These choices make the identifiability argument transparent. If O is unknown, singular, or state dependent in an unmodeled way, this result does not apply. A hidden-state representation may also have label symmetries in less constrained models.

2. What becomes identifiable
----------------------------
For one action a, let J_a(s,s') = mu(s) T_a(s'|s), the joint law of (S_0,S_1). Let Q_a(y,y') = P(Y_0=y,Y_1=y' | do(A_0=a)). In matrix notation,

  Q_a = O J_a O^T.

Since det(O)=1-2 eta != 0 and mu(s)>0,

  J_a = O^{-1} Q_a O^{-T},
  T_a(s'|s) = J_a(s,s') / mu(s).

Thus the interventional two-observation law uniquely determines both rows of T_a. Applying the same inversion separately to each action identifies (T_0,T_1). The direct effect on next-state probabilities is T_a(.|s)-T_b(.|s); the effect on the next observation is O T_a(.|s)-O T_b(.|s). Given these kernels, the full finite-horizon interventional prediction law follows by multiplying the kernels and emissions. For example, a belief update is

  b_{t+1}(s') proportional to O[y_{t+1},s'] sum_s b_t(s) T_{a_t}(s'|s).

The result identifies a named latent-state kernel because O and the state coordinates are known. If those are not supplied, the safer target is the observable controlled predictive law, such as probabilities of future observation tests under chosen action sequences. Predictive-state representations formalize that target without claiming a unique hidden-state ontology.

3. Intervention and query costs
-------------------------------
There are two notions of “minimum” to keep separate.

Population-law support minimum. If the action kernels are arbitrary and unrelated, every action value whose kernel is to be identified must appear in an interventional dataset. For m actions, at least m distinct intervention settings are necessary and sufficient at the population-law level. This is a regime-count statement, not a finite-sample statement. For the binary example, both do(A=0) and do(A=1) are necessary. If only one action is queried, the other kernel can be changed arbitrarily without changing any returned data.

Finite-sample upper bound. Write lambda=1-2 eta and mu_min=min_s mu(s). With N independent reset episodes for each action, estimate each Q_a by its empirical four-cell table and invert it as above. Hoeffding's inequality and ||(O tensor O)^{-1}||_infinity=lambda^{-2} give the following sufficient condition for every entry of every estimated T_a to be within epsilon, with probability at least 1-delta:

  N >= [ log(8 m / delta) ] / [ 2 epsilon^2 mu_min^2 lambda^4 ].

This bound is intentionally simple. It becomes ill conditioned as the sensor approaches a fair coin or the reset nearly omits a state. Projection of the raw inverse estimate onto valid probability matrices may be useful in implementation, but is not needed for this confidence calculation.

Finite-sample lower bound. Each action has two free Bernoulli transition parameters. Even with fully observed S_0 and S_1, estimating a row whose reset probability is mu(s) to additive error epsilon requires order 1/(mu(s) epsilon^2) episodes for that action in the worst case, up to confidence logarithms. This follows by choosing its transition probability near 1/2 and applying the ordinary two-point Bernoulli testing bound; only episodes beginning in s inform that row. Therefore all m arbitrary kernels require at least order m/(mu_min epsilon^2) queries in the well-conditioned reset regime. Noisy observations cannot improve that lower bound. With mu_min and lambda bounded away from zero, the upper and lower bounds match in their m and epsilon dependence, up to confidence factors and constants. The displayed inversion upper bound has a worse mu_min and lambda dependence; it is not claimed minimax-optimal outside that regime.

Charge one reset, one action selection, and two measurements for each one-step episode. For counts N_a, the active total is

  C_active = sum_a N_a [ c_reset + c_select(a) + 2 c_measure ].

Using the uniform sufficient count above, this gives an explicit sufficient cost. At the population level, the minimum support charge is one such episode per action. For finite error, replace each N_a by its required sample count. If reset or measurement can be batched, or action setting is free, the cost expression changes; those are different interfaces and must be charged accordingly.

4. Nonidentifiability without or with restricted interventions
---------------------------------------------------------------
No action variation. Suppose instead the environment chooses A independently and uniformly at every transition, but the learner cannot set or observe A. Consider two systems:

  System I:  T_0 = Identity, T_1 = Flip.
  System II: T_0 = Flip,     T_1 = Identity.

For either system, the uncontrolled one-step kernel is

  T_bar = (T_0 + T_1)/2,

which sends either hidden state to a fair bit. With the same reset law and same emission channel, the complete passive observation-sequence distributions are identical in the two systems. Yet under do(A=0), System I preserves the hidden state and System II flips it; under do(A=1), the effects reverse. Thus no passive learner can identify which action has which effect from this interface, even with infinitely many observations. The obstruction is causal-response nonidentifiability, not a sample shortage.

Restricted action support. If only do(A=0) is legal, compare two systems with the same T_0=Identity but T_1=Identity in one and T_1=Flip in the other. They induce exactly the same data for every legal query and disagree under do(A=1). Hence support over all target actions is necessary absent structural constraints tying their kernels together.

Important qualification: if passive data include the action labels and A is independently randomized with positive probability for every action, the first counterexample disappears. Those observations are themselves randomized experimental data. If actions are chosen by a policy correlated with hidden S_0, conditioning on the observed action generally does not reproduce do(A=a); hidden confounding remains possible.

5. Matched comparison with passive HMM learning
-----------------------------------------------
The weak passive baseline has no controllable action and sees a single uncontrolled HMM. With action values randomized but hidden from the learner, it learns the mixture kernel T_bar (and its resulting observation law), not the decomposition into action-specific kernels. The two-system counterexample shows this gap exactly.

A stronger, fair baseline observes naturally randomized action labels A and has the same reset distribution and emission measurements. If A is exogenous to S_0 and has full support pi(a)>0, the conditional data for each a have the same law as query(a). With exactly N_a action-tagged episodes per action, the estimators and identifiability are identical. The passive cost for M episodes is M(c_reset+2 c_measure); active querying pays the additional sum N_a c_select(a). Passive assignment may need a larger M to obtain the required counts: a standard concentration allocation is M on the order of max_a (N_a+log(m/delta))/pi(a). This treats the randomized assignment as a natural data source whose selection cost is borne outside the learner. If the learner or experimenter must randomize those actions, charge the same selection cost and call that controlled data collection rather than passive observation. Active selection is valuable when it supplies missing support (pi(a)=0) or avoids a skewed allocation, but it is not a free information gain. If passive action labels are absent, the mixture cannot be decomposed. If passive action assignment is endogenous to hidden state, the causal comparison is not matched.

This distinction also separates causal mechanism learning from predictive learning. Under a fixed passive HMM, the observable sequence law may be learnable without identifying a unique latent parameterization. Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang prove efficient HMM learning under a spectral separation condition, with joint and conditional sequence-prediction guarantees. That result does not identify the kernels of actions that the data never vary. Conversely, the present inversion identifies a latent transition family only because its observation map is known and invertible. Singh, James, and Rudary's predictive-state view offers a robust target for broader settings: represent state by predictions of observable future tests conditioned on action sequences.

6. Literature and scope of claims
---------------------------------
Primary sources consulted:

* Eberhardt, Glymour, and Scheines, “On the Number of Experiments Sufficient and in the Worst Case Necessary to Identify All Causal Relations Among N Variables,” UAI 2005 / arXiv:1207.1389. It gives experiment-count results for observed-variable DAGs under assumptions including no latent variables, no selection bias, and no feedback. Its log2(N)+1 bound is not a theorem about hidden Markov transition kernels; the regime-count argument here is derived separately from independent action-specific kernels. https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.1389
* Hsu, Kakade, and Zhang, “A Spectral Algorithm for Learning Hidden Markov Models,” JCSS 78(5), 2012; arXiv:0811.4413. It proves efficient passive HMM learning under a smallest-singular-value/rank separation condition and gives sequence prediction guarantees. https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.4413
* Singh, James, and Rudary, “Predictive State Representations: A New Theory for Modeling Dynamical Systems,” UAI 2004. It defines controlled-system state through predictions of observable action-observation tests. https://faculty.cc.gatech.edu/~isbell/reading/papers/uai2004psr.pdf

Claim ledger:

* Proven here by direct finite-dimensional inversion: population identifiability of each T_a under the stated known, invertible O and full-support known mu.
* Derived finite diagnostic guarantee: the stated empirical-inversion upper bound; confidence follows from Hoeffding plus the inverse-channel norm.
* Lower-bound result: action support is necessary exactly; finite sample order m/(mu_min epsilon^2) follows from Bernoulli estimation even with full state observation.
* Not established: minimax-optimal dependence on eta and mu_min for the noisy model; learning unknown O, unknown reset law, unknown state cardinality, or arbitrary hidden-state representations; an advantage over equally randomized action-tagged passive data; a general teacher-free acquisition solution for arbitrary HMMs.

Research implication
--------------------
This is a useful controlled subproblem because it shows exactly how the legal interface changes identifiability and exactly what it costs. It supports a narrow claim: interventions acquire action-conditioned predictive structure when the actions expose independent kernels and the observation/reset maps are sufficiently informative. It does not close the broader representation-acquisition barrier unless those maps, state coordinates, reset access, and action semantics can themselves be acquired or justified.
