# Thermoelectric conversion: bounded-transport Pareto design

## Verdict

The established thermoelectric efficiency formula is not new. The strongest result from this pass is a model-level, global reduction of the efficiency–power-factor frontier: with a bounded transport distribution and a fixed lattice heat leak, the best profile at any specified electrical conductance and Seebeck response is a saturated energy interval (“boxcar”). Therefore the maximum ZT subject to a minimum power factor reduces exactly to a two-endpoint search. This extends the commonly reported separate optima for ZT and power factor into an intermediate design target.

The proof is self-contained below. I found no direct source in the bounded-transport literature that states this fixed-power-factor envelope, but closely related boxcar and Pareto results make novelty and priority unresolved. There is no material-specific prediction here: realizing the interval while holding the lattice heat leak and transport cap fixed remains an acquisition and fabrication problem. Treat this as an internally reconstructed conditional theorem, not as a new physical effect.

## The model and the ordinary efficiency result

Let x=(E−μ)/(k_B T), f(x)=1/(1+e^x), and w(x)=−f′(x)=1/[4 cosh²(x/2)]. For a uniform material, define the transport moments

I_j = ∫ Σ(x) x^j w(x) dx,  j=0,1,2,

with 0≤Σ(x)≤Σ_max and Σ_max>0. The linear Boltzmann/RTA coefficients are

σ=e² I_0,  S=−(k_B/e) I_1/I_0,  κ_e=k_B²T(I_2−I_1²/I_0),

and κ=κ_e+κ_l, with κ_l>0 fixed. These are the moment formulas used in the primary bounded-transport analyses by Fan, Wang & Zheng (2011) and Maassen (2021).

For a reciprocal two-current Onsager device, nonnegative entropy production gives a positive-semidefinite symmetric response matrix. Writing the measured device conductance as G, open-circuit Seebeck coefficient as S, and zero-current thermal conductance as K, define ZT=G S²T/K. The load relation is I=G(SΔT−V); to first order in ΔT/T the hot-side heat input is Q_h=KΔT+TSI. Substituting r=V/(SΔT) gives, in linear response,

P=G S²(ΔT)² r(1−r),
η/η_C = ZT r(1−r)/[1+ZT(1−r)],  η_C=ΔT/T+o(ΔT/T).

Differentiation gives r_η=√(1+ZT)/(1+√(1+ZT)) and

η_max/η_C=(√(1+ZT)−1)/(√(1+ZT)+1).

Maximum power occurs at r=1/2 and is P_max=G S²(ΔT)²/4. This is the standard thermoelectric result, not a novelty claim. Onsager’s reciprocal-process treatment and Callen’s thermoelectric application are primary historical sources. Temperature-dependent transport and finite-ΔT temperature profiles are outside this small-gradient formula. It applies to complete-device coefficients: K must include every hot-to-cold heat path, including lattice, contacts, radiation, and parasitic leaks. A bulk intrinsic zT cannot be substituted for device ZT without accounting for those paths.

## Conditional Pareto theorem

Normalize φ=Σ/Σ_max, so 0≤φ≤1, and set

A=∫φw,  B=∫xφw,  C=∫x²φw,  a=κ_l/(Σ_max k_B²T).

Then

PF/(Σ_max k_B²)=B²/A,
ZT=B²/[A(C+a)−B²],
κ_e/(Σ_max k_B²T)=C−B²/A.

For any admissible φ with A>0, there exists an interval J=[ℓ,r] whose w-weighted mass and first moment agree with those of φ:

∫_J w = A,   ∫_J xw = B.

At fixed mass A, the smallest and largest feasible first moments are attained by the left and right tails of w-mass A (the one-dimensional bathtub principle). Slide an interval of mass A continuously from the left tail to the right tail. Its first moment changes continuously through the entire feasible range: when the interval moves right, equal w-mass is removed at lower x and added at higher x. Thus it matches every feasible B. At the extreme first moments, the matching profile is the corresponding half-line.

Let ψ=1_J. For finite ℓ,r, define g(x)=(x−ℓ)(x−r). On J, g≤0 and φ−ψ≤0; outside J, g≥0 and φ−ψ≥0. Therefore g(x)(φ(x)−ψ(x))≥0 pointwise. Since A and B agree,

C_φ−C_ψ
=∫g(x)(φ−ψ)w(x)dx
≥0.

The same conclusion at an extreme first moment follows from the unique left-tail or right-tail mass selector. Thus ψ has the same A and B (same σ, S, and power factor) and no larger C. It minimizes κ_e and maximizes ZT at those fixed electrical moments. Consequently, for every feasible p_0>0,

sup {ZT(φ): 0≤φ≤1, PF(φ)/(Σ_max k_B²)≥p_0}
=
sup {ZT(1_[ℓ,r]): PF(1_[ℓ,r])/(Σ_max k_B²)≥p_0}.

This is a global reduction, not a local stationarity calculation. It is equivalent to a two-variable search over interval endpoints, using the three Fermi-window moments. If the objective is device output, fixed leg length L and cross-section A_c give P_max=(PF A_c/L)(ΔT)²/4, so a minimum PF requirement sets a directly measurable minimum maximum-power capability for that geometry. It does not require operation at maximum efficiency and maximum power simultaneously.

The proof only uses the fixed cap, positive Fermi-window weight, and fixed additive lattice leak. It does not show that a real material can realize a chosen φ or alter its electronic profile without changing κ_l, the cap, contacts, or temperature dependence.

## Finite numerical probe

I enumerated saturated intervals on x∈[−1,15] at spacing 0.02, using cumulative trapezoidal moments, with normalized cap Σ_max=1 and a in {0.1,1,10}. This is a finite grid probe, not a proof or material prediction. For a=0.1, the grid gives:

| Design target | Interval [ℓ,r] | normalized PF | ZT | η_max/η_C in linear response |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| unconstrained max ZT | [2.22, 4.92] | 0.8499 | 6.0355 | 0.4524 |
| max ZT with PF≥1.23 | [1.52, 9.42] | 1.2300 | 4.2041 | 0.3905 |
| PF maximum on the finite grid | [1.14, 15] | 1.2653 | 3.3575 | 0.3522 |

This shows the design consequence: requiring about 45% more normalized PF than the unconstrained efficiency optimum lowers the best ZT from about 6.04 to 4.20, while retaining more efficiency than the grid’s PF-max profile. At a=1 the same PF floor changes the grid optimum only slightly (ZT 1.03185 to 1.03140); at a=10 it is inactive because the efficiency optimizer is already near the PF limit. The finite high-energy endpoint 15 approximates a half-line; it is not an exact PF supremum. Full output and reproducible calculation are in pareto_probe.py and pareto_probe_output.json.

## Prior-art attack and novelty boundary

The core ingredients are established. Onsager (1931) and Callen (1948) provide reciprocal thermoelectric transport; the maximum-efficiency formula is a century-old constant-property result. Mahan & Sofo (1996) characterize a delta-shaped transport distribution as the unconstrained optimum when arbitrarily sharp profiles are allowed. Fan, Wang & Zheng (2011) analyze normalized and bounded transport distributions, including rectangular profiles. Maassen (2021) proves that under a pointwise cap, constant lattice κ_l, and RTA, a boxcar maximizes ZT while a Heaviside profile maximizes power factor; he obtains upper limits scaling with Σ_max T/κ_l. The theorem here fills the intermediate fixed-PF Pareto envelope by preserving the first two moments and minimizing the second.

The nearest conceptual competitors are strong. Whitney’s nonlinear quantum-scattering bound says that at prescribed electrical output power the most efficient thermoelectric has a boxcar transmission, and phonon heat flow suppresses efficiency. Ding et al. (2023) directly optimize zT under a Landauer transmission cap given by an arbitrary density of modes and also obtain a boxcar transmission. Ashida & Sagawa compute power/efficiency Pareto fronts for interacting nanoscale heat engines. These works make the general trade-off and interval-shaped optimizers prior art. The possible increment is the simple global equal-moment proof and exact reduction for every PF floor in the bounded RTA material model. I did not establish priority; the 2021 and 2023 optimal-distribution literature may imply or already contain the same frontier.

## Costs, experiment, and next gate

The moments and interval search are cheap once the inputs are supplied: with N energy bins, moment acquisition is O(N) and a direct endpoint scan is O(N²); prefix sums reduce each candidate interval evaluation to O(1). The dominant charges are acquiring the band structure, density of modes, energy-dependent scattering time or transmission, and the bound Σ_max; measuring σ, S, κ_e, and κ_l separately; resolving contact and radiation leaks; fabricating the filter; and controlling its gate or magnetic field. The model does not subtract gate-control or field-generation work. It treats κ_l and the electronic filter as independent, so a physical optimization that couples phonon scattering to electron filtering must re-estimate κ_l for every candidate.

A discriminating experiment would compare energy-filter profiles in one fixed geometry at small ΔT/T, with a measured pointwise transmission/transport cap and independently measured lattice heat leak. Reconstruct A,B,C from the full energy-resolved response; select profiles with matched A,B (or equal PF and measured conductance), then compare κ_e and device ZT under identical contacts and boundaries. The strongest controls are the same-cap PF-max step and ZT-max boxcar, plus the constrained boxcar at the required PF. The theorem predicts that a non-boxcar profile cannot beat its matching interval at the same A,B when the model assumptions hold. A reproducible violation beyond propagated uncertainty would falsify the RTA/cap/fixed-κ_l description or reveal an uncounted channel; it would not contradict the algebraic theorem.

Next gate: reconstruct the exact derivations and assumptions in Fan (2011), Maassen (2021), and the 2023 quantum-limit optimization paper; search for a fixed-PF or fixed-first-moment result; then determine whether the interval theorem can be stated for a measured energy-dependent channel cap without losing the interval-existence argument. Until that gate and a real measured transport distribution are supplied, novelty and nature-level performance remain UNKNOWN.
