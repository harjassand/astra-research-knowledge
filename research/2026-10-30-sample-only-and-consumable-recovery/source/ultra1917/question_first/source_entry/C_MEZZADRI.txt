 How to generate random matrices from the classical compact
groups 
 
 
 Francesco Mezzadri
 

 Abstract 
    
 We discuss how to generate random unitary matrices from the
classical compact groups  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) ,  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  and  USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N)  with probability
distributions given by the respective invariant measures. The
algorithm is straightforward to implement using standard linear algebra
packages. This approach extends to the Dyson circular ensembles too.
This article is based on a lecture given by the author at the summer
school on Number Theory and Random Matrix Theory held at the
University of Rochester in June 2006. The exposition is addressed
to a general mathematical audience. 
  
 
 
 1  Introduction 

 
 Since Random Matrix Theory (RMT) was introduced by
Wishart  [ 17 ]  in 1928, it has found applications in a variety
of areas of physics, pure and applied mathematics, probability,
statistics and engineering. Few examples — far from being exhaustive
— include: analytic number theory, combinatorics, graph theory,
multivariate statistics, nuclear physics, quantum chaos, quantum
information, statistical mechanics, structural dynamics and wireless
telecommunications. The main reasons for the ever growing success of
RMT are mainly two. Firstly, in the limit of large matrix dimension
the statistical correlations of the spectra of a family, or ensemble,
of matrices are independent of the probability distribution that
defines the ensemble, but depend only on the invariant properties of
such a distribution. As a consequence random matrices turn out to be
very accurate models for a large number of mathematical and physical
problems. Secondly, RMT techniques allow analytical computations to an
extent that is often impossible to achieve in the contexts that they
are modelling. This predictive ability of RMT is particularly powerful
whenever in the original problem there are not any natural parameters
to average over. 
 
 
 Although the advantage of using RMT lies in the possibility of
computing explicit mathematical and physical quantities
analytically, it is sometimes necessary to resort to numerical
simulations. The purpose of this article is twofold. Firstly, we
provide the reader with a simple method for generating random
matrices from the classical compact groups that most
mathematicians — not necessarily familiar with computer
programming — should be able to implement in a code of only few
lines. This is achieved in section  5 . Secondly, we
discuss in detail the main ideas, which turn out be fairly general
and quite interesting, behind this algorithm. 
 
 
 An  N × N N\times N  unitary matrix  U = ( u j ​ k ) U=(u_{jk})  is defined by the relation
 U ∗ ​ U = U ​ U ∗ = I U^{*}U=UU^{*}=I , which in terms of the matrix elements reads 
 

 
 
 ∑ k = 1 N u j ​ k ∗ ​ u k ​ l = ∑ k = 1 N u ¯ k ​ j ​ u k ​ l = δ j ​ l and ∑ k = 1 N u j ​ k ​ u k ​ l ∗ = ∑ k = 1 N u ¯ j ​ k ​ u l ​ k = δ j ​ l , \sum_{k=1}^{N}u^{*}_{jk}u_{kl}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\overline{u}_{kj}u_{kl}=\delta_{jl}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{N}u_{jk}u^{*}_{kl}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\overline{u}_{jk}u_{lk}=\delta_{jl}, 
 
 (1.1) 
 
 where  U ∗ = ( u j ​ k ∗ ) U^{*}=(u^{*}_{jk})  is the conjugate transpose of  U U ,  i.e.  u j ​ k ∗ = u ¯ k ​ j u^{*}_{jk}=\overline{u}_{kj} . In this article we will use the
symbol  ¯ \bar{}  to denote complex conjugation, in order to
distinguish it from  ∗ , which is reserved to the conjugate
transpose of a matrix. The constraints ( 1.1 ) simply state
that the columns (rows) of a unitary matrix form an orthonormal
basis in  ℂ N \mathbb{C}^{N} . The set  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  of unitary matrices forms a
compact Lie group whose real dimension is  N 2 N^{2} ; it is then made
into a probability space by assigning as a distribution the unique
measure invariant under group multiplication, known as  Haar
measure.  Such a probability space is often referred to as
Circular Unitary Ensemble (CUE). 
 
 
 Usually the correct ensemble to model a particular situation depends
on the symmetries of the problem. Ensembles of unitary matrices are
constructed in two steps: we first identify a subset  𝖴 ⊂ U ⁡ ( N ) \mathsf{U}\subset\mathrm{U}(N)  by imposing further restrictions on  U U ; then we assign to
 𝖴 \mathsf{U}  a probability measure with the appropriate invariant
properties. As well as  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) , we will discuss how to generate random
matrices from the orthogonal  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  and unitary symplectic  USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N) 
groups with probability distributions given by the respective unique
invariant measures. We shall also consider the two remaining
 ​ D ​ y ​ s ​ o ​ n ​ c ​ i ​ r ​ c ​ u ​ l ​ a ​ r ​ e ​ n ​ s ​ e ​ m ​ b ​ l ​ e ​ s \emph{Dysoncircularensembles}   [ 2 ] , namely the Circular
Orthogonal Ensemble (COE) and Circular Symplectic Ensemble (CSE).
Other symmetric spaces appear in the applications  [ 18 ] , but we
will not concern ourselves with them. 
 
 
 Writing an algorithm to generate random unitary matrices which is both
correct and numerically stable presents some pitfalls. The reason
is that the conditions ( 1.1 ) imply that the matrix elements
are not independent and thus are statistically correlated. The main
ideas discussed in this article are centred around the QR
decomposition and go back to Wedderburn  [ 16 ] ,
Heiberger  [ 5 ]  (corrected by Tanner and Thisted  [ 15 ] ),
Stewart  [ 14 ]  and Diaconis and Shahshahani  [ 1 ] .
However, the technical literature may be difficult to access for a
reader without a background in numerical analysis or statistics, while
the implementation of such techniques is elementary. Another method
discussed in the literature involves an explicit representation of the
matrix elements of  U U  in terms of  N 2 N^{2}  independent parameters (Euler
angles)  [ 19 ] , but it does not seem to be equally efficient or
convenient. 
 
 
 
 2  Some examples and motivations 

 
 Before discussing how to generate random matrices it is helpful to
give few examples that show how they appear in the applications. 
 
 
 In quantum mechanics all the information about an isolated physical
system at a given time  t 0 t_{0}  is contained in a state vector  ψ 0 \psi_{0} 
belonging to a Hilbert space  ℋ \mathcal{H}  — in general infinite
dimensional. The time evolution of  ψ 0 \psi_{0} ,  i.e.  its dynamics, is
determined by a unitary operator  U U . In other words, at a time  t > t 0 t>t_{0} ,  ψ 0 \psi_{0}  has evolved into 
 

 
 
 ψ = U ​ ψ 0 . \psi=U\psi_{0}. 
 
 (2.1) 
 
 The fact that  U U  is unitary guarantees that
 ‖ ψ ‖ = ‖ ψ 0 ‖ = 1 \left\lVert\psi\right\rVert=\left\lVert\psi_{0}\right\rVert=1 , which is required by the probabilistic
interpretation of quantum mechanics. 
 
 
 If the dynamics is complicated — as in heavy nuclei or in
quantum systems whose classical limits are characterized by a
chaotic dynamics
— writing down an explicit expression for  U U  may be hopeless.
Therefore, we can attempt to replace  U U  by a random operator and
check if the predictions that we obtain are consistent with the
empirical observations. It is also reasonable to simplify the problem
even further and replace  U U  by a random unitary matrix of finite, but
large, dimension. Then the main question is: What are the matrix
space and the probability distribution that best model our system? 
 
 
 In physics the symmetries of a problem are often known a priori, even
if the details of the dynamics remain obscure. Now, suppose that our
system is invariant under time reversal but does not have any other
symmetry. From general considerations (see Mehta  [ 9 ]  p. 36)
we know that  U U  is always conjugate by a unitary transformation to a
symmetric matrix. Therefore, we can always choose  U U  so that 
 

 
 
 U = U t , U=U^{t}, 
 
 (2.2) 
 
 where  U t U^{t}  denotes the transpose of  U U . Since there are not
other symmetries in the problem, this is the only constraint that
we can impose. Therefore, the appropriate matrices that model
this physical system should be symmetric. Let us denote by
 𝖮 \mathsf{O}  the set of unitary symmeytric matrices. If  U ∈ 𝖮 U\in\mathsf{O}  it can be proved (see Meta  [ 9 ]  p. 499) that
it admits the representation 
 

 
 
 U = W ​ W t , W ∈ U ⁡ ( N ) . U=WW^{t},\quad W\in\mathrm{U}(N). 
 
 (2.3) 
 
 This factorization is not unique. Let  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  be the group of real
matrices  O O  such that  O ​ O t = O t ​ O = I OO^{t}=O^{t}O=I  and set  W ′ = W ​ O W^{\prime}=WO . By
definition we have 
 

 
 
 U = W ′ ​ W ′ t = W ​ O ​ O t ​ W t = W ​ W t . U=W^{\prime}W^{\prime t}=WOO^{t}W^{t}=WW^{t}. 
 
 (2.4) 
 
 This statement is true also in the opposite direction: if  W ​ W t = W ′ ​ W ′ t WW^{t}=W^{\prime}W^{\prime t}  there exists an  O ∈ O ⁡ ( N ) O\in\mathrm{O}(N)  such that  W ′ = W ​ O W^{\prime}=WO . Therefore,
 𝖮 \mathsf{O}  is isomorphic to the left coset space of  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  in  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) ,
 i.e. 
 

 
 
 𝖮 ≅ U ⁡ ( N ) / O ⁡ ( N ) . \mathsf{O}\cong\mathrm{U}(N)/\mathrm{O}(N). 
 
 (2.5) 
 
 
 
 Since a measure space with total mass equal to one is a probability
space, in what follows we shall use the two terminologies
interchangeably. An ensemble of random matrices is defined by a matrix
space and a probability measure on it. We have found the former; we
are left to identify the latter. Haar measure, which will be
discussed in detail in section  3 , provides a natural
probability distribution on  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) ; ‘natural’ in the sense that it
equally weighs different regions of  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) , thus it behaves like a
uniform distribution. From the factorization ( 2.3 ) the
probability distribution on  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  induces a measure on  𝖮 \mathsf{O} .
As a consequence, if  W W  is Haar distributed the resulting measure on
 𝖮 \mathsf{O}  will be uniform too. In section  8  we shall see
that such a measure is the unique probability distribution induced by
Haar measure on  𝖮 \mathsf{O} . Therefore, it provides a natural choice
to model a time reversal invariant quantum system. The space
 𝖮 \mathsf{O}  together with this measure is the COE ensemble. 
 
 
 If a quantum system does not have any symmetry, then there are no
restriction to  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  and the natural choice of probability
distribution is Haar measure. This is the CUE ensemble. If the system
is invariant under time reversal and has a half-integer spin, then the
appropriate ensemble is the CSE. The matrix space of the CSE is the
subset  𝖲 ⊂ U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathsf{S}\subset\mathrm{U}(2N)  whose elements admit the
representation 
 

 
 
 U = − W ​ J ​ W t ​ J , W ∈ U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) , U=-WJW^{t}J,\quad W\in\mathrm{U}(2N), 
 
 (2.6) 
 
 where 
 

 
 
 J = ( 0 I N − I N 0 ) . J=\begin{pmatrix}0&I_{N}\\
-I_{N}&0\end{pmatrix}. 
 
 (2.7) 
 
 From the factorization ( 2.6 ) the probability
distribution on  U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{U}(2N)  induces a measure on
 𝖲 \mathsf{S} . As previously, such a measure is fixed by assigning
Haar measure to  U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{U}(2N) . 
 
 
 The set  𝖲 \mathsf{S}  is isomorphic to a coset space too. The unitary
symplectic group  USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N)  is the subgroup of  U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{U}(2N)  whose
elements obey the relation 
 

 
 
 S ​ J ​ S t = J . SJS^{t}=J. 
 
 (2.8) 
 
 Therefore, the matrix  U U  in equation ( 2.6 ) does not change
if we replace  W W  with  W ′ = W ​ S W^{\prime}=WS , where  S ∈ USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) S\in\mathrm{USp}(2N) . Similarly, if  W W 
and  W ′ W^{\prime}  are such that 
 

 
 
 U = − W ​ J ​ W t ​ J = − W ′ ​ J ​ W ′ t ​ J , W , W ′ ∈ U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) , U=-WJW^{t}J=-W^{\prime}JW^{\prime t}J,\quad W,W^{\prime}\in\mathrm{U}(2N), 
 
 (2.9) 
 
 then  W ′ ​ W − 1 ∈ USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) W^{\prime}W^{-1}\in\mathrm{USp}(2N) . Therefore, 
 

 
 
 𝖲 ≅ U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) / USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) . \mathsf{S}\cong\mathrm{U}(2N)/\mathrm{USp}(2N). 
 
 (2.10) 
 
 The probability distribution of the CSE is the unique invariant
measure induced on the coset space ( 2.10 ) by Haar measure on
 U ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{U}(2N) . 
 
 
 From equations ( 2.3 ) and ( 2.6 ) all we need to
generate random matrices in the CUE, COE and CSE ensembles is an
algorithm whose output is Haar distributed unitary matrices. The
rest of this article will concentrate on generating random
matrices from all three classical compact groups  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) ,  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  and
 USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N)  with probability distributions given by the respective
Haar measures. These groups are not only functional to
constructing matrices in the COE and CSE, but are also important
ensembles in their own right. Indeed, the work of
Montgomery  [ 11 ] , Odlyzko  [ 12 ] , Katz and
Sarnak  [ 6 ] , Keating and Snaith  [ 7 ,  8 ]  and
Rubinstein  [ 13 ]  has shown beyond doubt that the local
statistical properties of the the Riemann zeta function and other
 L -functions can be modelled by the characteristic
polynomials of Haar distributed random matrices. Over the last
few years the predictive power of this approach has brought about
an impressive progress in analytic number theory that could not
have been achieved with traditional techniques. (See  [ 10 ] 
for a collection of review articles in the subject.) 
 
 
 
 3  Haar measure and invariance 

 
 Since the algorithm the we shall discuss is essentially based on the
invariant properties of Haar measure, in this section we introduce the
main concepts that are needed to understand how it works. We,
nevertheless, begin with another ensemble:  the Ginibre
ensemble.  Besides being a simpler illustration of the ideas we need,
generating a matrix in the Ginibre ensemble is the first step toward
producing a random unitary matrix. 
 
 
 The space of matrices for the Ginibre ensemble is
 GL ⁡ ( N , ℂ ) \mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C}) , the set of all the invertible  N × N N\times N  complex matrices  Z = ( z j ​ k ) Z=(z_{jk}) ; the matrix elements are independent
identically distributed ( i.i.d. ) standard normal complex random
variables. In other words, the probability density function
( p.d.f. ) of  z j ​ k z_{jk}  is 
 

 
 
 p ⁡ ( z j ​ k ) = 1 π ​ e − | z j ​ k | 2 . p(z_{jk})=\frac{1}{\pi}e^{-\left\lvert z_{jk}\right\rvert^{2}}. 
 
 (3.1) 
 
 By definition the matrix entries are statistically independent,
therefore the joint probability density function ( j.p.d.f. ) for
the matrix elements is 
 

 
 
 P ( Z ) = 1 π N 2 ∏ j , k = 1 N e − | z j ​ k | 2 = 1 π N 2 exp ( − ∑ j , k = 1 N | z j ​ k | 2 ) = 1 π N 2 exp ( − Tr Z ∗ Z ) . P(Z)=\frac{1}{\pi^{N^{2}}}\prod_{j,k=1}^{N}e^{-\left\lvert z_{jk}\right\rvert^{2}}=\frac{1}{\pi^{N^{2}}}\exp\left(-\sum_{j,k=1}^{N}\left\lvert z_{jk}\right\rvert^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi^{N^{2}}}\exp\left(-\trace Z^{*}Z\right). 
 
 (3.2) 
 
 Since  P ⁡ ( Z ) P(Z)  is a probability density, it is normalized to one,
 i.e. 
 

 
 
 ∫ ℂ N 2 P ⁡ ( Z ) ​ 𝑑 Z = 1 , \int_{\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}}}P(Z)\,dZ=1, 
 
 (3.3) 
 
 where  d ​ Z = ∏ j , k = 1 N d ​ x j ​ k ​ d ​ y j ​ k dZ=\prod_{j,k=1}^{N}dx_{jk}dy_{jk}  and  z j ​ k = x j ​ k + i ​ y j ​ k z_{jk}=x_{jk}+iy_{jk} . The  j.p.d.f.   P ⁡ ( Z ) P(Z)  contains all the statistical
information on the Ginibre ensemble. 
 
 
 Since  ℂ N × N ≅ ℂ N 2 \mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\cong\mathbb{C}^{N^{2}} , we will use the
two notations according to what is more appropriate for the context.
Thus, we can write 
 

 
 
 d ​ μ G ​ ( Z ) = P ⁡ ( Z ) ​ d ​ Z d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(Z)=P(Z)\,dZ 
 
 (3.4) 
 
 and think of  d ​ μ G d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}  as an infinitesimal volume or measure in
 ℂ N 2 \mathbb{C}^{N^{2}} .
If  f : ℂ N × N ⟶ ℂ N × N f:\mathbb{C}^{N\times N}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{N\times N} , we say that  d ​ μ G d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}  is invariant under  f f 
if 
 

 
 
 d ​ μ G ​ ( f ⁡ ( Z ) ) = d ​ μ G ​ ( Z ) . d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}\bigl(f(Z)\bigr)=d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(Z). 
 
 (3.5) 
 
 
 
 Lemma 1 . 
 
 The measure of the Ginibre ensemble is invariant under left and
right multiplication of  Z Z  by arbitrary unitary matrices, i.e. 
 

 
 
 d ​ μ G ​ ( U ​ Z ) = d ​ μ G ​ ( Z ​ V ) = d ​ μ G ​ ( Z ) , U , V ∈ U ⁡ ( N ) . d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(UZ)=d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(ZV)=d\mu_{\mathrm{G}}(Z),\quad U,V\in\mathrm{U}(N). 
 
 (3.6) 
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
 First we need to show that  P ⁡ ( U ​ Z ) = P ⁡ ( Z ) P(UZ)=P(Z) ; then we must prove
that the Jacobian of the map 
 

 
 
 Z ↦ U ​ Z Z\mapsto UZ 
 
 (3.7) 
 
 (seen as a transformation in  ℂ N 2 \mathbb{C}^{N^{2}} ) is one. Since by
definition  U ∗ ​ U = I U^{*}U=I , we have 
 

 
 
 P ⁡ ( U ​ Z ) = 1 π N 2 ​ exp ⁡ ( − Tr ⁡ Z ∗ ​ U ∗ ​ U ​ Z ) = 1 π N 2 ​ exp ⁡ ( − Tr ⁡ Z ∗ ​ Z ) = P ⁡ ( Z ) . P(UZ)=\frac{1}{\pi^{N^{2}}}\exp\left(-\trace Z^{*}U^{*}UZ\right)=\frac{1}{\pi^{N^{2}}}\exp\left(-\trace Z^{*}Z\right)=P(Z). 
 
 (3.8) 
 
 Now, the map ( 3.7 ) is isomorphic to 
 

 
 
 X = U ⊕ ⋯ ⊕ U ⏟ N ​ times . X=\underset{N\text{times}}{\underbrace{U\oplus\cdots\oplus U}}. 
 
 (3.9) 
 
 It follows immediately that  X X  is a  N 2 × N 2 N^{2}\times N^{2}  unitary
matrix, therefore  | det X | = 1 \left\lvert\det X\right\rvert=1 . The proof of right invariance
is identical.
∎ 
 
 
 
 Because the elements of a unitary matrix are not independent,
writing an explicit formula for the infinitesimal volume element
of  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  is more complicated than for the Ginibre ensemble. An  N × N N\times N  unitary matrix contains  2 ​ N 2 2N^{2}  real numbers and the
constraints ( 1.1 ) form a system of  N 2 N^{2}  real equations.
Therefore,  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  is isomorphic to a  N 2 N^{2} -dimensional manifold
embedded in  ℝ 2 ​ N 2 \mathbb{R}^{2N^{2}} . Such a manifold is compact and
has a natural group structure that comes from matrix
multiplication. Thus, an infinitesimal volume element on  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) 
will have the form 
 

 
 
 d μ ( U ) = m ( α 1 , … , α N 2 ) d α 1 ⋯ d α N 2 , d\mu(U)=m(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N^{2}})d\alpha_{1}\cdots d\alpha_{N^{2}}, 
 
 (3.10) 
 
 where  α 1 , … , α N 2 \alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N^{2}}  are local coordinates on
 U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) . Every compact Lie group has a unique (up to an arbitrary
constant) left and right invariant measure, known as  Haar
measure.  In other words, if we denote Haar measure on  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  by
 d ​ μ H ​ ( U ) d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(U) , we have 
 

 
 
 d ​ μ H ​ ( V ​ U ) = d ​ μ H ​ ( U ​ W ) = d ​ μ H ​ ( U ) , V , W ∈ U ⁡ ( N ) . d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(VU)=d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(UW)=d\mu_{\mathrm{H}}(U),\quad V,W\in\mathrm{U}(N). 
 
 (3.11) 
 
 Although an explicit expression for Haar measure on  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  in terms of
local coordinates can be written down (see
Życzkowski and Kus  [ 19 ]  for a formula), we
will see that in order to generate matrices distributed with Haar
measure we only need to know that is invariant and unique. 
 
 
 Haar measure normalized to one is a natural choice for a probability
measure on a compact group because, being invariant under group
multiplication, any region of  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  carries the same weight in a group
average. It is the analogue of the uniform density on a finite
interval. In order to understand this point consider the simplest
example:  U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) . It is the set  { e i ​ θ } \{e^{i\theta}\}  of the
complex numbers with modulo one, therefore it has the topology of the
unit circle  𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} . Since in this case matrix multiplication
is simply addition  mod  2 ​ π \bmod\>2\pi ,  U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1)  is isomorphic to
the group of translations on  𝕊 1 \mathbb{S}^{1} . A probability density
function that equally weighs any part of the unit circle is the
constant density  ρ ⁡ ( θ ) = 1 / ( 2 ​ π ) \rho(\theta)=1/(2\pi) . This is the standard
Lebesgue measure, which is invariant under translations. Therefore,
it is the unique Haar measure on  U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) . 
 
 
 Note that it is not possible to define an ‘unbiased’ measure on a
non-compact manifold. For example, we can provide a finite interval
with a constant  p.d.f.   ρ ⁡ ( x ) \rho(x) , but not the whole real line
 ℝ \mathbb{R} , since the integral  ∫ − ∞ ∞ ρ ⁡ ( x ) ​ 𝑑 x \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\rho(x)dx 
would diverge. 
 
 
 
 4  The QR decomposition and a numerical experiment 

 
 By definition the columns of a  N × N N\times N  unitary matrix are
orthonormal vectors in  ℂ N \mathbb{C}^{N} . Thus, if we take an arbitrary
complex  N × N N\times N  matrix  Z Z  of full rank and apply the Gram-Schmidt
orthonormalization to its columns, the resulting matrix  Q Q  is
unitary. It turns out that if the entries of  Z Z  ares  i.i.d. 
standard complex normal random variables,  i.e.  if  Z Z  belongs to the
Ginibre ensemble, then  Q Q  is distributed with Haar measure (see
Eaton  [ 3 ] , p. 234, for a proof). Unfortunately, the
implementation of this algorithm is numerically unstable. However, we
may observe that 
 

 
 
 Z = Q ​ R , Z=QR, 
 
 (4.1) 
 
 where  R R  is upper-triangular and invertible. In other words, the
Gram-Schmidt algorithm realizes the  QR decomposition.  This
factorization is widely used in numerical analysis to solve linear
least squares problems and as first step of a particular
eigenvalue algorithm. Indeed, every linear algebra package has a
routine that implements it. In most cases, however, the algorithm
adopted is not the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization but uses the
 Householder reflections , which are numerically stable. 
 
 
 Because of this simple observation, at first one might be tempted to
produce a matrix in the Ginibre ensemble and then to use a black box
QR decomposition routine. Writing such a code is straightforward. For
example, if we choose the SciPy library in Python, we may implement
the following function:

 Unfortunately, the output is not distributed with Haar measure,
as it was observed by Edelman and Rao  [ 4 ] . It is
instructive to give an explicit example of this phenomenon. 
 
 
 A unitary matrix can always be diagonalized in  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) . Therefore,
its eigenvalues  { e i ​ θ 1 , … , e i ​ θ N } \{e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{N}}\}  lie on
the unit circle. A classical calculation in RMT (see
Mehta  [ 9 ]  pp. 203–205) consists of computing the
statistical correlations among the arguments  θ j \theta_{j} . The
simplest correlation function to determine is the density of the
eigenvalues  ρ ⁡ ( θ ) \rho(\theta) , or — as sometimes it is called
— the one-point correlation. Since Haar measure is the analogue of a
uniform distribution, each set of eigenvalues must have the same
weight, therefore the normalized eigenvalue density is 
 

 
 
 ρ ⁡ ( θ ) = 1 2 ​ π . \rho(\theta)=\frac{1}{2\pi}. 
 
 (4.2)

mean that the eigenvalues are statistically uncorrelated. 
 
 
 Testing ( 4.2 ) numerically is very simple. We generated

all the spectral correlations and is easy to determine empirically.
For unitary matrices it is defined as follows. Take the arguments of
the eigenvalues and order them in ascending order:

 (4.3)

eigenvalues are

 (4.4) 
 
 The spacing distribution  p ⁡ ( s ) p(s)  is the probability density of  s s .
(For a discussion on the spacing distribution see
Mehta  [ 9 ]  p. 118.)

a combination of algorithms found in LAPACK routines. Changing

 5  A correct and efficient algorithm 

 
 What is wrong with standard QR factorization routines? Where do
they differ from the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization? Why is the
probability distribution of the output matrix not Haar measure? 
 
 
 The main problem is that QR decomposition is not unique. Indeed, let
 Z ∈ GL ⁡ ( N , ℂ ) Z\in\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})  and suppose that  Z = Q ​ R Z=QR , where  Q Q  is unitary and  R R  is
invertible and upper-triangular. If 
 

 
 
 Λ = ( e i ​ θ 1 ⋱ e i ​ θ N ) = diag ⁡ ( e i ​ θ 1 , … , e i ​ θ N ) , \Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta_{1}}&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&e^{i\theta_{N}}\end{pmatrix}=\diag\left(e^{i\theta_{1}},\ldots,e^{i\theta_{N}}\right), 
 
 (5.1) 
 
 then 
 

 
 
 Q ′ = Q ​ Λ and R ′ = Λ − 1 ​ R Q^{\prime}=Q\Lambda\quad\text{and}\quad R^{\prime}=\Lambda^{-1}R 
 
 (5.2) 
 
 are still unitary and upper-triangular respectively. Furthermore, 
 

 
 
 Z = Q ​ R = Q ′ ​ R ′ . Z=QR=Q^{\prime}R^{\prime}. 
 
 (5.3) 
 
 Therefore, the QR decomposition defines a multi-valued map 
 

 
 
 QR : GL ⁡ ( N , ℂ ) ⟶ U ⁡ ( N ) × T ⁡ ( N ) , \mathrm{QR}:\mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{C})\longrightarrow\mathrm{U}(N)\times\mathrm{T}(N), 
 
 (5.4) 
 
 where  T ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{T}(N)  denotes the group of invertible upper-triangular matrices. 
 
 
 In order to make the mapping ( 5.4 ) single-valued, we
need to specify the algorithm that achieves the factorization. In
most applications such a choice is dictated only by the
performance and stability of the code. For our purposes, however,
the subset of 
 U ⁡ ( N ) × T ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)\times\mathrm{T}(N) , in which the output of
the QR decomposition is chosen, is fundamental and we need to pay
particular attention to it. It is convenient from a mathematical
point of view to introduce a variation of the
mapping ( 5.4 ), which is not only single-valued but also
one-to-one. In this way we will not have to refer all the times to
a specific algorithm. Indeed, the idea is that we should be able
to alter the output of a QR decomposition routine without even
knowing the algorithm implemented. 
 
 
 We first need 
 
 
 Lemma 2 . 
 
 Equation ( 5.3 ) implies ( 5.2 ),
where  Λ ∈ Λ ⁡ ( N ) \Lambda\in\Lambda(N)  and  Λ ⁡ ( N ) \Lambda(N)  denotes the group of all

 Proof. 
 
 Equation ( 5.3 ) can be rearranged as 
 

 
 
 Q − 1 ​ Q ′ = R ​ R ′ − 1 . Q^{-1}Q^{\prime}=RR^{\prime-1}. 
 
 (5.5) 
 
 Since  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  and  T ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{T}(N)  are groups, both sides of

definition the inverse of a unitary matrix  U U  is its conjugate

decomposition ( 5.6 ) must be such that if

 6  The unitary symplectic group  USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N) 

 
 Up to now we have only considered  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) . The discussion for  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  is

 The algebra of unitary symplectic matrices can be rephrased in terms of
Hamilton’s quaternions; it is convenient for our purposes to
use this formalism. A quaternion  q ∈ ℍ q\in\mathbb{H}  is a linear

 Let  S ∈ USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) S\in\mathrm{USp}(2N)  be the complex representation of the quaternion

∎ 
 
 
 
 The algebra of  Sp ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{Sp}(N)  is the generalization to Hamilton’s quaternions
of the algebra of  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) . Therefore, it is not surprising that the
discussion of section  5  is not affected by replacing

particular, we can introduce the quaternion Ginibre ensemble, which is

 7  Householder reflections 

 
 Theorem  3  provides us with the theoretical tools to
generate a random matrix in  USp ⁡ ( 2 ​ N ) \mathrm{USp}(2N) . However, when we implement these
results in computer code, we need to devise an algorithm whose output
satisfies the condition ( 6.22 ). The first one that
comes to one’s mind is the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization. But given
that black box routines for quaternion matrices do not exists on the
market, and that we are forced to write the complete code ourselves,
we may as well choose one that is numerically stable and which, as it
turns out, requires the same effort. The most common algorithm that

 8  A group theoretical interpretation 

 
 We now know how to generate random matrices from any of the
classical compact groups  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N) ,  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  and  Sp ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{Sp}(N) . In order to
achieve this goal, we have used little more than linear algebra.
However simple and convenient this approach is (after all linear
algebra plays a major role in writing most numerical algorithms),
it hides a natural group theoretical structure behind the
Householder reflections, which was uncovered by Diaconis and
Shahshahani  [ 1 ] . Indeed, generating a random matrix as a
product of Householder reflections is only one example of a more
general method that can be applied to any finite or compact Lie
group. Our purpose in this section is to give a flavour of this
perspective. For the sake of clarity, as before, we will discuss the
orthogonal group  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N) ; the treatment of  U ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{U}(N)  and  Sp ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{Sp}(N) 
is, once again, almost identical. 
 
 
 The need of a more general and elegant approach arises also if one
observes that there is one feature of the QR decomposition that may
not be entirely satisfactory to a pure mathematician: Why in order to
generate a random point on a  N ⁡ ( N − 1 ) / 2 N(N-1)/2 -dimensional manifold —  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N) 
in this case — do we need to generate  N 2 N^{2}  random numbers? It does
not look like the most efficient option, even if it is a luxury that
can be easily afforded on today’s computers. 
 
 
 We will first show how the key ideas that we want to describe apply to
finite groups, as in this setting they are more transparent. Suppose
that we need to generate a random element  g g  in a finite group
 Γ N \Gamma_{N} . In this context, if  Γ N \Gamma_{N}  has  p p  elements, uniform
distribution simply means that the probability of extracting any  g ∈ Γ N g\in\Gamma_{N}  is  1 / p 1/p . In addition, we assume that there exists a
chain of subgroups of  Γ N \Gamma_{N} : 
 

 
 
 Γ 1 ⊂ Γ 2 ⊂ ⋯ ⊂ Γ N . \Gamma_{1}\subset\Gamma_{2}\subset\cdots\subset\Gamma_{N}. 
 
 (8.1) 
 
 In practical situations it is often easier to generate a random
element  g ~ \tilde{g}  in a smaller subgroup, say  Γ m − 1 ∈ Γ N \Gamma_{m-1}\in\Gamma_{N} , than in  Γ N \Gamma_{N}  itself; we may also know how to take a
random representative  g m g_{m}  in the left coset  C m = Γ m / Γ m − 1 C_{m}=\Gamma_{m}/\Gamma_{m-1} . Now, write the decomposition 
 

 
 
 Γ m ≅ C m × Γ m − 1 . \Gamma_{m}\cong C_{m}\times\Gamma_{m-1}. 
 
 (8.2) 
 
 Once we have chosen a set of representatives of  C m C_{m} , an element
 g ∈ Γ m g\in\Gamma_{m}  is uniquely factorized as  g = g m ​ g ~ g=g_{m}\,\tilde{g} ,
where  g m ∈ C m g_{m}\in C_{m} . If both  g m g_{m}  and  g ~ \tilde{g}  are uniformly
distributed in  C m C_{m}  and  Γ m − 1 \Gamma_{m-1}  respectively, then  g g  is
uniformly distribute in  Γ m \Gamma_{m} . 
 
 
 We can apply this algorithm iteratively starting from  Γ 1 \Gamma_{1}  and
eventually generate a random element in  Γ N \Gamma_{N} . In other words,
we are given the decomposition 
 

 
 
 Γ N ≅ C N × ⋯ × C 2 × Γ 1 . \Gamma_{N}\cong C_{N}\times\cdots\times C_{2}\times\Gamma_{1}. 
 
 (8.3) 
 
 An element  g ∈ Γ N g\in\Gamma_{N}  has a unique representation as a product 
 

 
 
 g = g N ⋯ g 1 , g=g_{N}\cdots g_{1}, 
 
 (8.4) 
 
 where  g m g_{m}  is a representative in  C m C_{m} . If the  g m g_{m} s are
uniformly distributed in  C m C_{m}  so is  g g  in  Γ N \Gamma_{N} . This is known
as the  subgroup algorithm   [ 1 ] . 
 
 
 This technique applies to random permutations of  N N  letters. The
chains of subgroups is 
 

 
 
 { Id } ⊂ S 2 ⊂ ⋯ ⊂ S N , \left\{\mathrm{Id}\right\}\subset S_{2}\subset\cdots\subset S_{N}, 
 
 (8.5) 
 
 where  S m S_{m}  is the  m m -th symmetric group. Other examples include
generating random positions of Rubik’s cube and random elements in
 GL ⁡ ( N , 𝔽 p ) \mathrm{GL}(N,\mathbb{F}_{p}) , where  𝔽 p \mathbb{F}_{p}  is a finite field
with  p p  elements. 
 
 
 For  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  the decompositions ( 8.3 )
and ( 8.4 ) are hidden behind the
factorization ( 7.7 ) in terms of Householder reflections.
Indeed, the subgroup algorithm for  O ⁡ ( N ) \mathrm{O}(N)  is contained in 
 
 
 Theorem 4 . 
 
 Let  𝐯 ^ 1 , … , 𝐯 ^ N \mathbf{\hat{v}}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{\hat{v}}_{N}  be uniformly
distributed on  𝕊 0 , … , 𝕊 N − 1 \mathbb{S}^{0},\ldots,\mathbb{S}^{N-1} 
respectively, where 
 

 
 
 𝕊 m − 1 = { 𝐯 ^ m = ( x 1 , … , x m ) ∈ ℝ m | ∑ j = 1 m x j 2 = 1 } \mathbb{S}^{m-1}=\Bigl\{\mathbf{\hat{v}}_{m}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\bigl\lvert\>\textstyle{\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{j}^{2}=1}\Bigr\} 
 
 (8.6) 
 
 is the unit sphere in  ℝ m \mathbb{R}^{m} . Furthermore, let

in equation ( 7.18 ). The product
