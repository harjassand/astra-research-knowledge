 Abstract 
    
 The Petz recovery channel plays an important role in quantum information science as an operation that approximately reverses the effect of a quantum channel. The pretty good measurement is a special case of the Petz recovery channel, and it allows for near-optimal state discrimination. A hurdle to the experimental realization of these vaunted theoretical tools is the lack of a systematic and efficient method to implement them. This paper sets out to rectify this lack: using the recently developed tools of quantum singular value transformation and oblivious amplitude amplification, we provide a quantum algorithm to implement the Petz recovery channel when given the ability to perform the channel that one wishes to reverse. Moreover, we prove that, in some sense, our quantum algorithm’s usage of the channel implementation cannot be improved by more than a quadratic factor. Our quantum algorithm also provides a procedure to perform pretty good measurements when given multiple copies of the states that one is trying to distinguish. 
  
 
 
 Introduction —Pretty good measurements  [ 1 ,  2 ,  3 ,  4 ,  5 ]  and Petz recovery channels  [ 6 ,  7 ,  8 ,  9 ,  10 ]  are workhorses of quantum information theory: they are used ubiquitously to prove
basic results in quantum communication and measurement  [ 11 ] . Although important for attaining quantum channel capacities  [ 12 ,  13 ,  14 ,  15 ,  16 ]  and performing
state discrimination  [ 1 ,  2 ,  3 ,  4 ,  9 ] , these useful theoretical constructions are less common in experiment, for the simple reason that
there has not been a systematic method for performing them efficiently in practice. Our goal here is to fill this gap. 
 
 
 The Petz recovery channel was introduced in the context of quantum sufficiency in  [ 6 ,  7 ]  and later rediscovered in  [ 9 ]  in the context of quantum error correction.
It can be understood as a critical part of a quantum version of the Bayes theorem  [ 17 , Section IV] . To review it, let us begin with the classical case. A classical channel with input system  X X  and output system  Y Y  over the alphabets  𝒳 , 𝒴 \mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}  is a conditional probability distribution  { p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) } x ∈ 𝒳 , y ∈ 𝒴 \{p_{Y|X}(y|x)\}_{x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y}} . We consider a probability distribution  p X ​ ( x ) p_{X}(x)  over the alphabet  𝒳 \mathcal{X}  as the input to the channel. It then follows from the Bayes theorem that
 p X ​ ( x ) ​ p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) = p Y ​ ( y ) ​ p X | Y ​ ( x | y ) p_{X}(x)p_{Y|X}(y|x)=p_{Y}(y)p_{X|Y}(x|y) , where  p Y ​ ( y ) = ∑ x p X ​ ( x ) ​ p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) p_{Y}(y)=\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)p_{Y|X}(y|x) . Hence, for all  x ∈ 𝒳 , y ∈ 𝒴 x\in\mathcal{X},y\in\mathcal{Y} , we define the “reversal channel” via the formula 
 

 
 
 p X | Y ​ ( x | y ) = p X ​ ( x ) ​ p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) ∑ x p X ​ ( x ) ​ p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) . p_{X|Y}(x|y)=\frac{p_{X}(x)p_{Y|X}(y|x)}{\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)p_{Y|X}(y|x)}. 
 
 (1) 
 
 This channel acts on the output system  Y Y . If the particular distribution  p Y ​ ( y ) p_{Y}(y)  defined above is “sent in” through this channel, then the input  p X ​ ( x ) p_{X}(x)  is recovered perfectly:  p X ​ ( x ) = ∑ y p X | Y ​ ( x | y ) ​ p Y ​ ( y ) p_{X}(x)=\sum_{y}p_{X|Y}(x|y)p_{Y}(y) . The computation of the reversal channel  p X | Y ​ ( x | y ) p_{X|Y}(x|y)  requires a specification of the input probability distribution  p X ​ ( x ) p_{X}(x)  and the forward channel  p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) p_{Y|X}(y|x) . The Petz recovery channel is a quantum generalization of the reversal channel above: it is a function of a quantum channel  𝒩 \mathcal{N}  and an input state  σ \sigma  to the channel, with the former generalizing  p Y | X ​ ( y | x ) p_{Y|X}(y|x)  and the latter  p X ​ ( x ) p_{X}(x) . We discuss it in more detail in what follows. 
 
 
 The Petz recovery channel appears often in quantum information as a proof tool, showing that near-optimal recovery from undesired quantum operations is possible. Ref.  [ 9 ]  demonstrated how this recovery channel can be an effective means for reversing the effects of noise. Thereafter,  [ 18 ]  showed that the Petz recovery channel (therein called “transpose channel”) is a universal recovery operation for approximate quantum error correction, which performs comparably to the best possible one in terms of worst-case fidelity (see also  [ 19 ,  20 ] ).
The Petz recovery channel also goes by the name “pretty good recovery,” as used in  [ 21 ,  22 ] , due to the result of  [ 9 ] .
Yet another application comes from the field of quantum communication:  [ 16 ]  showed explicitly how to use the Petz recovery channel in a decoder to achieve the coherent information rate of quantum communication. It has also found use in developing physically meaningful refinements of quantum entropy inequalities  [ 23 ,  24 ,  25 ,  26 ,  27 ] .
See  [ 28 ,  29 ,  30 ,  31 ,  32 ]  for further uses. 
 
 
 As an application of our results, our quantum algorithm can be used to implement the pretty good measurement (PGM)  [ 1 ,  2 ,  3 ,  4 ,  5 ] . This measurement was used in  [ 12 ,  15 ]  as part of a coding scheme to approach the Holevo information rate for classical communication over a quantum channel. It has also been instrumental in proving bounds for quantum algorithms. Ref.  [ 33 ]  showed that the PGM
is an optimal measurement for solving the dihedral hidden subgroup problem and that it is helpful in proving a lower bound on the sample complexity of this problem. Similar techniques have been used for quantum probably-approximately-correct learning  [ 34 ] .
Ref.  [ 35 ]  showed how to implement the PGM for pure states, while our algorithm for Petz recovery channels is capable of performing the PGM in the general case. 
 
 
 We now begin the technical part of our paper, starting with an explicit description of the Petz recovery channel and the resources that we work with for its implementation.

 Block-encoding —The Petz recovery channel depends on the state  σ A \sigma_{A} , and so our algorithm needs some form of access to it. In order to cover a wide range of scenarios, we employ the block-encoding formalism, which generalizes the most common input models for matrices used in quantum algorithms  [ 36 ,  37 ] . 
 
 
 Let  ∥ ⋅ ∥ \lVert\cdot\rVert  denote the spectral norm of a matrix (also known as the Schatten  ∞ \infty -norm).

\cdot&\cdot\end{array}\right]\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A=\alpha(\langle 0|\otimes I)U(|0\rangle\otimes I). 
 
 (6)

If the linear map  A / α A/\alpha  acts on  a a  qubits, then the unitary  U U  can be thought of as a probabilistic implementation of this map: given an  a a -qubit input state  | ψ ⟩ , |\psi\rangle,  applying the unitary  U U  to the state  | 0 ⟩ ​ | ψ ⟩ , |0\rangle|\psi\rangle,  measuring the first system, and post-selecting on the  | 0 ⟩ |0\rangle  outcome, the second system contains a state proportional to  A ​ | ψ ⟩ / α A|\psi\rangle/\alpha .

 Quantum singular value transformation —Our implementation is based on quantum singular value transformation (QSVT)  [ 37 ] . QSVT transforms the singular values of a block-encoded matrix and thus provides an efficient means of quantum matrix arithmetic. Often we need to rely on approximations, and so when doing so, we keep track of the error/precision  δ \delta , as well as the sub-normalization factor  α \alpha : we say that  U U  is an  ( α , δ ) (\alpha,\delta) -block-encoding of  A A  if  ‖ A − α ⁡ ( ⟨ 0 | ⊗ I ) ​ U ​ ( | 0 ⟩ ⊗ I ) ‖ ≤ δ . \left\|A-\alpha(\langle 0|\otimes I)U(|0\rangle\otimes I)\right\|\leq\delta. 
 
 
 In what follows, we manipulate block-encodings  U ρ U^{\rho}  of density operators  ρ \rho . The power of QSVT is that it allows for transforming  U ρ U^{\rho}  to a block-encoding of  f ~ ​ ( ρ ) \tilde{f}(\rho) , where  f ~ \tilde{f}  is a function applied to the singular values of its argument. More precisely,  f ~ \tilde{f}  denotes a polynomial approximation of some function  f f ; in view of the maps given in ( 3 ) and ( 5 ) above, the particular functions of interest here are  f 1 ( x ) ≔ x − 1 / 2 f_{1}(x)\coloneqq x^{-1/2}  and  f 2 ​ ( x ) ≔ x 1 / 2 f_{2}(x)\coloneqq x^{1/2} . 
 
 
 The complexity of realizing the transformed block-encoding unitary  U f ~ ​ ( ρ ) U^{\tilde{f}(\rho)}  is stated in terms of the number of uses of  U ρ U^{\rho}  (which dominates the overall gate complexity), and it depends on the parameters of the functional approximation  f ~ \tilde{f} .
For a function  f f , let  ‖ f ⁡ ( x ) ‖ ℐ ≔ sup x ∈ ℐ | f ⁡ ( x ) | \left\|f(x)\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}\coloneqq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{I}}\left|f(x)\right| .

If  ρ \rho  has minimum singular value  λ min \lambda_{\min} , then it suffices to set  θ ≤ λ min \theta\leq\lambda_{\min} . Since  1 / λ min 1/\lambda_{\min}  behaves like a “condition number” for  ρ \rho , being proportional to the difficulty of transforming  ρ \rho , we denote it with the symbol  κ \upkappa  and employ this notation later. Indeed, using the functional approximations from  [ 41 ,  41 ] , QSVT achieves the desired transformations up to the errors indicated above, with  𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 θ ​ log ⁡ 1 δ ) \mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\log\frac{1}{\delta}\right)  uses of  U ρ U^{\rho} . 
 
 
 The quantum algorithm —We implement the isometric extension of the Petz recovery channel given in ( 9 ). This consists of applying the maps in ( 3 ), ( 4 ), and ( 5 ) sequentially, with the first and third steps employing QSVT. Eq. ( 9 ) also has a measurement component as the final step, arising from the implementation of the map in ( 4 ). By exploiting the trace-preserving property of the Petz recovery channel, we amplify the probability of success of this measurement (i.e., the projection onto  | 0 ⟩ E ′ |0\rangle_{E^{\prime}} ) using oblivious amplitude amplification  [ 42 ] , which is a special case of QSVT  [ 37 ] . Overall, the implementation is precise up to  ε \varepsilon  error in diamond distance  [ 43 ]  (see  [ 11 ]  for a definition of diamond distance). Theorem  1  below states the guarantees of this technique.

 Conclusion —We have developed a quantum algorithm for implementing the Petz recovery channel and the pretty good measurement. This solves an important open problem in quantum computation, and more generally, it opens up a new research paradigm for realizing fully quantum Bayesian inference on quantum computers.

 Definition 2  (Subnormalized density operator)
