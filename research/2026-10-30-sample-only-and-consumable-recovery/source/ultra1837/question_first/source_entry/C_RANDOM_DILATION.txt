 Abstract 
    
 We present a quantum circuit that implements the  random dilation superchannel , transforming parallel queries of an unknown quantum channel into the same number of parallel queries of a randomly chosen dilation isometry of the input channel.
This is a natural generalization of the random purification channel, that transforms copies of an unknown mixed state to copies of a randomly chosen purification state.
The circuit complexity of our construction is  O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( n , log ⁡ d ℐ , log ⁡ d 𝒪 ) ) O(\mathrm{poly}(n,\log d_{\mathcal{I}},\log d_{\mathcal{O}})) , where  n n  is the number of queries and  d ℐ d_{\mathcal{I}}  and  d 𝒪 d_{\mathcal{O}}  are the input and output dimensions of the input channel, respectively.
This random dilation superchannel is extended to the sequential queries approximately, by transforming the parallel random dilation isometry into sequential random dilation unitaries with  O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( d ℐ ) ) O(\mathrm{poly}(d_{\mathcal{I}}))  overhead in the number of queries.
We also show that our results can be further extended to the case of quantum superchannels.
On the other hand, we show a no-go theorem on the exact random dilation of sequential queries with  o ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( min ⁡ { d ℐ , d 𝒪 } ) ) o(\mathrm{poly}(\min\{d_{\mathcal{I}},d_{\mathcal{O}}\}))  query overhead, showcasing a fundamental difference between the parallel and sequential cases.
As an application, we show an efficient storage-and-retrieval of an unknown quantum channel, which improves the program cost exponentially in the retrieval error  ε \varepsilon .
For the case where the Kraus rank  r r  is the least possible (i.e.,  r = d ℐ / d 𝒪 r=d_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\mathcal{O}} ), we show quantum circuits that transform  n n  parallel queries of an unknown quantum channel  Λ \Lambda  to  Θ ⁡ ( n α ) \Theta(n^{\alpha})  parallel queries of  Λ \Lambda  for any  α < 2 \alpha<2  approximately, and implement its Petz recovery map for the maximally mixed reference state probabilistically and exactly. 
  
 
 
 I  Introduction 

 
 Any quantum state  ρ ∈ ℒ ⁡ ( ℂ d ) \rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d}) 1 1 
             1 
            
            
            
          In this work,  ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒱 ) \mathcal{L}(\mathcal{V})  denotes the set of linear operators acting on the vector space  𝒱 \mathcal{V} .  can be represented as a reduced state of a pure state  | ψ ⟩ ∈ ℂ d ⊗ ℂ d \ket{\psi}\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbb{C}^{d}  called a purification of  ρ \rho , i.e.,  ρ = Tr ℂ d ⁡ [ | ψ ⟩ ⟨ ψ | ] \rho=\Tr_{\mathbb{C}^{d}}[\outerproduct{\psi}{\psi}] .
Although universal transformation from a mixed state to its purification is impossible due to the no-purification theorem  [ 1 ] , it is possible to construct a quantum channel that transforms  n n  copies of an unknown quantum state  ρ \rho  into  n n  copies of a  randomly chosen  purification of  ρ \rho   [ 2 ,  3 ,  4 ] .
We call such a quantum channel the  random purification channel  following Ref.  [ 5 ] .
This technique is used to lift the distinguishability of mixed states to that of bipartite pure states  [ 2 ] , lift the property testing of mixed states to that of bipartite pure states  [ 3 ] , lift the sample access to the query access of a state preparation unitary  [ 4 ] , realize smaller sampling cost for fidelity estimation  [ 6 ] , construct the optimal mixed state tomography  [ 7 ]  and channel tomography  [ 8 ] , and show Uhlmann’s theorem for quantum divergences  [ 5 ] , with extensions to the fermionic and bosonic systems  [ 9 ,  10 ] , showcasing the power of random purification in analyzing various operational tasks. 
 
 
 This series of developments motivates us to extend the notion of random purification to quantum channels.
Any quantum channel  Λ : ℒ ⁡ ( ℐ ) → ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒪 ) \Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})  admits its Stinespring representation  Λ ⁡ ( ⋅ ) = Tr ⁡ [ V ⁡ ( ⋅ ) ​ V † ] \Lambda(\cdot)=\Tr[V(\cdot)V^{\dagger}]  using an isometry  V : ℐ → ℰ ⊗ 𝒪 V:\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{E}\otimes\mathcal{O}  called the dilation isometry and an auxiliary system  ℰ \mathcal{E} .
Since the Stinespring dilation can be considered as a natural generalization of the purification, we consider the following task: given  n n  queries of an unknown quantum channel  Λ : ℒ ⁡ ( ℐ ) → ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒪 ) \Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O}) , implement  n n  queries of a randomly chosen dilation isometry of  Λ \Lambda .
We call such a universal transformation  random dilation superchannels , where superchannels are universal transformations of a channel to another channel implementable within quantum mechanics  [ 11 ,  12 ,  13 ,  14 ] .
Since processing unknown quantum channels in a quantum circuit is often more challenging than processing their Stinespring dilation  [ 15 ,  16 ] , it is of great interest to decide if such a random dilation superchannel exists and is efficiently implementable.
Indeed, the feasibility of constructing random dilation superchannels was posed as an important open problem in Ref.  [ 4 ] .
In Ref.  [ 17 ] , a transformation of a quantum tester, a special type of quantum superchannels that transform a quantum channel to a measurement outcome, is presented.
However, it is difficult to assess whether it is efficiently implementable in a quantum circuit, since it relies on an existence proof in representation theory, and no explicit quantum circuit is provided. 
 
 
 This work provides a partial answer to the above open problem by constructing an efficient quantum circuit implementing the random dilation superchannel that transforms  n n  parallel queries of an unknown quantum channel  Λ \Lambda  into  n n  parallel queries of a randomly chosen dilation isometry of  Λ \Lambda .
Our construction is based on the quantum Fourier transform over the symmetric group and the quantum Schur transform, both of which can be efficiently implemented  [ 18 ,  19 ,  20 ,  21 ,  22 ] .
By using this random dilation superchannel for the unitary superreplication algorithm  [ 23 ] , we show a superreplication algorithm of an unknown quantum channel  Λ : ℒ ⁡ ( ℐ ) → ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒪 ) \Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O})  with Kraus rank at most  r = d ℐ / d 𝒪 r=d_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\mathcal{O}}  that transforms  n n  parallel queries of  Λ \Lambda  to  Θ ⁡ ( n α ) \Theta(n^{\alpha})  parallel queries of  Λ \Lambda  for any  α < 2 \alpha<2 .
We also construct a quantum circuit implementing the Petz recovery map  [ 24 ]  of an unknown quantum channel  Λ \Lambda  with at most Kraus rank  r = d ℐ / d 𝒪 r=d_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\mathcal{O}}  probabilistically and exactly based on the probabilistic unitary inversion algorithm  [ 25 ,  26 ] .
We also extend our results to further higher-order cases from states and channels, namely, the random dilation of quantum superchannels. We construct a quantum circuit implementing the random dilation supersuperchannel that transforms  n n  parallel queries of an unknown quantum superchannel into  n n  parallel queries of a randomly chosen dilation superchannel of the input superchannel.
By using a similar idea to the sample-to-query lifting of Ref.  [ 4 ] , we also show an approximate implementation of the sequential random dilation superchannel by transforming the parallel random dilation isometry into sequential random dilation unitaries with  O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( d ℐ ) ) O(\mathrm{poly}(d_{\mathcal{I}}))  overhead in the number of queries, which is exponential in the number of qubits in the input system. 
 
 
 On the other hand, we also show that the exact random dilation of sequential queries of an unknown quantum channel is  impossible  with subexponential overhead  o ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( min ⁡ { d ℐ , d 𝒪 } ) ) o(\mathrm{poly}(\min\{d_{\mathcal{I}},d_{\mathcal{O}}\}))  in the number of queries,

 The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In Sec.  II , we summarize the representation theory and quantum superchannels.
In Sec.  III , we present the quantum circuits implementing the random dilation superchannel.
By constructing a random purification channel based on the quantum Fourier transform over the symmetric group and the quantum Schur transform in Sec.  III.1 , we construct the random dilation superchannel and the random dilation supersuperchannel in Sec.  III.2 .
We also construct another quantum circuit of the parallel random dilation superchannel based on the Clebsch–Gordan transform over the symmetric group in Sec.  III.3 .
We present an approximate transformation of parallel random dilation isometries to sequential random dilation unitaries in Sec.  III.4  to obtain an approximate implementation of the sequential random dilation superchannel.
In Sec.  IV , we show the no-go theorem on the exact random dilation of sequential queries of an unknown quantum channel.
In Sec.  V , we present three applications of the random dilation superchannel: storage and retrieval of quantum channels, superreplication of quantum channels, and probabilistic implementation of the Petz recovery map of quantum channels.
Finally, we conclude this work in Sec.  VI . 
 
 
 
 II  Preliminaries 

 
 We summarize the essential notions of representation theory of the unitary group and the symmetric group, and quantum superchannels.
Readers familiar with representation theory can skip Sec.  II.1 , and readers familiar with quantum superchannels can skip Secs.  II.2  and  II.3 .

 II.2  Quantum superchannels 

 
 A quantum superchannel is a linear map that transforms quantum channels to quantum channels.
It is given as a linear map

 As a natural extension of the parallel random dilation superchannel, we consider the task of sequential random dilation of quantum channels.
With  n n  queries of an unknown quantum channel  Λ : ℒ ⁡ ( ℐ ) → ℒ ⁡ ( 𝒪 ) \Lambda:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{I})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{O}) , we aim to exactly implement sequential  m m  queries of the dilation isometry  V V  of  Λ \Lambda  where  V V  is randomly chosen from  Dil r ​ ( Λ ) \mathrm{Dil}_{r}(\Lambda) .
However, we show that such a transformation is impossible for any  m ≥ 2 m\geq 2  and  n ≤ max ⁡ ( 2 , 2 ⌊ log 2 ⁡ min ⁡ ( d ℐ , d 𝒪 ) ⌋ − 1 ) n\leq\max(2,2^{\lfloor\log_{2}\min(d_{\mathcal{I}},d_{\mathcal{O}})\rfloor}-1)  within the conventional quantum circuit model, where  d ℐ = dim ( ℐ ) d_{\mathcal{I}}=\dim(\mathcal{I})  and  d 𝒪 = dim ( 𝒪 ) d_{\mathcal{O}}=\dim(\mathcal{O}) .
To show this, we first define the task of sequential random dilation more rigorously and then show the no-go theorem.

 VI  Conclusion 

 
 This work provides an efficient quantum circuit transforming  n n  parallel queries of an unknown quantum channel into  n n  parallel queries of a randomly chosen dilation isometry of the channel.
This transformation enables us to generalize various quantum information processing tasks for unitary and isometry channels to those for general quantum channels.
In particular, we show our protocol enables an efficient storage and-retrieval for unknown quantum channels with an exponentially improved program cost in the retrieval error, together with two other applications: superreplication of quantum channels and probabilistic implementation of the Petz recovery map of quantum channels when the Kraus rank of the input quantum channel is at most  d ℐ / d 𝒪 d_{\mathcal{I}}/d_{\mathcal{O}} .
We also show the extension to the case of quantum superchannels.
Our results open a new avenue for exploring quantum information processing tasks on general quantum channels by leveraging techniques originally developed for unitary or isometry channels. 
 
 
 Although this work positively answers a part of the open problem raised in Ref.  [ 4 ] , our result also provides a negative answer to the other part of the open problem, which is whether there exists a quantum circuit implementing the random dilation superchannel with sequential queries of the input quantum channel.
We show that such an exact sequential random dilation superchannel is impossible with subexponential overhead  o ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( min ⁡ { d ℐ , d 𝒪 } ) ) o(\mathrm{poly}(\min\{d_{\mathcal{I}},d_{\mathcal{O}}\}))  in Thm.  8 .
This no-go result showcases the fundamental difference between parallel and sequential queries of quantum channels.
It also poses an open problem for future work: finding a quantum circuit that implements the  approximate  or  probabilistic  sequential random dilation superchannel.
By transforming the parallel random dilation isometry obtained by Thm.  3  into sequential random dilation unitaries by Thm.  5 , we can implement an approximate implementation of the sequential random dilation superchannel.
The port-based teleportation  [ 49 ,  50 ,  51 ]  can transform parallel queries into sequential ones approximately or probabilistically.
However, both of them incur  O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( d ℐ ) ) O(\mathrm{poly}(d_{\mathcal{I}}))  overhead in the number of queries to achieve constant success probability or approximation error.
It remains open whether we can construct a sequential random dilation superchannel with  O ⁡ ( poly ⁡ ( log ⁡ d ) ) O(\mathrm{poly}(\log d))  overhead in the number of queries to achieve a constant success probability or approximation error.

 Appendix A  Another construction of random dilation superchannel 

 
 Here we present the proof that the quantum circuit shown in Fig.  4  implements the random dilation superchannel.
We apply the proof idea of Thm.  3 , which is to convert the random purification channel to the random dilation superchannel by using the P-CTC.
By applying this idea to the random purification channel shown in Ref.  [ 4 ] , we obtain the quantum circuit shown in Fig.  10  (a).
By decomposing the quantum Schur transform  U Sch ( n , d ℐ ​ d 𝒪 ) U_{\mathrm{Sch}}^{(n,d_{\mathcal{I}}d_{\mathcal{O}})}  into  U Sch ( n , d ℐ ) U_{\mathrm{Sch}}^{(n,d_{\mathcal{I}})}  and  U Sch ( n , d 𝒪 ) U_{\mathrm{Sch}}^{(n,d_{\mathcal{O}})}  using the Kronecker transform, we obtain the quantum circuit shown in Fig.  10  (b).
By bending the wires in the P-CTCs, we obtain the quantum circuit shown in Fig.  10  (c), where the control on  | i μ ⟩ \ket{i_\mu}  means that the Young diagram  μ \mu  is extracted from  | i μ ⟩ \ket{i_\mu} , and it is used to control the gate  CG μ ​ ν \mathrm{CG}_{\mu\nu} .
Note that the standard encoding of  i μ i_{\mu}  has  μ \mu  in the last system  [ 22 ] .
In Fig.  10  (c), a P-CTC only appears after the inverse Kronecker transform  CG μ ​ ν † \mathrm{CG}_{\mu\nu}^{\dagger} , and this P-CTC can be removed by considering the following relation on the Clebsch–Gordan coefficients [see Eq. (10), p. 298 in Ref.  [ 58 ] ]: 
 

 
 
 ⟨ i μ | ​ ⟨ a | ​ CG λ ​ ν ​ | i λ ⟩ ​ | i ν ⟩ = m μ m λ ​ ⟨ i λ | ​ ⟨ a | ​ CG μ ​ ν ​ | i μ ⟩ ​ | i ν ⟩ . \displaystyle\bra{i_\mu}\bra{a}\mathrm{CG}_{\lambda\nu}\ket{i_{\lambda}}\ket{i_{\nu}}=\sqrt{m_{\mu}\over m_{\lambda}}\bra{i_\lambda}\bra{a}\mathrm{CG}_{\mu\nu}\ket{i_\mu}\ket{i_\nu}. 
 
 (72) 
 
 From this expression, the inverse Kronecker transform  CG μ ​ ν † \mathrm{CG}_{\mu\nu}^{\dagger}  can be replaced with the inverse Kronecker transform  CG λ ​ ν † \mathrm{CG}_{\lambda\nu}^{\dagger}  without P-CTCs, and we obtain the quantum circuit shown in Fig.  4 .
