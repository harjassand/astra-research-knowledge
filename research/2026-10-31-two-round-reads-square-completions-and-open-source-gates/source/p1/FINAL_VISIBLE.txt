1. The organizing mechanism: make the remaining target easy to measure

The important choice is what the first measurements should accomplish.

For a nonlinear target, a measurement can have little immediate predictive value while revealing which later measurement would be valuable. The rare-gate construction already exposed this phenomenon. Here it leads to a constructive strategy:

Choose a first batch that removes much of the nonlinear dependence on the unobserved input.
Use its outcomes to identify the remaining linear functional.
Measure that functional directly.

For the quadratic problem, this can be done exactly. The first batch is chosen from the target’s positive and negative eigendirections. Opposing signs allow a rotation that turns a quadratic interaction into an observed coefficient multiplying an unobserved linear quantity. One final measurement can collect all those remaining linear contributions together.

This is relevant to goal-oriented sensing: collect information to estimate a particular scientific quantity accurately. Existing work by Neuberger, Alexanderian, and van Bloemen Waanders already develops quadratic approximations, Gaussian posterior-variance formulas, and computational methods for this objective. Their designs select sensor locations or weights before observing outcomes. The opening pursued here is the additional power of an outcome-dependent measurement direction. Their physical sensor constraints and matrix-free computational alternatives remain substantive comparators; arbitrary programmable directions are a stronger access contract. Primary manuscript, particularly Theorems 3.1–3.2
