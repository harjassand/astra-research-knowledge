PAID FORWARD CERTIFICATES AND EXACT ADAPTIVE INVERSE TASKS
24 October 2026. Lead derivation after the frozen opening; no novelty claim.

1. What finite observations cannot certify

For an otherwise arbitrary C-infinity map on a compact domain with nonempty
interior, finitely many point evaluations do not imply a nontrivial global
range envelope. Fix any finite adaptive transcript from the constant-zero
map. There is an open ball disjoint from its queried points. A smooth bump
supported inside that ball, multiplied by any amplitude, produces exactly
the same transcript, including any finitely many derivatives queried there.
Consequently a finite upper envelope inferred only from this transcript is
invalid for some smooth compatible map. If range [0,1] is prior knowledge,
the same argument still prevents reducing the bound below one in an unseen
region. This is a transcript-extension obstruction, not a claim that finite
random data can never give probabilistic conclusions under a declared class.

Randomization does not repair an assumption-free uniform certificate. For
example, place J disjoint candidate bump supports in the domain. An algorithm
using at most B point queries in expectation can hit at most B supports in
expectation. At least one fixed support is hit with probability at most B/J
under the null. Coupling the runs until that support is hit leaves the entire
data/output transcript unchanged on its miss event. A conclusion that requires
excluding all such bumps therefore needs a declared modulus/class, validated
program access, or a weaker probabilistic target. No true modulus is learned
merely by fitting the observed samples.

Our interface instead supplies known finite recurrence code. Each paid source
query executes its validated range extension. The code and its invariant are
legitimate shared priors; its cell values/ranges are computed experience.

2. Fixed solver and finite interface

Let Q=2^J. Parameters are the Q^2 dyadic cell centers
 a_i=3+(i+1/2)/Q,
 b_l=1/8+(3/4)(l+1/2)/Q,    0<=i,l<Q.
The known program is
 u_0=b, u_{j+1}=a u_j(1-u_j), f(a,b)=u_K.
This is a nonlinear numerical inverse problem with aliases/folds, not a
validated population model or physical experiment. The uniform prior on this
finite grid is shared by all controls. Other known finite priors are covered
by the sampling proof if exact cell masses/conditional draws are available.

At a dyadic tree node C, form the smallest coordinate intervals containing
its grid centers (a singleton at depth J). Iterate exact rational intervals.
For I subset [0,1], g(u)=u(1-u) has minimum at an endpoint and maximum at an
endpoint or 1/2. Multiply its exact range by the nonnegative a interval;
intersect with [0,1]. The invariant follows because 0<=a<=4 and 0<=g<=1/4.
Intersect a child's final interval with its parent's final interval. This is
a sound nested certifier with no floating-point remainder claim. Every new
node execution is paid, including K interval iterations and rational widths.
At a singleton it returns the exact finite recurrence value.

Since |g'(u)|<=1 on [0,1],
 width I_{j+1} <= 4 width I_j + width(a_C)/4.
Thus
 width I_C <= 4^K width(b_C)+(4^K-1)width(a_C)/12
            <= C_K 2^{-depth(C)},
 C_K=(3/4)4^K+(4^K-1)/12=(5/6)4^K-1/12.
This conservative analysis constant exposes exponential dependence on K.
The learner does not receive a true local Lipschitz constant; it executes
the specified interval program. Interval overestimation can make it useless.

Each new task supplies integers 0<=s<=n and known 0<epsilon<1/2:
 p=epsilon+(1-2epsilon)f, W=p^s(1-p)^(n-s).
At an interval I_C, exact likelihood extrema use its p endpoints plus s/n
when 0<s<n and that point lies inside. All weights are strictly positive.
One uniform bound is
 |d log W/df| <= Lambda=n(1-2epsilon)/epsilon.
Counts n need not be fixed, but growing n raises the cost envelope. Rescaling
W by a known task-only positive constant changes neither posterior nor
Lambda; normalization does not remove posterior concentration.

3. Exact sampling with an acquired tree

Immediately before a proposal, condition on the full past. Its current task
and tree partition are now fixed. With exact prior mass pi_C and bounds
0<L_C<=W(x)<=U_C, write Z_U=sum_C pi_C U_C and Z=sum_x pi(x)W(x).
Choose C with probability pi_C U_C/Z_U, then x from pi restricted to C.
Draw an independent uniform u. Retain U_old=U_C throughout this proposal.
Nested refinements decide the same event
                       u < W(x)/U_old.                   (1)
They may depend on both x and u, but do not alter the event. In particular,
an early accept uses L_child/U_old and early reject U_child/U_old. Replacing
this denominator with a child height is wrong unless the proposal law is
changed consistently. The exact accepted mass is
 q(x) Pr(accept|x,past) = pi(x)W(x)/Z_U.                   (2)
Conditional on success this is the desired posterior pi(x)W(x)/Z.

After a rejected proposal, the next tree may depend on its candidate and
threshold. Formula (2) applies again given that new preproposal history.
Summing over histories of the first successful trial mixes identical target
posteriors. Therefore each output has the correct posterior conditional on
the history at its TASK START. A later task can depend arbitrarily on earlier
outputs and paid queries, provided its own prior/weight is fixed while drawing
that output and all new randomness is fresh.

Do not claim posterior independence conditional on the post-acceptance tree:
that tree can encode information about the accepted candidate. Future draws
remain correct conditional on their own starting history through (2).

4. Exact counterexample to a tempting changed denominator

For K=1, a=3, b in {1/4,1/2}, epsilon=1/4, s=n=1, exact forward values are
(9/16,3/4), hence W=(17/32,20/32). The quadratic interval extension gives
the exact parent bounds L=17/32,U_old=20/32. The proposal is uniform.
Pinned acceptance probabilities are 17/20 and 1, so accepted probabilities
are (17/37,20/37). If an ambiguous proposal refines to its singleton and
replaces U_old by W(x), its new cutoff is one. The initial squeeze plus this
wrong refinement accepts both points surely and produces (1/2,1/2).
This is bias from a changed proposal correction, not an interval-rounding
effect. The exact fractional replay checks this example.

5. Termination, randomness and what is actually exact

Each pending finite-grid decision uses at most J successive splits before
its singleton weight is exact. Threshold comparison with a rational can be
implemented using a lazily revealed fair-bit binary uniform: extend its prefix
until its dyadic interval lies wholly on one side of the rational threshold.
This terminates almost surely, with unbounded worst-case bits. Categorical
draws with rational masses likewise have exact fair-bit implementations.
Ideal independent fair bits are the mathematical interface. A seeded PRNG
replay checks implementation, not physical independence of its random tape.

The rational denominator does not itself force that many random bits. For
m fixed rational cutoffs in [0,1], after k uniform bits the prefix interval
can cross a cutoff in at most m dyadic bins. Therefore
 Pr(more than k bits) <= min(1,m 2^{-k}),
and expected bits to identify the resulting interval are at most
ceil(log2 m)+2 for m>=1 (zero if m=0). A lazy-uniform CDF categorical draw
with G categories thus uses O(log G) expected random bits even if exact CDF
fractions have thousands of bits. Big-integer comparison work still costs.
Conditional on its candidate and preproposal history, the nested decision
path has at most 2(J+1) deterministic likelihood-ratio cutoffs, so the same
argument bounds its uniform-threshold bit consumption by O(log(J+1)). This
is a conventional exact-randomness implementation observation, not a new
sampler or a bound on arithmetic precision. Refining just because a current
bit prefix overlaps a cutoff can cause extra source calls; our source-count
theorem instead assumes each rational comparison is decided exactly before
testing whether the true uniform is inside the certified uncertainty band.

For each fixed finite task Z>0 and Z_U<=1, so every proposal has conditional
acceptance probability at least Z. Tail domination by (1-Z)^a gives almost
sure completion and finite expected proposals for that task. At task start,
c=Z_L/Z_U>0, Z_L=sum pi_C L_C, is an acquired computable lower acceptance
certificate. During that task refinement decreases Z_U; expected proposals
are at most 1/c. Across arbitrarily adaptive tasks, finite expected aggregate
cost additionally requires an integrable sum of reciprocal certificates;
individual finite task times alone do not imply this expectation is finite.

These guarantees concern exact rational code, finite grid, and fresh ideal
random bits. They do not validate a continuous posterior, a floating-point
simulator, a physical law, or a useful runtime advantage. Exact response-table
enumeration for independent checking is evaluator work and is not passed to
the learner. Full enumeration remains an allowed paid conventional control.

6. Conventional boundary

The basic interval envelope/proposal/acceptance mechanism is established:
Sainudiin and York, An Auto-validating Rejection Sampler, arXiv:math/0611479,
Sections 3.1--3.2 and Lemmas 1--2. OS* (Dymetman et al., arXiv:1207.0742)
also adapts exact rejection bounds from earlier rejected candidates. A*
sampling (Maddison et al., arXiv:1411.0030) is a strong bound-aware incumbent.
Our retained FORWARD intervals can be recomposed with new likelihoods; that
is a cache/validated-solver representation and is not claimed new merely
because it is used for multiple tasks. The next theorem isolates a uniform
cross-task source-cost consequence. Priority and algorithmic advantage of
that consequence remain unestablished; standard retained interval rejection
must receive the same code, arithmetic, queries and prior assumptions.
