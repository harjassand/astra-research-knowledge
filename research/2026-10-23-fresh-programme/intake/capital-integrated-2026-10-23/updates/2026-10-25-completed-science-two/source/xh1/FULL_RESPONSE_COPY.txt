# Astra Chemical Invention — A product-controlled chemical permission system

## 1. The PNA-shadow architecture needs a correction

The strongest retained construction combines three operations: azide-masked PNA growth, removal of unattached building blocks, and DNA copying from the actual PNA product to authorize its next reaction.

The chemistry remains promising, but I found a weakness in the proposed DNA reader.

The two primary demonstrations of PNA-to-DNA copying are not equivalent to an autonomous four-base molecular checkpoint:

- Misra et al. (1998) demonstrated polymerase recognition of PNA-containing templates and extension of PNA–DNA chimeric primers. This supports polymerase compatibility with certain PNA-containing substrates, but does not establish efficient, repeated copying across newly synthesized PNA amide junctions.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.acs.org\&sz=32)

  ACS Publications

- Kuwayama (2021) demonstrated PNA-templated DNA synthesis using Taq polymerase and subsequent PCR amplification. PCR is a thermally cycled process; that experiment does not establish continuous product reading in the near-neutral, comparatively low-temperature environment of the proposed chemical assembler.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.sciencedirect.com\&sz=32)

  ScienceDirect



There is a second problem. DNA polymerases can sometimes extend across discontinuous templates. García, Robledo and Islas demonstrated this using separate DNA templates, although the corresponding activity has not been quantified for disconnected PNA.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov



Consequently, DNA extension is evidence of available template information, not automatically evidence of the intended organic bond.

I am retaining product-derived DNA copying as a promising additional capability, but not as an already validated chemical permission signal.

The more consequential problem is to construct an interface where the PNA product controls both the availability of a DNA readout and the physical position of the next reactive chemical end.

## 2. A more serious compatibility obstacle: activated donors can modify the DNA-reading enzymes

The earlier design treated the PNA synthesis reaction and DNA-polymerase reading reaction as though putting them in the same aqueous buffer were the main challenge.

That is incomplete.

The activated sulfo-NHS esters used for PNA coupling are amine-reactive. Protein enzymes, including DNA polymerases, contain lysine side chains and other potentially accessible amino groups.

NHS reagents are routinely used to covalently modify proteins. Their reactions can alter enzyme structure or activity, depending on the modification site.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed

+1



Therefore, a continuously mixed solution containing active PNA esters and the proposed DNA-reading polymerases has a potential enzyme-acylation pathway that the previous construction did not account for.

For a simplified enzyme population whose essential reactive sites are modified with instantaneous hazard

\\[ \lambda_E(t)=k_E D(t), \\]

where \\(D(t)\\) is accessible active-ester concentration, the surviving unmodified fraction is

\\[ \boxed{ S_E(T)=\exp\left(-k_E\int_0^T D(t)\\,dt\right). } \\]

This is exact for the assumed irreversible first-order hazard, not a measured polymerase survival law.

If enzyme modification can occur indefinitely at a constant positive hazard, the unmodified fraction approaches zero. More sophisticated DNA logic does not eliminate this chemical exposure.

The issue is particularly serious for a reusable molecular assembler because polymerases are meant to participate in many chemical cycles.

### Consequence

I am changing the construction from a single, permanently mixed chemical environment to a spatially separated but continuously operating chemical process.

The objective remains independent molecular progression, but the active-ester synthesis chemistry and enzymatic product-reading chemistry would occupy distinct local environments.

This is not the same as externally synchronizing every molecule through a common synthesis cycle. Different molecules could enter the chemical and reading operations at different times, retaining their individual instruction positions.

The cost is that transport, boundary chemistry, molecular retention and readout recovery become real parts of the manufacturing process.

## 3. New integrated construction: a circulating, product-qualified PNA carrier

The proposed architecture now has three functional environments rather than one permanently mixed reaction bath.

Continuous molecular transaction — proposed topology

A. Chemical incorporation

A mobile carrier retains a DNA-encoded PNA parent through a permanent covalent root. Its current free terminal amine can react with the next DNA-templated, azide-masked PNA tetramer.

Correct coupling produces a longer, chemically dormant PNA chain. Hydrolysis or donor departure can leave the original parent competent.

B. Electrophile-quenching boundary

Free activated donors are captured or hydrolyzed before encountering protein enzymes. Short, unincorporated PNA donors must dissociate from their DNA recognition sites and be cleared.

The long, covalently rooted PNA parent remains with its carrier.

C. Enzymatic product qualification

A first DNA-polymerase operation removes residual PNA–DNA delivery-template pairing. A second, separately controlled DNA primer interrogates the retained PNA product.

Only a sufficiently qualified new PNA segment should generate a DNA signal permitting terminal-azide reduction.

D. Reactivation and return

The successful product's new amine is regenerated. Temporary DNA-reading structures are cleared and the synthesis template restored.

An unsuccessful parent returns chemically unchanged to its previous instruction; a successful parent returns competent for its next instruction.

This changes an important requirement.

The different chemical operations need not all function in precisely the same local solvent composition or in the presence of precisely the same proteins and activated reagents. They must instead be compatible with transporting and preserving the same rooted molecular product between environments.

That interface is not free. It introduces transport times, reagent quenching, enzyme retention, DNA-template regeneration and potential loss of molecular carriers.

### A real precedent for the first enzymatic operation

Brudno, Birnbaum, Kleiner and Liu (2010) demonstrated a closely related physical operation.

After DNA-templated PNA synthesis, they used Herculase II DNA polymerase to extend a DNA hairpin along the encoding template. This replaced the PNA–DNA duplex with a DNA–DNA duplex while maintaining the covalent association between the translated PNA and its encoding DNA.

A restriction-enzyme assay supported displacement of the PNA.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PMC



This gives us an experimentally established way to separate a newly formed PNA product from the original DNA instruction template without severing its covalent ancestry.

It does not distinguish a completed organic bond from hydrolysis. Polymerase displacement would also release an unreacted PNA donor.

That is why the subsequent reading operation must interrogate the retained PNA chain, not the fact that DNA displacement occurred.

## 4. A physical product-to-permission interface

The most useful revised mechanism combines template-end copying with chemically masked PNA propagation.

Consider a growing PNA chain containing \\(4i\\) bases, with its newly added tetramer occupying the last four bases.

A DNA read primer is designed to hybridize to an older portion of the rooted PNA, ending immediately before the newly added tetramer.

If incorporation succeeded, the PNA molecule contains another four bases beyond the primer. DNA polymerase may be able to copy them.

If incorporation failed and the free donor was cleared, the rooted PNA terminates at the old position.

The distinguishing physical operation is:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Rooted PNA extension} \longrightarrow \text{additional DNA primer extension}. } \\]

The product's own terminal position supplies a natural stopping boundary. In the ideal orientation, the polymerase would reach the end of the PNA template after copying the new four-base segment.

This avoids introducing an arbitrary DNA stop sequence after every instruction.

### Turning the DNA copy into chemical permission

There is a credible, although still unacquired, route for the next operation.

The four newly synthesized DNA bases create a specific terminal sequence that the unextended read primer lacks.

A DNA gate could recognize that completed sequence and release a phosphine-bearing nucleic-acid probe targeted near the corresponding PNA terminus.

This is related to experimentally demonstrated primer-exchange and polymerase-dependent strand-displacement mechanisms:

- Kishi and colleagues demonstrated programmable, autonomous DNA primer-exchange cascades.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

  PMC

- Derauf and Thachuk developed polymerase-dependent toehold-mediated strand-displacement gates, including a mechanism for producing sequence-specific outputs only after the appropriate DNA input reaction. Their general leak-suppression framework is principally theoretical, with preliminary experimental demonstrations.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://drops.dagstuhl.de\&sz=32)

  drops.dagstuhl.de



The specific proposed transaction is therefore:

\\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{Read primer copies new PNA bases}\\\ &\quad\Downarrow\\\ &\text{New DNA terminal sequence becomes available}\\\ &\quad\Downarrow\\\ &\text{DNA gate recruits a local phosphine}\\\ &\quad\Downarrow\\\ &\text{PNA terminal azide is reduced}\\\ &\quad\Downarrow\\\ &\text{Next chemical incorporation becomes possible}. \end{aligned} \\]

Altrichter and colleagues provide the relevant primary chemical precedent. Their PNA-templated phosphine system gave approximately 60% azide reduction in 15 minutes and approached 70% after 60 minutes under a pH-7 MOPS-buffer condition. That was a different PNA-conjugated substrate and cannot be interpreted as a successful reduction of the proposed growing PNA end.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

Wiley Online Library



The remaining interface is specific: a newly copied DNA terminal sequence must recruit reducing chemistry near the correct, still-masked PNA end while leaving unused donors and unrelated programs largely unaffected.

One complication deserves particular attention. Newly synthesized DNA will initially be hybridized to its PNA template. The downstream gate must either act on a physically accessible part of that DNA or remove it through a real strand-exchange operation.

Merely specifying a newly copied DNA sequence does not prove that another oligonucleotide can access it.

## 5. A new scaling obstruction: repeatedly copying the entire growing polymer is expensive

The two-pass construction initially appeared to offer a straightforward way to reuse Brudno's established strand-displacement machinery.

However, their demonstrated operation displaces an entire translated PNA chain after synthesis.

Applying that same whole-chain operation after every incorporation creates a quadratic cost.

Suppose \\(L\\) building blocks are incorporated and each contains \\(b\\) PNA bases.

After stage \\(j\\), the PNA chain contains \\(bj\\) bases.

If the first DNA-polymerase operation must displace all \\(bj\\) bases every time, the total number of DNA nucleotides polymerized solely for this displacement is

\\[ N\_{\rm displacement} =b\sum\_{j=1}^{L}j =\boxed{\frac{bL(L+1)}{2}}. \\]

That is \\(O(L^2)\\) nucleotide consumption.

If a second DNA-reading operation copies the newest \\(b\\) PNA bases once per stage, it adds another \\(bL\\) nucleotide incorporations.

For the retained four-base building blocks:

| Program length      | Whole-chain displacement plus local read | Local displacement plus local read |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 20 building blocks  | 920 nucleotides                          | 160 nucleotides                    |
| 100 building blocks | 20,600 nucleotides                       | 800 nucleotides                    |

These are exact nucleotide counts for the stated architectures, not complete material or energy costs.

The difference is substantive. A practical molecular instruction controller needs a bounded local displacement operation, rather than repeatedly rewriting the entire DNA template.

### New architectural requirement

Each instruction should have a local DNA cassette covering a bounded number of PNA bases.

Polymerase displacement should stop at the end of that cassette. The upstream PNA parent remains permanently rooted, while the newest PNA segment becomes accessible for local reading.

This changes the scaling from quadratic to linear in program length.

But it also introduces an additional physical challenge: the local cassette must be reset after an unsuccessful attempt without unlocking later instructions.

A conventional DNA polymerase can continue beyond the intended cassette unless a genuine molecular terminator or structural stop is supplied.

Brudno's apparatus used a backbone spacer to block polymerization at a predetermined location. That establishes the class of stopping mechanism but not an independently resetting stop for every one of Astra's proposed instructions.&#x20;

## 6. A promising route for recycling DNA instruction cassettes

A further primary mechanism provides a possible reset operation.

Bacteriophage λ exonuclease preferentially digests DNA strands from suitable 5′-phosphorylated duplex termini, progressing in the 5′→3′ direction and leaving the complementary strand available.

This has substantial structural and biochemical evidence.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov

+1



Suppose the local polymerase-displacement primer is deliberately provided with a 5′-phosphate, while the persistent DNA coding template is protected from the same digestion route.

After the displacement operation creates a DNA duplex, λ exonuclease could preferentially remove the newly synthesized complementary DNA strand.

The original coding strand would become single-stranded again and could potentially recruit another PNA building block.

This offers a physically specific reset candidate:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{PNA–DNA instruction duplex} \rightarrow \text{DNA–DNA locked duplex} \rightarrow \text{regenerated DNA instruction strand}. } \\]

The middle state is valuable because it can temporarily prevent a new donor from docking while the PNA product is being inspected.

The final transition is needed if the previous chemical attempt failed.

There are important limitations. λ exonuclease has substrate-end requirements, its activity can depend on sequence and DNA-end geometry, and it operates with a different preferred biochemical environment from some of the synthesis chemistry. Noncanonical digestion of modified or nonphosphorylated DNA ends has also been reported.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://doi.org\&sz=32)

Oxford Academic



The persistent coding DNA therefore cannot be assumed immune merely because the newly synthesized strand has the intended phosphate.

This is an experimentally grounded candidate for the reset operation, not a demonstrated reusable PNA instruction cassette.

## 7. Exact reliability result for the complete transaction

The physical mechanism now supports a sharper reliability model.

Let one chemical donor encounter have three outcomes:

- Probability \\(p\\): correct incorporation creates a masked PNA product.
- Probability \\(w\\): an irreversible incorrect chemical product or damaged parent.
- Probability \\(h=1-p-w\\): chemically harmless failure.

On the chemically harmless branch, let \\(\alpha\\) be the probability that the subsequent separation and DNA-reading operations nevertheless create an irreversible false advancement.

On the correctly incorporated branch, let \\(v\\) be the probability that the product is eventually correctly read, reduced to a functional amine, and remains able to propagate.

Assuming every genuinely harmless failure restores the same parent state, the renewal equation is

\\[ P\_{\rm step}=pv+h(1-\alpha)P\_{\rm step}. \\]

Therefore,

\\[ \boxed{ P\_{\rm step} = \frac{pv}{p+w+h\alpha}. } \\]

This is exact under the stated renewal assumptions.

It charges failures from all three physical interfaces: incorrect chemical incorporation, false product reading, and loss of the correct product's next functional end.

### Consequence for the existing 20-stage target

For illustration, assume a 20% successful chemical coupling probability per encounter, no other incorrect chemistry, and a 99.98% probability that a correctly joined product survives reading and reactivation.

Then achieving at least 99% correct completion across 20 stages requires

\\[ \boxed{\alpha\leq7.56\times10^{-5}.} \\]

That is fewer than approximately 76 false advancements per million chemically unsuccessful encounters.

Conditional renewal model: p = 0.20, w = 0, v = 0.9998; 20 independent stages.

This calculation is not an experimentally acquired error probability. It identifies how stringent the complete physical interlock must be.

The error cannot simply be assigned to the DNA gates.

Even a theoretically leak-resistant downstream circuit cannot recover the distinction between correct and incorrect chemistry after its input has been falsified.

Formally, if \\(P_Q\\) and \\(P_U\\) are the distributions of the reader's observations under correctly joined and unjoined substrates, and \\(K\\) is any subsequent randomized DNA-processing circuit, then

\\[ \boxed{ \operatorname{TV}(P_QK,P_UK) \leq \operatorname{TV}(P_Q,P_U). } \\]

This is the data-processing inequality for total variation.

It means the downstream DNA machinery can suppress its own spontaneous leakage, but cannot manufacture additional evidence of the organic bond merely by processing an ambiguous molecular readout.

The actual physical discrimination must arise before or during the read of the rooted PNA product.

## 8. A finite-state check identifies the exact unsafe operations

I also implemented an exhaustive state-transition check for three programmed incorporations.

The abstract controller distinguishes:

- A ready parent with an active terminal amine.
- A correctly extended parent with a masked terminal azide.
- The delivery, stripping and reading configurations.
- A chemically successful reading event that unmasks the next terminus.
- Harmless failures that return to the old instruction.

Under these restricted operations, the reachable system contains 13 states and preserves the invariant

\\[ \boxed{ \begin{cases} n=i, & \text{when the growing end is an active amine},\\\ n=i+1, & \text{when the product is masked and awaits qualification}. \end{cases} } \\]

Here \\(n\\) is the actual number of incorporated building blocks and \\(i\\) the current unresolved instruction index.

All three incorporations are reachable in the abstract model without allowing a duplicate insertion.

Adding either of two prohibited physical transitions immediately breaks the invariant.

A false DNA copy after stripping an unjoined donor permits the program counter to advance without incorporation:

\\[ (i,n)=(0,0)\longrightarrow(1,0). \\]

Premature terminal-azide reduction permits a newly incorporated product to become chemically active before its current instruction has been retired:

\\[ (i,n)=(0,1),\quad \text{masked}\longrightarrow\text{active}. \\]

The check establishes the logical necessity of the two safeguards. It does not establish their molecular implementation or reaction kinetics.

The strongest physical test of the first safeguard remains a side-by-side comparison of a correct continuous PNA chain, two unjoined adjacent PNA fragments, and a chemically stable wrong-site connection.

## 9. What survives comparison with the established alternatives?

The strongest competitor remains the Bath–Turberfield chain-transfer architecture because it has a more developed DNA controller and does not require PNA polymerase copying after every synthetic bond.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.rsc.org\&sz=32)

pubs.rsc.org



However, its proposed signal from restoring a DNA 3′-OH still needs chemical qualification: hydrolysis can in principle restore the same functionality without transferring the growing polymer.

The present PNA architecture attacks that weakness differently. It attempts to make the actual rooted product determine the existence of the next readable template segment.

The self-disarming α-azido-phenyl-ester alternative remains useful but weaker as the principal architecture: it gives a chemically plausible way of eliminating unused donors, yet replacing Joshi's established PNA tetramers sacrifices an important experimentally demonstrated recognition system. The phosphorus-mediated reaction fork also retains unresolved product-poisoning and wrong-reaction pathways.

Conventional staged PNA chemistry remains the strongest demonstrated manufacturing comparison. It has none of the proposed DNA-reading requirements, but does not grant independently timed chemical progression.

The revised circulating carrier therefore has a clear potential advantage and a clear cost: independent chemical progression with local retries, purchased through extra DNA synthesis, enzyme reactions, waste quenching and transport.

No measured throughput or material advantage has been established.

## 10. The decisive remaining physical interface

The strongest construction is now:

\\[ \boxed{ \begin{gathered} \text{DNA-rooted PNA parent}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Masked PNA amidation}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Electrophile removal and donor separation}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Local copying of the retained PNA product}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Product-specific DNA authorization}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Terminal amine regeneration}\\\ \Downarrow\\\ \text{Return with the next instruction enabled}. \end{gathered} } \\]

The meaningful progress in this continuation is the replacement of an assumed homogeneous chemical environment with a physically motivated separation-and-reading interface, together with two candidate DNA operations supported by primary molecular mechanisms: polymerase-mediated PNA displacement and selective degradation of a disposable DNA complement.

The main unknown is no longer simply whether a DNA polymerase can recognize PNA. It is whether local PNA copying, DNA-readout release, phosphine recruitment and cassette reset can form a productive serial operation while preserving an intact chemically competent parent.

That question has four decisive constraints: activated esters must not poison the enzymes; free or hydrolyzed donors must not supply the same readout; correct but temporarily unread PNA products must remain safely masked; and the temporary DNA readout must not permanently obstruct the next synthetic reaction.

The experimentally established mechanisms support individual parts of that operation. They do not yet establish their compatible integration. The current construction therefore remains an analytical architecture, with real candidate physical interfaces and quantified failure conditions, rather than a demonstrated molecular assembler.

Its broader scientific principle is product-rooted, recoverable control: each synthetic molecule carries its own chemical progress in a persistent backbone, and downstream chemical activation is conditioned on information obtained from that actual product—not on the passage of a global synthesis clock.

That is the capability worth preserving and extending.
