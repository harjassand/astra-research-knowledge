 Strong data-processing inequalities for channels and Bayesian networks

  Abstract 
    
 The data-processing inequality, that is, I(U;Y)\leq I(U;X) for a Markov chain U\to X\to Y, has been the method of
choice for proving impossibility (converse) results in information theory and many other disciplines. Various
channel-dependent improvements (called strong data-processing inequalities, or SDPIs) of this inequality have been
proposed both classically and more recently. In this note we first survey known results relating various notions of
contraction for a single channel. Then we consider the basic extension: given SDPI for each constituent channel in a
Bayesian network, how does one produce an end-to-end SDPI? 
    
 Our approach is based on the (extract of the) Evans-Schulman
method, which is demonstrated for three different kinds of SDPIs, namely, the usual Ahslwede-Gács type contraction
coefficients (mutual information), Dobrushin’s contraction coefficients (total variation), and finally the F_{I}-curve (the best possible SDPI for a given channel).
As an example, we demonstrate how to obtain SDPI for an n-letter memoryless channel with feedback given an SDPI for n=1. 
    
 Finally, we discuss a simple observation on the equivalence of SDPI and comparison to erasure channel (in the sense of “less
noisy” order). This leads to a simple proof of a curious inequality of Samorodnitsky (2015).

  1  Introduction 

 
 The observation that multiplying a componentwise non-negative vector by a stochastic matrix results in a vector that is
“more uniform” dates back to Birkhoff  [ Bir57 ] . We will not discuss here this type of
distance-contraction (sometimes known as geometric ergodicity). Instead, we will be interested in contraction of
statistical distances and information measures involving probability distributions. 
 
 
 Fix a transition probability kernel (channel) P_{Y|X}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y} acting between two measurable spaces.
We denote
by P_{Y|X}\circ P the distribution on \mathcal{Y} induced by the push-forward of the distribution P, which is the distribution of the output Y when the input X is distributed according to P,
and by P\times P_{Y|X} the joint distribution P_{XY} if P_{X}=P.
We also denote by P_{Z|Y}\circ P_{Y|X} the serial composition of channels. 
 
 
 We define three quantities that will play key role in our discussion: the total variation, the
Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence and the mutual information 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle d_{\rm TV}(P,Q) 
 \displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{E}|P[E]-Q[E]|={1\over 2}\int|{\rm d}P-{\rm d}Q|, 
  
  (1)    
  
  
 \displaystyle D(P\|Q) 
 \displaystyle\triangleq\int\log{{\rm d}P\over{\rm d}Q}\,{\rm d}P, 
  
  (2)    
  
  
 \displaystyle I(A;B) 
 \displaystyle\triangleq D(P_{AB}\|P_{A}P_{B}). 
  
  (3)    
 
 
 
 The purpose of this paper is to give exposition to the phenomenon that upon passing through a non-degenerate noisy channel
distributions become strictly closer and this leads to a loss of information. Namely we have three effects: 
 
  1.  
 
 Total-variation (or Dobrushin) contraction: 
 

  
  
 d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X}\circ P,P_{Y|X}\circ Q)<d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)\,. 
    
 
  
  2.  
 
 Divergence contraction: 
 

  
  
 D(P_{Y|X}\circ P\|P_{Y|X}\circ Q)<D(P\|Q) 
    
 
  
  3.  
 
 Information loss: For any Markov chain  1    1 
                   1 
                  
                  
                  
                The notation A\to B\to C simply means that A\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}C|B.    U\to X\to Y we
have 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)<I(U;X)\,. 
    
 
  
 
 These strict inequalities are collectively referred to as  strong data-processing inequalities  (SDPIs).
The goal of this paper is to show intricate interdependencies between these effects, as well as introducing tools for
quantifying how strict these SDPIs are. 
 
 
 Organization 

 
 In Section   2   we overview the case of a single channel. Notably, most of the results in the literature are proved for
finite alphabets, i.e., |{\mathcal{X}}||{\mathcal{Y}}|<\infty, with a few exceptions such as  [ CKZ98 ,  PW16 ] .
We provide in Appendix   B   a self-contained proof of some of these results for general alphabets. 
 
 
 From then on we focus on the question:  Given a multi-terminal network with a single source and multiple sinks, and given
SDPIs for each of the channels comprising the network, how do we obtain an SDPI for the composite channel from source to
sinks? 
It turns out that this question has been addressed implicitly in the work of Evans and Schulman  [ ES99 ] 
on redundancy required in circuits of noisy gates. Rudiments also appeared in Dawson  [ Daw75 ]  as well as Boyen and Koller  [ BK98 ] . 
 
 
 In Section   3   we present the essence of the Evans-Schulman method and derive upper bounds on the mutual
information contraction coefficient \eta_{\rm KL} for Bayesian networks (directed graphical models). Then in
Section   4   we derive analogous estimates for Dobrushin’s coefficient \eta_{\rm TV} that governs the contraction of
the total variation on networks. While the results exactly parallel those for mutual information, the proof relies on new arguments using coupling.
Finally, Section   5   extends the technique to bounding the F_{I}-curves (the
non-linear SDPIs).
Section   6   concludes with an alternative point of view on mutual information contraction, namely that
of comparison to an erasure channel. As an example we give a short proof of a result of
Samorodnitsky  [ Sam15 ]  about distribution of information in subsets of channel outputs. 
 
 
 
 Notation 

 
 Elements of the Cartesian product {\mathcal{X}}^{n} are denoted x^{n}\triangleq(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}) to emphasize
their dimension. Given a transition probability kernel from P_{Y|X}:{\mathcal{X}}\to{\mathcal{Y}} we denote P_{Y|X}^{n}=P_{Y^{n}|X^{n}} the kernel
acting from {\mathcal{X}}^{n}\to{\mathcal{Y}}^{n} componentwise independently: 
 

  
  
 P_{Y^{n}|X^{n}}(y^{n}|x^{n})\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{n}P_{Y|X}(y_{j}|x_{j}). 
    
 
 To demonstrate the general bounds we consider the running example of P_{Y|X} being an n-letter binary symmetric channel (BSC), given by 
 

  
  
 Y=X+Z,\quad X,Y\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n},~Z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(\delta)^{n} 
  
  (4)    
 
 and denoted by \mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n}. 
 
 
 
 
  2  SDPI for a single channel 

 
  2.1  Contraction coefficients for f-divergence and mutual information 

 
 Let f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
be a convex function that is strictly convex at 1 and f(1)=0.
Let D_{f}(P||Q)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{Q}[f(\frac{{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}Q})] denote the f-divergence of P and Q with P\ll Q,
cf.  [ Csi67 ] .  2    2 
               2 
              
              
              
            More generally, D_{f}(P||Q)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[f\left(\frac{{\rm d}P/{\rm d}\mu}{{\rm d}Q/{\rm d}\mu}\right)\right], where \mu is a dominating probability measure of P and Q, e.g., \mu=(P+Q)/2, with the understanding that f(0)=f(0+), 0f(\frac{0}{0})=0 and 0f(\frac{a}{0})=\lim_{x\downarrow 0}xf(\frac{a}{x}) for a>0.    For example, the total variation (  1  ) and the KL divergence (  2  ) correspond to f(x)=|x-1| and f(x)=x\log x respectively;
taking f(x)=(x-1)^{2} we obtain the \chi^{2}-divergence: \chi^{2}(P\|Q)\triangleq\int({{\rm d}P\over{\rm d}Q})^{2}{\rm d}Q-1. 
 
 
 For any Q that is not a point mass, define: 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{f}(P_{Y|X},Q) 
 \displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{P:0<D_{f}(P\|Q)<\infty}{D_{f}(P_{Y|X}\circ P\|P_{Y|X}\circ Q)\over D_{f}(P\|Q)}, 
  
  (5)    
  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{f}(P_{Y|X}) 
 \displaystyle\triangleq\sup_{Q}\eta_{f}(Q)\,. 
  
  (6)    
 
 It is easy to show that the supremum is over a non-empty set whenever Q is not a point mass (see Appendix   B  ).
For notational simplicity when the channel is clear from context we abbreviate \eta_{f}(P_{Y|X}) as \eta_{f}.
For contraction coefficients of total variation, \chi^{2} and KL divergence, we write
\eta_{\rm TV},\eta_{\chi^{2}} and \eta_{\rm KL}, respectively, which play prominent roles in this exposition. 
 
 
 One of the main tools for studying ergodicity property of Markov chains as well as Gibbs measures, \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X}) is known as the  Dobrushin’s coefficient  of the kernel P_{Y|X}.
Dobrushin  [ Dob56 ]  showed that the supremum in the definition of \eta_{\rm TV} can be
restricted to point masses, namely, 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X})=\sup_{x,x^{\prime}}d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X=x},P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}), 
  
  (7)    
 
 thus providing a simple criterion for strong ergodicity of Markov processes.
Later  [ CKZ98 , Proposition II.4.10(i)]  (see also  [ CIR+93 , Theorem 4.1]  for finite alphabets)
demonstrated that all other contraction coefficients
are upper bounded by the Dobrushin’s coefficient, with inequality being typically strict (cf. the BSC example below): 
 
 
   Theorem 1     ( [ CKZ98 , Proposition II.4.10] ) .  
 
  For every f-divergence, we have  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{f}(P_{Y|X})\leq\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X}). 
  
  (8)    
 
 
 
 
 For the opposite direction, lower bounds on \eta_{f} typically involves \eta_{\chi^{2}}, the contraction coefficient of the \chi^{2}-divergence.
It is well-known, e.g. Sarmanov  [ Sar58 ] , that \eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X},P_{X}) is the squared  maximal correlation 
coefficient of the joint distribution P_{XY}: 
 

  
  
 S(X;Y)\triangleq\sup_{f,g}\rho(f(X),g(Y))=\sqrt{\eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})}\,, 
  
  (9)    
 
 where \rho(\cdot,\cdot) denotes the correlation coefficient and
the supremum is over real-valued functions f,g such that f(X) and g(Y) are square integrable. 
 
 
 The relationship between \eta_{\rm KL} and \eta_{\chi^{2}} on finite alphabets has been systematically studied by Ahlswede and Gács  [ AG76 ] . In particular,  [ AG76 ]  proved 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})\leq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X},P_{X}), 
  
  (10)    
 
 and noticed that the inequality is frequently strict.  3    3 
               3 
              
              
              
            See  [ AG76 , Theorem 9]  and
 [ AGKN13 ]  for examples.   
Furthermore, for finite alphabets, the following equivalence is demonstrated in  [ AG76 ] : 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{X},P_{Y|X})<1\iff 
 \displaystyle~\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X},P_{Y|X})<1 
  
  (11)    
  
  
 \displaystyle\iff 
 \displaystyle~\text{graph~}\{(x,y):P_{X}(x)>0,P_{Y|X}(y|x)>0\}\mbox{~is connected}. 
  
  (12)    
 
 As a criterion for \eta_{f}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})<1, this is an improvement of (  8  ) only for channels with
\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X})=1.
The lower bound (  10  ) can in fact be considerably generalized: 
 
 
   Theorem 2   .  
 
  Let f be twice continuously differentiable on (0,\infty) with f^{\prime\prime}(1)>0. Then for any P_{X} that is not a point mass,  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})\leq\eta_{f}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})\,, 
  
  (13)    
 
  and  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X})\leq\eta_{f}(P_{Y|X})\,. 
  
  (14)    
 
 
 
 
 See Appendix   B.1   for a proof of (  14  ) for the general case, which yields (  13  ) by taking suprema over P_{X} on both sides.
Note that (  13  ) (resp. (  14  )) have been proved in  [ CKZ98 , Proposition II.6.15]  for the general alphabet (resp. in  [ Rag14 , Theorem 3.3]  for finite alphabets). 
 
 
 Moreover, (  13  ) in fact holds with equality for all
nonlinear and operator convex f, e.g., for KL divergence and for squared Hellinger distance; see  [ CRS94 , Theorem
1]  and  [ CKZ98 , Proposition II.6.13 and Corollary II.6.16] . Therefore, we have: 
 
 
   Theorem 3   .  
 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X})=\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}), 
  
  (15)    
 
 
 
 
 See Appendix   B.1   for a self-contained proof. This result was first obtained in  [ AG76 ]  using different methods for discrete space.
Rather naturally, we also have  [ CKZ98 , Proposition II.4.12] : 
 

  
  
 \eta_{f}(P_{Y|X})=1\quad\iff\quad\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X})=1 
    
 
 for any non-linear f. 
 
 
 As an illustrating example, for \mathsf{BSC}(\delta) defined in (  4  ),
we have cf.  [ AG76 ]  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\chi^{2}}=\eta_{\rm KL}=(1-2\delta)^{2}<\eta_{\rm TV}=|1-2\delta|. 
  
  (16)    
 
 
 
 We also note in passing that SDPIs are intimately related to hypercontractivity and maximal correlation, as
discovered by Ahlswede and Gács  [ AG76 ]  and recently improved by Anantharam et al.  [ AGKN13 ]  and
Nair  [ Nai14 ] . Indeed, the main result of  [ AG76 ]  characterizes \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})
as the maximal ratio of hyper-contractivity of the conditional expectation operator
\mathbb{E}[\cdot|X]. 
 
 
 The fixed-input contraction coefficient \eta_{\rm KL}(Q) is closely related to the (modified) log-Sobolev inequalities. Indeed, if \eta_{\rm KL}(Q)<1 where Q is the invariant measure for the Markov kernel P_{Y|X}, i.e., P_{Y|X}\circ Q=Q, then any initial distribution P such that D(P\|Q)<\infty
converges to Q exponentially fast
since 
 

  
  
 D(P_{Y|X}^{n}\circ P||Q)\leq\eta_{\rm KL}^{n}(P_{Y|X},Q)D(P||Q), 
    
 
 where the exponent \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X},Q) can in turn be
estimated from log-Sobolev inequalities, e.g.  [ Led99 ] . When Q is not invariant,
it was shown  [ DMLM03 ]  that 
 

  
  
 1-\alpha(Q)\leq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X},Q)\leq 1-C\alpha(Q) 
    
 
 holds for some universal constant C, where \alpha(Q) is a modified log-Sobolev (also known as 1-log-Sobolev) constant: 
 

  
  
 \alpha(Q)=\inf_{f\perp 1,\|f\|_{2}=1}{\mathbb{E}\left[f^{2}(X)\log{f^{2}(X)\over f^{2}(X^{\prime})}\right]\over\mathbb{E}[f^{2}(X)\log f^{2}(X)]},\qquad P_{XX^{\prime}}=Q\times(P_{X|Y}\circ P_{Y|X}). 
    
 
 For further connections between \eta_{\rm KL} and log-Sobolev inequalities on finite alphabets see  [ Rag13 ,  Rag14 ] . 
 
 
 There exist several other characterizations of \eta_{\rm KL}, such as the following one in terms of the contraction of mutual information (cf.  [ CK81 , Exercise III.3.12, p. 350]  for finite alphabet): 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})=\sup{I(U;Y)\over I(U;X)}\,, 
  
  (17)    
 
 where the supremum is over all Markov chains U\to X\to Y with fixed P_{Y|X} (or equivalently, over all joint
distributions P_{XU}) such that I(U;X)<\infty. This result is an immediate consequence of the following input-dependent version
(see Appendix   B.3   for a proof in the general case; the finite alphabet case has been shown in  [ AGKN13 ] ) 
 
 
   Theorem 4   .  
 
  For any P_{X} that is not a point mass,  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})=\sup{I(U;Y)\over I(U;X)}\,, 
  
  (18)    
 
  where the supremum is taken over all Markov chains U\to X\to Y with fixed P_{XY}=P_{X}\circ P_{Y|X} such that 0<I(U;X)<\infty.  
 
 
 
 Another characterization of \eta_{\rm KL}, in view of (  15  ) and (  9  ), is 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})=\sup\rho^{2}(f(X),g(Y))\,, 
    
 
 where the supremum is over all P_{X}
and real-valued square-integrable f(X) and g(Y). 
 
 
 
  2.2  Non-linear SDPI 

 
 How to quantify the information loss if \eta_{\rm KL}=1 for the channel of interest? In fact this situation can arise in very basic settings, such as the
additive-noise Gaussian channel under the moment constraint on the input distributions (cf.  [ PW16 , Theorem 9, Section 4.5] ),
where the mutual information does not contract linearly as in (  17  ), but can still contract  non-linearly .
In such cases, establishing a strong-data processing inequality can be done by following the joint-range idea of
Harremoës and Vajda  [ HV11 ] . Namely, we aim to find (or bound) the  best possible
data-processing function  F_{I} defined as follows. 
 
 
   Definition 1     (F_{I}-curve) .  
 
 Fix P_{Y|X} and define 
 

  
  
 F_{I}(t,P_{Y|X})\triangleq\sup_{P_{UX}}\left\{I(U;Y)\colon I(U;X)\leq t,P_{UXY}=P_{UX}P_{Y|X}\right\}. 
  
  (19)    
 
 Equivalently, the supremum is taken over all joint distributions P_{UXY} with a given conditional P_{Y|X} and
satisfying U\rightarrow X\rightarrow Y.
The upper concave envelope of F_{I} is denoted by F_{I}^{c}, or
equivalently, 
 

  
  
 F_{I}^{c}(t,P_{Y|X})\triangleq\sup_{P_{VUX}}\left\{I(U;Y|V)\colon I(U;X|V)\leq t,P_{VUXY}=P_{VUX}P_{Y|X}\right\}. 
  
  (20)    
 
 Whenever it does not lead to confusion we will write F_{Y|X}(t) instead of F_{I}(t,P_{Y|X}). 
 
 
 
 The operational significance of the F_{I}-curve is that it gives the optimal input-independent strong data processing inequality: 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)\leq F_{I}(I(U;X)), 
    
 
 which generalizes (  17  ) since F_{I}^{\prime}(0)=\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}) and t\mapsto\frac{1}{t}F_{I}(t) is decreasing (see, e.g.,  [ CPW15 , Section I] ).
See  [ CPW15 ]  for bounds and expressions for BSC and Gaussian channels. 
 
 
 Frequently it is more convenient to work with the concavified version F_{I}^{c} as it allows for some natural extension of the results about
contraction coefficients. Proposition   17   shows that F_{I} may not be concave. 
 
 
 
  2.3  Some applications: classical and new 

 
 The main example of a strong data-processing inequality (SDPI) was discovered by Ahlswede and Gács  [ AG76 ] . They have shown, using the characterization (  11  ),
that whenever P_{Y|X} is a discrete memoryless channel that does not admit zero-error communication, we have \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})\leq\eta<1 and 
 

  
  
 I(W;Y)\leq\eta I(W;X) 
  
  (21)    
 
 for all Markov chains W\to X\to Y. 
 
 
 Classically and more recently SDPIs have been increasingly popular for establishing lower (impossibility) bounds in
various setups. We mention only a few of these applications: 
 
  •  
 
 By Dobrushin for showing non-existence of multiple phases in Ising models at high temperatures  [ Dob70 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Erkip and Cover in portfolio theory  [ EC98 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Evans and Schulman in analysis of noise-resistant circuits  [ ES99 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Evans, Kenyon, Peres and Schulman in the analysis of inference on trees and percolation  [ EKPS00 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Courtade in distributed data-compression  [ Cou12 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Duchi, Wainwright and Jordan in statistical limitations of differential privacy  [ DJW13 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By the authors to quantify optimal communication and optimal control in line networks  [ PW16 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Liu, Cuff and Verdú in key generation  [ LCV15 ] ; 
  
  •  
 
 By Xu and Raginsky in distributed estimation  [ XR15 ] . 
  
 
 
 
 All of the applications above use SDPI (  21  ) to prove negative (impossibility) statements. A notable exception
is the work of Boyen and Koller  [ BK98 ] , who considered the basic problem of computing the posterior-belief
vector of a hidden Markov model: that is, given a Markov chain \{X_{j}\} observed over a memoryless channel P_{Y|X},
one aims to recompute P_{X_{j}|Y^{j}_{-\infty}} as each new observation Y_{j} arrives. The problem arises when X is of large
dimension and then for practicality one is constrained to approximate (quantize) the posterior. However, due to
the recursive nature of belief computations, the cumulative effect of these approximations may become overwhelming.
Boyen and Koller  [ BK98 ]  proposed to use the SDPI similar to (  21  ) with \eta<1 for the Markov chain
\{X_{j}\} and show that this cumulative
effect stays bounded since \sum\eta^{n}<\infty. Similar considerations also enable one to provide provable
guarantees for simulation of inter-dependent stochastic processes. 
 
 
 
 
  3  Contraction of mutual information in networks 

 
 We start by defining a  Bayesian network  (also known as a  directed graphical model ).
Let G be a finite directed acyclic graph with set of vertices \{Y_{v}:v\in{\mathcal{V}}\} denoting random
variables taking values in a fixed finite alphabet.  4    4 
             4 
            
            
            
          At the expense of technical details, the alphabet can be
replaced with any countably-generated (e.g. Polish) measurable space. For clarity of presentation we focus here on finite alphabets.   
We assume that each
vertex Y_{v} is associated with a conditional distribution P_{Y_{v}|Y_{\mathrm{pa}(v)}} where \mathrm{pa}(v) denotes parents of v, with the
exception of one special “source” node X that has no inbound edges (there may be other nodes without inbound edges,
but those have to have their marginals specified). Notice that if V\subset{\mathcal{V}} is an arbitrary set of nodes we can
progressively chain together all the random transformations and compute unequivocally P_{V|X} (here and below we use V and Y_{V}=\{Y_{v}:v\in V\} interchangeably).
We assume that vertices in {\mathcal{V}} are topologically sorted so that v_{1}>v_{2} implies there is no path from v_{1} to
v_{2}. Associated to each node we also define 
 

  
  
 \eta_{v}\triangleq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y_{v}|Y_{\mathrm{pa}(v)}})\,. 
    
 
 See the excellent book of Lauritzen  [ Lau96 ]  for a thorough introduction to a graphical model language of specifying
conditional independencies. 
 
 
 The following result can be distilled from  [ ES99 ] : 
 
 
   Theorem 5   .  
 
  Let W\in{\mathcal{V}} and V\subset{\mathcal{V}} such that W>V.
Then  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V,W|X})\leq\eta_{W}\cdot\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V,\mathrm{pa}(W)|X})+(1-\eta_{W})\cdot\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X})\,. 
  
  (22)    
 
  In particular, if \eta_{v}<1 for all v\in{\mathcal{V}} then \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X})<1.  
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Consider an arbitrary random variable U such that  
 

  
  
 U\to X\to(V,W)\,. 
    
 
  Let A=\mathrm{pa}(W)\setminus V. Without loss of generality we may assume A does not contain X: indeed, if A
includes X then we can introduce an artificial node X^{\prime} such that X^{\prime}=X and include X^{\prime} into A instead of X.
Relevant conditional independencies are encoded in the following graph:  
 

  
  
           {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces U}        {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}        {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces V}          {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}        {\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces W}                                                           
    
 
 
 
  From the
characterization (  17  ) it is sufficient to show  
 

  
  
 I(U;V,W)\leq(1-\eta_{W})I(U;V)+\eta_{W}I(U;V,A)\,. 
  
  (23)    
 
  Denote B=V\backslash\mathrm{pa}(W) and C=V\cap\mathrm{pa}(W). Then \mathrm{pa}(W)=(A,C) and V=(B,C).
To verify (  23  ) notice that by assumption we have  
 

  
  
 U\to X\to(V,A)\to W\,. 
    
 
  Therefore conditioned on V we have the Markov chain  
 

  
  
 U\to X\to A\to W\qquad|V\, 
    
 
  and the channel A\to W is a restriction of the original P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)} to a subset of the inputs.

Indeed, P_{W|A,V}=P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W),B}=P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)} by assumption of the graphical model. Thus, for every
realization v=(b,c) of V, we have P_{W|A=a,V=v}=P_{W|A=a,C=c} and therefore  
 

  
  
 I(U;W|V=v)\leq\eta(P_{W|A,C=c})I(U;A|V=v)\leq\eta(P_{W|A,C})I(U;A|V=v), 
  
  (24)    
 
  where the last inequality uses the following property of the contraction coefficient which easily follows from either (  6  ) or (  17  ):  
 

  
  
 \sup_{c}\eta(P_{W|A,C=c})\leq\eta(P_{W|A,C}). 
  
  (25)    
 
  Averaging both sides of (  24  ) over v\sim P_{V} and using the definition \eta_{W}=\eta(P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)})=\eta(P_{W|A,C}), we have  
 

  
  
 I(U;W|V)\leq\eta_{W}I(U;A|V)\,. 
  
  (26)    
 
  Adding I(U;V) to both sides yields (  23  ).
∎  
 
 
 
 Theorem   5   allows us to estimate contraction coefficients in arbitrary (finite) networks by peeling off last nodes
one by one. Next we derive a few corollaries: 
 
 
   Corollary 6   .  
 
  Consider a fixed (single-letter) channel P_{Y|X} and assume that it is used repeatedly and with perfect feedback
to send information from W to (Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}).
That is, we have for some encoder functions f_{j}  
 

  
  
 P_{Y^{n}|W}(y^{n}|w)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}P_{Y|X}(y_{j}|f_{j}(w,y^{j-1})), 
    
 
  which corresponds to the graphical model:  
 

  
  
        \textstyle{W\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}                                    \textstyle{Y_{1}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}                            \textstyle{Y_{2}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}                    \textstyle{Y_{3}\cdots}        
    
 
  Then  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y^{n}|W})\leq 1-(1-\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}))^{n}<n\cdot\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}) 
    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Apply Theorem   5   n times. ∎  
 
 
 
 Let us call a path \pi=\{X,\cdots,v\} with v\in V to be  shortcut-free from X to V , denoted
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V, if there does not
exist another path \pi^{\prime} from X to any node in V such that \pi^{\prime} is a subset of \pi. (In particular v
necessarily is the first node in V that \pi visits.) Also for every path
\pi=(X,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}) we define 
 

  
  
 \eta^{\pi}\triangleq\prod_{j=1}^{m}\eta_{v_{j}}\,. 
    
 
 
 
   Corollary 7   .  
 
  For any subset V we have  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X})\leq\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V}\eta^{\pi}\,. 
  
  (27)    
 
  In particular, we have the estimate of Evans-Schulman  [ ES99 ] :  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X})\leq\sum_{\pi:X\to V}\eta^{\pi}\,. 
  
  (28)    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  First notice the following two self-evident observations:  
 
  1.  
 
 If A and B are disjoint sets of nodes, then 
 

  
  
 \sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}A\cup B}\eta^{\pi}=\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}A,\mbox{avoid $B$}}\eta^{\pi}+\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}B,\mbox{avoid $A$}}\eta^{\pi}. 
  
  (29)    
 
  
  2.  
 
 Let \pi:X\to V and \pi_{1} be \pi without the last node, then 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V\quad\iff\quad\pi_{1}:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}\{\mathrm{pa}(V)\setminus V\}. 
  
  (30)    
 
  
 
 
 
  Now represent V=(V^{\prime},W) with W>V^{\prime}, denote P=\mathrm{pa}(W)\setminus V and assume (by induction) that  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V^{\prime}|X}) 
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (31)    
  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V^{\prime},P|X}) 
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}\{V^{\prime},P\}}\eta^{\pi}\,. 
  
  (32)    
 
  By (  29  ) and (  30  ) we have  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V}\eta^{\pi} 
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime}}\eta^{\pi}+\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}W,\mbox{avoid $V^{\prime}$}}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (33)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime}}\eta^{\pi}+\eta_{W}\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}P,\mbox{avoid $V^{\prime}$}}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (34)    
 
  Then by Theorem   5   and induction hypotheses (  31  )-(  32  ) we get  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X}) 
 \displaystyle\leq\eta_{W}\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}\{V^{\prime},P\}}\eta^{\pi}+(1-\eta_{W})\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime}}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (35)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=\eta_{W}\left(\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}P,\mbox{avoid $V^{\prime}$}}\eta^{\pi}-\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime},\mbox{pass $P$}}\eta^{\pi}\right)+\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime}}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (36)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq\eta_{W}\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}P,\mbox{avoid $V^{\prime}$}}\eta^{\pi}+\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime}}\eta^{\pi} 
  
  (37)    
 
  where in (  36  ) we applied (  29  ) and split the summation over \pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V^{\prime} into
paths that avoid and pass nodes in P. Comparing (  34  )
and (  37  ) the conclusion follows.
∎

  3.1  Evaluation for the BSC 

 
 We consider the contraction coefficient for the n-letter binary symmetric channel \mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n} defined in (  4  ).
By (  16  ), for n=1 we have \eta_{\rm KL}=(1-2\delta)^{2}. Then by Corollary   6   we have
for arbitrary n: 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}\leq 1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{n}\,. 
  
  (38)    
 
 
 
 A simple lower bound for \eta_{\rm KL} can be obtained by considering (  17  ) and taking U\sim\text{Bern}(1/2) and
U\to X being an n-letter repetition code, namely, X=(U,\ldots,U). Let  5    5 
               5 
              
              
              
            For elements of \mathbb{F}_{2}^{n} |\cdot| is the Hamming
weight.   
\epsilon=\mathbb{P}[|Z|\geq n/2] be the probability of error for the maximal likelihood decoding of U based on Y, which satisfies the Chernoff bound \epsilon\leq(4\delta(1-\delta))^{n/2}.
We have from Jensen’s inequality 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)=H(U)-H(U|Y)\geq 1-h(\epsilon)=1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,, 
    
 
 and thus 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}\geq 1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,. 
  
  (39)    
 
 Comparing (  38  ) and (  39  ) we see that \eta_{\rm KL}\to 1 exponentially fast. To get the exact
exponent we need to replace (  38  ) by the following improvement: 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}\leq\eta_{\rm TV}\leq 1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,, 
    
 
 where the first inequality is from (  8  ) and the second is from (  46  ) below. Thus, all in all we
have for \mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n} as n\to\infty 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL},\eta_{\rm TV}=1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,. 
  
  (40)    
 
 
 
 
 
  4  Dobrushin’s coefficients in networks 

 
 The proof of Theorem   5   relies on the characterization (  17  ) of \eta_{\rm KL} via mutual
information, which satisfies the chain rule. Neither of these two properties is enjoyed by the total variation.
Nevertheless, the following is an exact counterpart of Theorem   5   for total variation. 
 
 
   Theorem 8   .  
 
  Under the same assumption of Theorem   5  ,  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{V,W|X})\leq(1-\eta_{W})\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{V|X})+\eta_{W}\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X})\,, 
  
  (41)    
 
  where \eta_{W}=\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)}). In particular, if \eta_{v}<1 for all v\in V, then \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{V|X})<1.  
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Fix x,{\tilde{x}} and denote by P (resp. Q) the distribution conditioned on X=x (resp. x^{\prime}). Denote Z=\mathrm{pa}(W).
The goal is to show  
 

  
  
 d_{\rm TV}(P_{VW},Q_{VW})\leq(1-\eta_{W})d_{\rm TV}(P_{V},Q_{V})+\eta_{W}d_{\rm TV}(P_{ZV},Q_{ZV})\,. 
  
  (42)    
 
  which, by the arbitrariness of x,x^{\prime} and in view of the characterization of \eta in (  7  ), yields the desired (  41  ).
By Lemma   20   in Appendix   A  , there exists a coupling of P_{ZV} and Q_{ZV}, denoted by
\pi_{ZVZ^{\prime}V^{\prime}}, such that  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\pi[(Z,V)\neq(Z^{\prime},V^{\prime})] 
 \displaystyle=d_{\rm TV}(P_{ZV},Q_{ZV})\,, 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\pi[V\neq V^{\prime}] 
 \displaystyle=d_{\rm TV}(P_{V},Q_{V}) 
    
 
  simultaneously (that is, this coupling is jointly optimal for the total variation of the joint distributions and one pair of
marginals).  
 
 
  Conditioned on Z=z and Z^{\prime}=z^{\prime} and independently of VV^{\prime}, let WW^{\prime} be distributed according to a maximal coupling of the conditional laws P_{W|Z=z} and P_{W|Z=z^{\prime}} (recall that Q_{W|Z}=P_{W|Z}=P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)} by definition).
This defines a joint distribution \pi_{ZVWZ^{\prime}V^{\prime}W^{\prime}}, under which we have the Markov chain VV^{\prime}\to ZZ^{\prime}\to WW^{\prime}.
Then  
 

  
  
 \pi[W\neq W^{\prime}|ZVZ^{\prime}V^{\prime}]=\pi[W\neq W^{\prime}|ZZ^{\prime}]=d_{\rm TV}(P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)=Z},P_{W|\mathrm{pa}(W)=Z^{\prime}})\leq\eta_{W}{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{Z\neq Z^{\prime}}\right\}}}. 
    
 
  Therefore we have  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\pi[W\neq W^{\prime}|V=V^{\prime}]= 
 \displaystyle~\mathbb{E}[\pi[W\neq W^{\prime}|ZZ^{\prime}]|V=V^{\prime}] 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~\eta_{W}\pi[Z\neq Z^{\prime}|V=V^{\prime}]. 
    
 
  Multiplying both sides by \pi[V=V^{\prime}] and then adding \pi[V\neq V^{\prime}], we obtain  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\pi[(W,V)\neq(W^{\prime},V^{\prime})]\leq 
 \displaystyle~(1-\eta_{W})\pi[V\neq V^{\prime}]+\eta_{W}\pi[(Z,V)\neq(Z^{\prime},V^{\prime})] 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~(1-\eta_{W})d_{\rm TV}(P_{V},Q_{V})+\eta_{W}d_{\rm TV}(P_{ZV},Q_{ZV}), 
    
 
  where the LHS is lower bounded by d_{\rm TV}(P_{WV},Q_{WV}) and the equality is due to the choice of \pi. This yields the desired (  42  ), completing the proof.
∎  
 
 
 
 As a consequence of Theorem   8  , both Corollary   6   and   28   extend to total
variation verbatim with \eta_{\rm KL} replaced by \eta_{\rm TV}: 
 
 
   Corollary 9   .  
 
  In the setting of Corollary   6   we have  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y^{n}|W})\leq 1-(1-\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X}))^{n}<n\cdot\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})\,. 
  
  (43)    
 
 
 
 
   Corollary 10   .  
 
  In the setting of Corollary   28   we have  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm TV}(P_{V|X})\leq\sum_{\pi:X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle sf}}{{\to}}V}\eta_{\rm TV}^{\pi}\leq\sum_{\pi:X\to V}\eta_{\rm TV}^{\pi}\,, 
    
 
  where for any path \pi=(X,v_{1},\ldots,v_{m}) we denoted \eta_{\rm TV}^{\pi}\triangleq\prod\limits_{j=1}^{m}\eta_{\rm TV}(P_{v_{j}|\mathrm{pa}(v_{j})}).  
 
 
 
  4.1  Evaluation for the BSC 

 
 Consider the n-letter BSC defined in (  4  ), where Y=X+Z with Z\sim\text{Bern}(\delta)^{n} and |Z|\sim\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta).
By Dobrushin’s characterization (  7  ), we have 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\eta_{\rm TV} 
 \displaystyle=\max_{x,x^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}}d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y|X=x},P_{Y|X=x^{\prime}}) 
    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=d_{\rm TV}(\mathrm{Bern}(\delta)^{n},\mathrm{Bern}(1-\delta)^{n}) 
    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=d_{\rm TV}(\mathrm{Binom}(n,\delta),\mathrm{Binom}(n,1-\delta)) 
  
  (44)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=1-2\mathbb{P}[|Z|>n/2]-\mathbb{P}[|Z|=n/2] 
  
  (45)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle=1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,, 
  
  (46)    
 
 where (  44  ) follows from the sufficiency of |Z| for testing the two distributions, (  45  ) follows from d_{\rm TV}(P,Q)=1-\int P\wedge Q and (  46  ) follows from standard binomial tail estimates (see, e.g.,  [ Ash65 , Lemma 4.7.2] ).
The above sharp estimate should be compared to the bound obtained by applying Corollary   9  : 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm TV}\leq 1-(2\delta)^{n}\,. 
  
  (47)    
 
 Although (  47  ) correctly predicts the exponential convergence of \eta_{\rm TV}\to 1 whenever \delta<\frac{1}{2}, the exponent estimated is not
optimal. 
 
 
 
 
  5  Bounding F_{I}-curves in networks 

 
 In this section our goal is to produce upper bound bounds on the F_{I}-curve of a Bayesian network F_{V|X}
in terms of those of the constituent channels. For any vertex v of the network, denote the F_{I}-curve of the channel P_{v|\mathrm{pa}(v)} by F_{v|\mathrm{pa}(v)}, abbreviated by F_{v}, and the concavified version by F_{v}^{c}. 
 
 
   Theorem 11   .  
 
  In the setting of Theorem   5  ,  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle F_{V,W|X}\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{V|X}+F_{W}^{c}\circ(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}-F_{V|X})\,, 
  
  (48)    
  
  
 \displaystyle F_{V,W|X}^{c}\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{V|X}^{c}+F_{W}^{c}\circ(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}^{c}-F_{V|X}^{c})\,. 
  
  (49)    
 
  Furthermore, the right-hand side of (  49  ) is non-negative, concave,
nondecreasing and upper bounded by the identity mapping {\rm id}.  
 
 
 
   Remark 1   .  
 
 The F_{I}-curve estimate in Theorem   11   implies that of contraction coefficients of Theorem   5  . To see this, note that since F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}\leq{\rm id}, the following is a relaxation of (  48  ): 
 

  
  
 {\rm id}-F_{V,W|X}\geq({\rm id}-F_{W})\circ({\rm id}-F_{V|X}). 
  
  (50)    
 
 Consequently, if each channel in the network satisfies an SDPI, then the end-to-end SDPI is also satisfied. That is, if each
vertex has a non-trivial F_{I}-curve, i.e., F_{v}<{\rm id} for all v\in{\mathcal{V}},
then the channel X\to V also has a strict contractive property, i.e., F_{V|X}<{\rm id}. 
 
 
 Furthermore, since F_{W}^{c}(t)\leq\eta_{W}t, noting the fact that F_{V|X}^{\prime}(0)=\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{V|X}) and taking the derivative
on both sides of (  48  ) we see that the latter implies (  22  ). 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  We first show that for any channel P_{Y|X}, its F_{Y|X}-curve satisfies that t\mapsto t-F_{Y|X}(t) is nondecreasing. Indeed, it is known,
cf.  [ CPW15 , Section I] , that t\mapsto{F_{Y|X}(t)\over t} is nonincreasing. Thus, for t_{1}<t_{2} we have  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle t_{2}-F_{Y|X}(t_{2}) 
 \displaystyle\geq t_{2}-{t_{2}\over t_{1}}F_{Y|X}(t_{1}) 
    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle={t_{2}\over t_{1}}\left(t_{1}-F_{Y|X}(t_{1})\right) 
    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle\geq t_{1}-F_{Y|X}(t_{1})\,, 
    
 
  where the last step follows from the fact that F_{Y|X}(t)\leq t. Similarly, for any concave function
\Phi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+} s.t. \Phi(0)=0 we have {\Phi(t_{2})\over t_{2}}\leq{\Phi(t_{1})\over t_{1}}. Therefore, the argument above implies t\mapsto t-\Phi(t) is nondecreasing and, in particular, so is t\mapsto t-F_{W}^{c}(t).  
 
 
  Let P_{UX} be such that I(U;V)\leq t and I(U;W,V)=F_{V,W|X}(t).
By the same argument that leads to (  26  ) we obtain  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle I(U;W|V=v_{0}) 
 \displaystyle\leq F_{W}(I(U;A|V=v_{0})) 
    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq F_{W}^{c}(I(U;A|V=v_{0}))\,. 
    
 
  Averaging over v_{0}\sim P_{V} and applying Jensen’s inequality we get  
 

  
  
 I(U;W,V)\leq F_{W}^{c}(I(U;\mathrm{pa}(W),V)-I(U;V))+I(U;V). 
    
 
  Therefore,  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle F_{V,W|X}(t)\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{W}^{c}(I(U;\mathrm{pa}(W),V)-I(U;V))+I(U;V) 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{W}^{c}(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-I(U;V))+I(U;V) 
  
  (51)    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-({\rm id}-F_{W}^{c})(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-I(U;V)) 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-({\rm id}-F_{W}^{c})(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-F_{V|X}(t)) 
  
  (52)    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{V|X}(t)+F_{W}^{c}(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}(t)-F_{V|X}(t)) 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~F_{V|X}^{c}(t)+F_{W}^{c}(F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}^{c}(t)-F_{V|X}^{c}(t)) 
  
  (53)    
 
  where
(  51  ) and (  52  ) follow from the facts that t\mapsto F_{W}(t) and t\mapsto t-F_{W}(t) are both nondecreasing, and
(  53  ) follows from that a+F_{W}^{c}(b-a) is nondecreasing in both a and b.  
 
 
  Finally, we need to show that the right-hand side of (  53  ) is nondecreasing and concave (this automatically
implies that (  53  ) is an upper-bound to the concavification F_{V|X}^{c}). To that end, denote t_{\lambda}=\lambda t_{1}+(1-\lambda)t_{0}, f_{\lambda}=F_{V|X}^{c}(t_{\lambda}), g_{\lambda}=F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}^{c}(t_{\lambda}) and notice the
chain  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle f_{\lambda}+F_{W}^{c}(g_{\lambda}-f_{\lambda}) 
 \displaystyle\geq\lambda f_{1}+(1-\lambda)f_{0}+F_{W}^{c}(\lambda(g_{1}-f_{1})+(1-\lambda)(g_{0}-f_{0})) 
  
  (54)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle\geq\lambda(f_{1}+F_{W}^{c}(g_{1}-f_{1}))+(1-\lambda)(f_{0}+F_{W}^{c}(g_{0}-f_{0})) 
  
  (55)    
 
  where (  54  ) is from concavity of F_{V|X}^{c}, F_{\mathrm{pa}(W),V|X}^{c} and monotonicity of (a,b)\mapsto a+F_{W}^{c}(b-a), and (  55  ) is from concavity of F_{W}^{c}.
∎  
 
 
 
   Corollary 12   .  
 
  In the setting of Corollary   6   we have  
 

  
  
 F_{Y^{n}|W}(t)\leq t-\psi^{(n)}(t)\,, 
    
 
  where \psi^{(1)}=\psi, \psi^{(k+1)}=\psi^{(k)}\circ\psi and \psi:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+} is a
 convex  function such that  
 

  
  
 F_{Y|X}(t)\leq t-\psi(t)\,. 
    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  The case of n=1 follows from the assumption on \psi. The case of n>1 is proved by induction, with the induction step being an
application of Theorem   11   with V=Y^{n-1} and W=Y_{n}.
∎  
 
 
 
 Generally, the bound of Corollary   12   cannot be improved in the vicinity of zero. As an example where this is
tight, consider a parallel erasure channel, whose F_{I}-curve for t\leq\log q is computed in Theorem   16  
below. 
 
 
  5.1  Evaluation for the BSC 

 
 To ease the notation, all logarithms are with respect to base two in this section.
Let h(y)=y\log\frac{1}{y}+(1-y)\log\frac{1}{1-y} denote the binary entropy function and h^{-1}:[0,1]\to[0,{1\over 2}] its functional inverse.
Let p*q\triangleq p(1-q)+q(1-p) for p,q\in[0,1] denote binary convolution and define 
 

  
  
 \psi(t)\triangleq t-1+h(\delta*h^{-1}(\max(1-t,0))) 
  
  (56)    
 
 which is convex and increasing in t on \mathbb{R}_{+}.
For n=1 it was shown in  [ CPW15 , Section 2]  that the F_{I}-curve of \mathsf{BSC}(\delta) is given
by 
 

  
  
 F_{I}(t,\mathsf{BSC}(\delta))=F_{I}^{c}(t,\mathsf{BSC}(\delta))=t-\psi(t)\,. 
    
 
 
 
 Applying Corollary   12   we obtain the following bound on the F_{I}-curve of BSC of blocklength n (even with feedback): 
 
 
   Proposition 13   .  
 
  Let Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{n}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d. }}}{{\sim}}}\text{Bern}(\delta) be independent of
U. For any (encoder) functions f_{j},j=1,\ldots,n, define  
 

  
  
 X_{j}=f_{j}(U,Y^{j-1}),\quad Y_{j}=X_{j}+Z_{j}\,. 
    
 
  Then  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y^{n})\leq I(U;X^{n})-\psi^{(n)}(I(U;X^{n}))\,, 
  
  (57)    
 
  where \psi^{(1)}=\psi, \psi^{(k+1)}=\psi^{(k)}\circ\psi and \psi is defined in (  56  ).  
 
 
 
   Remark 2   .  
 
 The estimate (  57  ) was first shown by A. Samorodnitsky (private
communication) under extra technical constraints on the joint distribution of (X^{n},W) and in the absence of feedback.
We have then observed that
Evans-Schulman type of technique yields (  57  ) generally even with feedback. 
 
 
 
 Since \psi(t)=4\delta(1-\delta)t+o(t) as t\to 0 we get 
 

  
  
 F_{I}^{c}(t,\mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n})\leq t-t(4\delta(1-\delta))^{n+o(n)} 
    
 
 as n\to\infty for any fixed t. A simple lower bound, for comparison purposes, can be inferred from (  39  )
after noticing that there we have I(U;X)=1, and so 
 

  
  
 F_{I}^{c}(1,\mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n})\geq 1-(4\delta(1-\delta))^{{n\over 2}+O(\log n)}\,, 
    
 
 This shows that the bound of Proposition   13   is order-optimal: F(t)\to t exponentially fast. Exact
exponent is given by (  40  ). 
 
 
 As another point of comparison, we note the following.
Existence of capacity-achieving error-correcting codes then easily implies 
 

  
  
 \lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over n}F_{I}^{c}(n\theta,\mathsf{BSC}(\delta)^{n})=\min(\theta,C)\,, 
    
 
 where C=1-h(\delta) is the Shannon capacity of \mathsf{BSC}(\delta). Since for t>1 we have \psi(t)=t-C one can show that 
 

  
  
 \lim_{n\to\infty}{1\over n}\psi^{(n)}(n\theta)=\left|\theta-C\right|^{+}\,, 
    
 
 and therefore we conclude that in this sense the bound (  57  ) is asymptotically tight. 
 
 
 
 
  6  SDPI via comparison to erasure channels 

 
 So far our leading example has been the binary symmetric channel (  4  ). We now consider another important
example: 
 
 
   Example 1   .  
 
 For any set {\mathcal{X}}, the  erasure channel  on {\mathcal{X}} with erasure probability \delta is a random transformation from {\mathcal{X}} to {\mathcal{X}}\cup\{?\}, where ?\notin{\mathcal{X}} defined as 
 

  
  
 P_{E|X}(e|x)=\begin{cases}\delta,&e=?\\
1-\delta,&e=x\end{cases}\,. 
    
 
 For {\mathcal{X}}=[q], we call it the q -ary erasure channel  denoted by \mathsf{EC}_{q}(\delta).
In the binary, we denote the binary erasure channel by \mathsf{BEC}(\delta)\triangleq\mathsf{EC}_{2}(\delta).
A simple calculation shows that for every P_{UX} we have 
 

  
  
 I(U;E)=(1-\delta)I(U;X) 
  
  (58)    
 
 and therefore for \mathsf{EC}_{q}(\delta) we have \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{E|X})=1-\delta and F_{I}(t)=\min((1-\delta)t,\log q). 
 
 
 
 Next we recall a standard information-theoretic ordering on channels, cf.  [ EGK11 , Section 5.6] : 
 
 
   Definition 2   .  
 
 Given two channels with common input alphabet, P_{Y|X} and P_{Y^{\prime}|X}, we say that P_{Y^{\prime}|X} is less noisy than P_{Y|X}, denoted by P_{Y|X}\leq_{l.n.}P_{Y^{\prime}|X} if for all joint distributions
P_{UX} we have 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)\leq I(U;Y^{\prime})\,. 
    
 
 
 
 
 The following result shows that contraction coefficient of KL divergence can be equivalently formulated as being less-noisy than the corresponding erasure channel: 
 
 
   Proposition 14   .  
 
  For an arbitrary channel P_{Y|X} we have  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})\leq\eta\quad\iff\quad P_{Y|X}\leq_{l.n.}P_{E|X}\,, 
  
  (59)    
 
  where P_{E|X} is the erasure channel on the same input alphabet and erasure probability 1-\eta.  
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  The definition of \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}) guarantees for every P_{UX}  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)\leq(1-\delta)I(U;X), 
  
  (60)    
 
  where the right-hand side is precisely I(U;E) by (  58  ).
∎  
 
 
 
 It turns out the notion of less-noisiness tensorizes: 
 
 
   Proposition 15   .  
 
  If P_{Y_{1}|X_{1}}\leq_{l.n.}P_{Y^{\prime}_{1}|X_{1}} and P_{Y_{2}|X_{2}}\leq_{l.n.}P_{Y^{\prime}_{2}|X_{2}} then  
 

  
  
 P_{Y_{1}|X_{1}}\times P_{Y_{2}|X_{2}}\leq_{l.n.}P_{Y^{\prime}_{1}|X_{1}}\times P_{Y^{\prime}_{2}|X_{2}} 
    
 
  In particular,  
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})\leq\eta\quad\implies\quad P_{Y|X}^{n}\leq_{l.n.}P_{E|X}^{n}\,. 
  
  (61)    
 
  where P_{E|X} is the erasure channel on the same input alphabet and erasure probability 1-\eta.  
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Construct a relevant joint distribution U\to X^{2}\to(Y^{2},Y^{\prime 2}) and consider  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y_{1},Y_{2})=I(U;Y_{1})+I(U;Y_{2}|Y_{1})\,. 
  
  (62)    
 
  Now since U\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Y_{2}|Y_{2} we have by P_{Y_{2}|X_{2}}\leq_{l.n.}P_{Y^{\prime}_{2}|X_{2}}  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y_{2}|Y_{1})\leq I(U;Y_{2}^{\prime}|Y_{1}) 
    
 
  and putting this back into (  62  ) we get  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y_{1},Y_{2})\leq I(U;Y_{1})+I(U;Y_{2}^{\prime}|Y_{1})=I(U;Y_{1},Y_{2}^{\prime})\,. 
    
 
  Repeating the same argument, but conditioning on Y_{2}^{\prime} we get  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y_{1},Y_{2})\leq I(U;Y_{1}^{\prime},Y_{2}^{\prime})\,, 
    
 
  as required. The last claim of the proposition follows from Proposition   14  .
∎  
 
 
 
 Consequently, everything that has been said in this paper about \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}) can be restated in terms of seeking to
compare a given channel in the sense of the \leq_{l.n.} order to an erasure channel. It seems natural, then, to
consider erasure channel in somewhat greater details. 
 
 
  6.1  F_{I}-curve of erasure channels 

 
   Theorem 16   .  
 
  Consider the q-ary erasure channel of blocklength n and erasure probability \delta. Its F_{I}-curve is bounded by  
 

  
  
 F_{I}^{c}(t,\mathsf{EC}_{q}(\delta)^{n})\leq\mathbb{E}[\min(B\log q,t)],\qquad B\sim\mathrm{Binom}(n,1-\delta)\,. 
  
  (63)    
 
  The bound is tight in the following cases:  
 
  1.  
 
  at  t=k\log q  with integral  k\leq n  if and only if an  (n,k,n-k+1)_{q}  MDS code exists   6    6 
                       6 
                      
                      
                      
                    We remind that a
subset \mathcal{C} of [q]^{n} is called an (n,k,d)_{q} code if |\mathcal{C}|=q^{k} and Hamming distance between any two points
from \mathcal{C} is at least d. A code is called maximum-distance separable (MDS) if d=n-k+1. This is equivalent to
the property that projection of \mathcal{C} onto any subset of k coordinates is bijective.    
  
  2.  
 
  for  t\leq\log q  and  t\geq(n-1)\log q ;  
  
  3.  
 
  for all  t  when  n=1,2,3 .  
  
 
 
 
 
   Remark 3   .  
 
 Introducing B^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Binom}(n-1,1-\delta) and using the identity \mathbb{E}[B{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{B\leq a}\right\}}}]=n(1-\delta)\mathbb{P}[B^{\prime}\leq a-1], we can express the right-hand side of (  63  ) in terms of binomial CDFs: 
 

  
  
 \mathbb{E}[\min(B,x)]=x+\mathbb{P}[B^{\prime}\leq\lfloor x\rfloor-1](1-\delta)(n-x)-x\delta\mathbb{P}[B^{\prime}\leq\lfloor x\rfloor] 
    
 
 This implies that the upper bound (  63  ) is piecewise-linear, increasing and concave. 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Consider arbitrary U\to X^{n}\to E^{n} with P_{E^{n}|X^{n}}=\mathsf{EC}_{q}(\delta)^{n}.
Let S be random subset of [n] which includes each i\in[n] independently with probability 1-\delta.
A direct computation, shows that  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle I(U;E^{n}) 
 \displaystyle=I(U;X_{S},S)=\sum_{\sigma\subset[n]}\mathbb{P}[S=\sigma]I(U;X_{\sigma}) 
  
  (64)    
  
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq\sum_{\sigma\subset[n]}\mathbb{P}[S=\sigma]\min(|\sigma|\log q,t)=\mathbb{E}[\min(B\log q,t)]\,. 
  
  (65)    
 
  From here (  63  ) follows by taking supremum over P_{U,X^{n}}.  
 
 
  Claims about tightness follow by constructing U=X^{n} and taking X^{n} to be the output of the MDS code (so that
H(X_{\sigma})=\min(|\sigma|\log q,t)) and invoking the concavity of F_{I}(t). One also notes that [n,1,n]_{q}
(repetition code) and [n,n-1,2] (single parity check code) show tightness at t=\log q and t=(n-1)\log q.  
 
 
  Finally, we prove that when t=k\log q and the bound (  63  ) is tight then a (possibly non-linear)
(n,k,n-k+1)_{q} MDS code must exist. First, notice that the right-hand side of (  63  ) is a piecewise-linear and
concave function. Thus the bound being tight for F_{I}(t) (that is a concave-envelope of F_{I}(t)) should also
be tight as a bound for F_{I}(t). Consequently, there must exist U\to X^{n}\to E^{n} such that the
bound (  65  ) is tight with t=I(U;X^{n}). This implies that we should have  
 

  
  
 I(U;X_{\sigma})=\min(\sigma\log q,t) 
  
  (66)    
 
  for all \sigma\subset[n]. In particular, we have I(U;X_{i})=\log q and thus H(X_{i}|U)=0 and without loss of
generality we may assume that U=X^{n}. Again from (  66  ) we have that H(X^{n})=H(X^{k})=k\log q. This implies
that X^{n} is a uniform distribution on a set of size q^{k} and projection on any k coordinates is injective. This is
exactly the characterization of an MDS code (possibly non-linear) with parameters (n,k,n-k+1)_{q}.
∎  
 
 
 
 We also formulate some interesting observations for binary erasure channels: 
 
 
   Proposition 17   .  
 
  For \mathsf{BEC}(n,\delta) we have:  
 
  1.  
 
  For  n\geq 3  we have that  F_{I}(t)  is not concave. More exactly,  F_{I}(t)<F_{I}^{c}(t)  for  t\in(1,2) .  
  
  2.  
 
  For arbitrary  n  and  t\leq\log 2  or  t\geq(n-1)\log 2  we have  F_{I}(t)=F_{I}^{c}(t)=\mathbb{E}[\min(B\log 2,t)]  with  B  defined in
in (   63   ).  
  
  3.  
 
  For  t=2,n=4  the bound (   63   ) is not tight and  F_{I}^{c}(t)<\mathbb{E}[\min(B\log 2,t)] .  
  
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  First note that in Definition   1   of F_{I}(t) the supremum is a maximum and
and U can be restricted to alphabet of size |{\mathcal{X}}|+2. So in
particular, F_{I}(t)=f if and only if there exists I(U;Y^{n})=f,
I(U;X^{n})\leq t.  
 
 
  Now consider t\in(1,2) and n=3 and suppose (U,X^{n}) achieves the
bound. For the bound to be tight we must have I(U;X^{3})=t. For the bound to
be tight we must have I(U;X_{i})=1 for all i, that is H(X_{i})=1, H(X_{i}|U)=0 and H(X^{n}|U)=0. Consequently,
without loss of generality we may take U=X^{n}. So for the bound to be tight we need to have distribution s.t.  
 

  
  
 H(X^{3})=H(X_{1},X_{2})=H(X_{2},X_{3})=H(X_{1},X_{3})=t,H(X_{1})=H(X_{2})=H(X_{3})=1. 
  
  (67)    
 
  Note that since it is straightforward to verify that this set of entropies satisfies Shannon
inequalities (easy to check), the main result of  [ ZY97 ]  shows that there exist a sequence of triples
X^{3} (over large alphabets) which attains this point. We will show that this is impossible for binary-valued random variables.
First, notice that the set of achievable entropy vectors by binary triplets is a closed subset of \mathbb{R}_{+}^{7} (as a
continuous image of a compact set). Thus, it is sufficient to show that (  67  ) itself is not achievable.  
 
 
  Second, note that for any pair A,B of binary random variables with uniform marginals we must have  
 

  
  
 A=B+Z,\qquad B\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}Z\sim\text{Bern}(p)\,. 
    
 
  Without loss of generality, assume that X_{2}=X_{1}+Z where H(Z)=t-1>0. Moreover, H(X_{3}|X_{1},X_{2})=0 implies that
X_{3}=f(X_{1},X_{2}) for some function f.  
 
 
  Given X_{1} we have H(X_{3}|X_{1}=x)=H(X_{3}|X_{2}=x)=t-1>0.
So the function X_{1}\mapsto f(X_{1},x) should not be constant for either
choice of x\in\{0,1\} and the same holds for X_{2}\mapsto f(x,X_{2}). Eliminating cases leaves us
with f=X_{1}+X_{2} or f=X_{1}+X_{2}+1. But then X_{3}=X_{1}+X_{2}=Z and H(X_{3})<1, which is a contradiction.  
 
 
  Since by Theorem   16   we know that the bound (  63  ) is tight for F_{I}(t) we conclude
that  
 

  
  
 F_{I}(t)<F_{I}^{c}(t),\quad\forall t\in(1,2)\,. 
    
 
 
 
  To show the second claim consider U=X^{n} and X_{1}=\cdots=X_{n}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p) for t\leq\log 2. For t\geq(n-1)\log 2
take X^{n-1} to be iid \mathrm{Bern}({1\over 2}) and  
 

  
  
 X_{n}=X_{1}+\cdots+X_{n-1}+Z\,, 
    
 
  where Z\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p). This yields I(U;X_{\sigma})=H(X_{\sigma})=|\sigma|\log 2 for every subset
\sigma\subset[n] of size up to n-1. Consequently, the bound (  63  ) must be tight.  
 
 
  Finally, third claim follows from Theorem   16   and the fact that there is no [4,2,3] binary code,
e.g.  [ MS77 , Corollary 7, Chapter 11] .
∎  
 
 
 
 Putting together (  61  ) and (  63  ) we get the following upper bound on the concavified F_{I}-curve of n-letter product channels in terms of the contraction coefficient of the single-letter channel. 
 
 
   Corollary 18   .  
 
  If \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X})=\eta, then  
 

  
  
 F_{I}^{c}(t,P_{Y|X}^{n})\leq\mathbb{E}[\min(B\log q,t)],\qquad B\sim\mathrm{Binom}(n,1-\delta)\,. 
    
 
 
 
 
 This gives an alternative proof of Corollary   6   for the case of no feedback. 
 
 
 
  6.2  Samorodnitsky’s SDPI 

 
 So far, we have been concerned with bounding the “output” mutual information in terms of a certain “input” one. However,
frequently, one is interested in bounding some “output” information given knowledge of several input ones. For
example, for the parallel channel we have shown that 
 

  
  
 I(W;Y^{n})\leq(1-(1-\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{Y|X}))^{n})I(W;X^{n})\,. 
    
 
 But it turns out a stronger bound can be given if we have finer knowledge about the joint distribution of W,X^{n}. 
 
 
 The following bound can be distilled from  [ Sam15 ] : 
 
 
   Theorem 19     (Samorodnitsky) .  
 
  Consider the Bayesian network  
 

  
  
 U\to X^{n}\to Y^{n}\,, 
    
 
  where P_{Y^{n}|X^{n}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}P_{Y_{i}|X_{i}} is a memoryless channel with \eta_{i}\triangleq\eta_{KL}(P_{Y_{i}|X_{i}}).
Then we have  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y^{n})\leq I(U;X_{S}|S)=I(U;X_{S},S)\,, 
  
  (68)    
 
  where S\mathchoice{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\displaystyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\displaystyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\textstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\textstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptstyle\perp}}}{\mathrel{\hbox to0.0pt{$\scriptscriptstyle\perp$\hss}\mkern 2.0mu{\scriptscriptstyle\perp}}}(U,X^{n},Y^{n}) is a random subset of [n] generated by independently sampling each element i with
probability \eta_{i}. In particular, if \eta_{i}=\eta for all i, then  
 

  
  
 I(U;Y^{n})\leq\sum_{\sigma\subset[n]}\eta^{|\sigma|}(1-\eta)^{n-|\sigma|}I(U;X_{\sigma}) 
  
  (69)    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Just put together characterization (  59  ), tensorization property Proposition   15   to
get I(U;Y^{n})\leq I(U;E^{n}), where E^{n} is the output of the product of erasure channels with erasure probabilities
1-\eta_{i}. Then the calculation (  64  ) completes the proof.
∎  
 
 
 
   Remark 4   .  
 
 Let us say that “total” information I(U;X^{n}) is distributed among subsets
of [n] as given by the following numbers: 
 

  
  
 I_{k}\triangleq{n\choose k}^{-1}\sum_{T\in{[n]\choose k}}I(U;X_{T})\,. 
    
 
 Then bound (  69  ) says (replacing \mathrm{Binom}(n,\eta) by its mean value \eta n): 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y^{n})\lesssim I_{\eta n}\,. 
    
 
 Informally: the only kind of information about U that has a chance to be inferred on the basis of Y^{n} is one that is
contained in subsets of X of size \eta n (or smaller).

  Appendix A  Simultaneously maximal couplings 

 
   Lemma 20   .  
 
  Let {\mathcal{X}} and {\mathcal{Y}} be Polish spaces.
Given any pair of Borel probability measures P_{XY},Q_{XY} on {\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}, there exists a coupling \pi of P_{XY} and Q_{XY}, namely, a joint distribution of (X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}) such that {\mathcal{L}}(X,Y)=P_{XY} and {\mathcal{L}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=Q_{XY} under \pi, such that  
 

  
  
 \pi\{(X,Y)\neq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\}=d_{\rm TV}(P_{XY},Q_{XY})\quad\text{and}\quad\pi\{X\neq X^{\prime}\}=d_{\rm TV}(P_{X},Q_{X}). 
  
  (70)    
 
 
 
 
   Remark 5   .  
 
 After submitting this manuscript, we were informed that this result is the main content of  [ Gol79 ] .
For interested reader we keep our original proof which is based on Kantorovich’s dual representation (though non-constructive). 
 
 
 
   Remark 6   .  
 
 A triply-optimal coupling achieving in addition to (  70  ) also \pi[Y\neq Y^{\prime}]=d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y},Q_{Y}) need not exist.
Indeed, consider the example where X,Y are \{0,1\}-valued and 
 

  
  
 P_{XY}=\begin{pmatrix}{1\over 2}&0\\
0&{1\over 2}\end{pmatrix},\quad Q_{XY}=\begin{pmatrix}0&{1\over 2}\\
{1\over 2}&0\end{pmatrix}\,. 
    
 
 In other words, X,Y\sim\text{Bern}(1/2) under both P and Q; however, X=Y under P and X=1-Y under Q.
Furthermore, since d_{\rm TV}(P_{X},Q_{X})=d_{\rm TV}(P_{Y},Q_{Y})=0, under any coupling \pi_{XYX^{\prime}Y^{\prime}} of P_{XY} and Q_{XY}
that simultaneously couples P_{X} to Q_{X} and P_{Y} to Q_{Y} maximally, we have
X=X^{\prime} and Y=Y^{\prime}, which contradicts X=Y and X^{\prime}=1-Y^{\prime}.
On the other hand, it is clear that a doubly-optimal coupling (as
claimed by Lemma   20  ) exists: just take X=X^{\prime}=Y\sim\mathrm{Bern}(1/2) and Y^{\prime}=1-X^{\prime}. It is not hard to show that such a coupling also attains the minimum 
 

  
  
 \min_{\pi}\pi[(X,Y)\neq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})]+\pi[X\neq X^{\prime}]+\pi[Y\neq Y^{\prime}]=2. 
    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  Define the cost function c(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\triangleq{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{(x,y)\neq(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})}\right\}}}+{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{x\neq x^{\prime}}\right\}}}=2{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{x\neq x^{\prime}}\right\}}}+{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{x=x^{\prime},y\neq y^{\prime}}\right\}}}. Since the indicator of any open set is lower semicontinuous, so is (x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\mapsto c(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}). Applying Kantorovich’s duality theorem (see, e.g.,  [ Vil03 , Theorem 1.3] ), we have  
 

  
  
 \min_{\pi\in\Pi(P_{XY},Q_{XY})}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}c(X,Y,X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})=\max_{f,g}\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[g(X,Y)]. 
  
  (71)    
 
  where f\in L_{1}(P),g\in L_{1}(Q) and  
 

  
  
 f(x,y)-g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq c(x,y,x^{\prime},y^{\prime}). 
  
  (72)    
 
  Since the cost function is bounded, namely, c takes values in [0,2], applying  [ Vil03 , Remark 1.3] , we conclude that it suffices to consider 0\leq f,g\leq 2. Note that constraint (  72  ) is equivalent to  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle f(x,y)-g(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}) 
 \displaystyle\leq 2,\forall x\neq x^{\prime},\forall y\neq y^{\prime} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle f(x,y)-g(x,y^{\prime}) 
 \displaystyle\leq 1,\forall x,\forall y\neq y^{\prime} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle f(x,y)-g(x,y) 
 \displaystyle\leq 0,\forall x,\forall y 
    
 
  where the first condition is redundant given the range of f,g. In summary, the maximum on the right-hand side of (  71  ) can be taken
over all f,g satisfying the following constraints:  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle 0\leq f,g 
 \displaystyle\leq 2 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle f(x,y)-g(x,y^{\prime}) 
 \displaystyle\leq 1,\forall x,y\neq y^{\prime} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle f(x,y)-g(x,y) 
 \displaystyle\leq 0,\forall x,y 
    
 
  Then  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\max_{f,g}\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[g(X,Y)]= 
 \displaystyle~\int_{{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{\phi,\psi}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}p(x,y)\phi(y)-q(x,y)\psi(y)\right\} 
  
  (73)    
 
  where the maximum on the right-hand side is over \phi,\psi:{\mathcal{Y}}\to\mathbb{R} satisfying  
 
 
 
  
 \displaystyle 0\leq\phi,\psi\leq 2 
  
  (74)   
 
  
 \displaystyle\phi(y)-\psi(y^{\prime})\leq 1,\forall y\neq y^{\prime} 
   
 
  
 \displaystyle\phi(y)-\psi(y)\leq 0,\forall y 
    
 
 
 
  The optimization problem in the bracket on the RHS of (  73  ) can be solved using the following lemma:  
 
 
   Lemma 21   .  
 
  Let p,q\geq 0. Let (x)_{+}\triangleq\max\{x,0\}. Then  
 

  
  
 \max_{\phi,\psi}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}p\phi-q\psi:0\leq\phi\leq\psi\leq 2,\sup\phi\leq 1+\inf\psi\right\}=\int(p-q)_{+}+\left(\int(p-q)\right)_{+}. 
  
  (75)    
 
 
 
 
 Proof. 
 
  First we show that it suffices to consider \phi=\psi. Given any feasible pair (\phi,\psi), set \phi^{\prime}=\max\{\phi,\inf\psi\}. To check that (\phi^{\prime},\phi^{\prime}) is a feasible pair, note that clearly \phi^{\prime} takes values in [0,2]. Furthermore, \sup\phi^{\prime}\leq\sup\phi\leq 1+\inf\psi\leq 1+\inf\phi^{\prime}. Therefore the maximum on the left-hand side of (  75  ) is equal to  
 

  
  
 \max_{\phi}\left\{\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(p-q)\phi:0\leq\phi\leq 2,\sup\phi\leq 1+\inf\phi\right\}. 
    
 
  Let a=\inf\phi. Then  
 

  
  
 \displaystyle\max_{\phi}\left\{\int(p-q)\phi:0\leq\phi\leq 2,\sup\phi\leq 1+\inf\phi\right\}= 
 \displaystyle~\sup_{0\leq a\leq 2}\max_{\phi}\left\{\int(p-q)\phi:a\leq\phi\leq 2\wedge(1+a)\right\} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\sup_{0\leq a\leq 1}\max_{\phi}\left\{\int(p-q)\phi:a\leq\phi\leq 1+a\right\} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\sup_{0\leq a\leq 1}\left\{(1+a)\int(p-q)_{+}+a\int(p-q)_{-}\right\} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\sup_{0\leq a\leq 1}\left\{\int(p-q)_{+}+a\int(p-q)\right\} 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\int(p-q)_{+}+\left(\int(p-q)\right)_{+}. 
    
 
  ∎  
 
 
 
  Applying Lemma   21   to (  73  ) for fixed x, we have  
 

  
  
  
 \displaystyle~\max_{f,g}\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(X,Y)]-\mathbb{E}_{Q}[g(X,Y)] 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(p(x,y)-q(x,y))_{+}+(p(x)-q(x))_{+}\right) 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle= 
 \displaystyle~\int_{\mathcal{X}}\int_{\mathcal{Y}}(p(x,y)-q(x,y))_{+}+\int_{\mathcal{X}}(p(x)-q(x))_{+}=d_{\rm TV}(P_{XY},Q_{XY})+d_{\rm TV}(P_{X},Q_{X}) 
    
 
  Combining the above with (  71  ), we have  
 

  
  
 \min_{\pi_{XYX^{\prime}Y^{\prime}}}\pi\{(X,Y)\neq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\}+\pi\{X\neq X^{\prime}\}=d_{\rm TV}(P_{XY},Q_{XY})+d_{\rm TV}(P_{X},Q_{X}). 
    
 
  Since \pi\{(X,Y)\neq(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\}\geq d_{\rm TV}(P_{XY},Q_{XY}) and \pi\{X\neq X^{\prime}\}\geq d_{\rm TV}(P_{X},Q_{X}) for any \pi, the minimizer
of the sum on the left-hand side achieves equality simultaneously for both terms, proving the theorem.
∎  
 
 
 
 
  Appendix B  Contraction coefficients on general spaces 

 
  B.1  Proof of Theorem   2   

 
 We show that 
 

  
  
 \eta_{f}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})=\sup_{Q_{X}}\frac{D_{f}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{D_{f}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})}\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{Y|X},P_{X})=\sup_{Q_{X}}\frac{\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})}, 
  
  (76)    
 
 where both suprema are over all Q_{X} such that the respective denominator is in (0,\infty). With the assumption that P_{X} is not a point mass, namely, there exists a measurable set E such that P_{X}(E)\in(0,1), it is clear that such Q_{X} always exists. For example, let Q_{X}=\frac{1}{2}(P_{X}+P_{X|X\in E}), then \frac{1}{2}\leq\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\leq\frac{1}{2}(1+\frac{1}{P_{X}(E)}) and hence D_{f}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})<\infty since f is continuous. Furthermore, Q_{X}\neq P_{X} implies that D_{f}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})\neq 0  [ Csi67 ] . Let 
 

  
  
 \eta_{I}(P_{X})=\sup\frac{I(U;Y)}{I(U;X)} 
    
 
 where the supremum is over all Markov chains U\to X\to Y with fixed P_{XY} such that 0<I(U;X)<\infty.
First note that 
 
 
 The proof follows that of  [ CIR+93 , Theorem 5.4]  using the local quadratic behavior of f-divergence; however, in order to deal with general alphabets, additional approximation steps are needed to ensure the likelihood ratio is bounded away from zero and infinity. 
 
 
 Fix Q_{X} such that \chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})<\infty. Let A=\{x:\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}(x)<a\} where a>0 is sufficiently large such that Q_{X}(A)\geq 1/2.
Let Q_{X}^{\prime}=Q_{X|X\in A} and Q^{\prime}_{Y}=P_{Y|X}\circ Q_{X}^{\prime}. Then \frac{{\rm d}Q^{\prime}_{Y}}{{\rm d}P_{Y}}\leq\frac{a}{Q_{X}(A)}\leq 2a.
Let Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}=\frac{1}{a}P_{X}+(1-\frac{1}{a})Q_{X}^{\prime} and Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}=P_{Y|X}\circ Q_{X}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{a}P_{Y}+(1-\frac{1}{a})Q_{Y}^{\prime}.
Then we have 
 

  
  
 \frac{1}{a}\leq\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\leq 2a+\frac{1}{a},\quad\frac{1}{a}\leq\frac{{\rm d}Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}}{{\rm d}P_{Y}}\leq 2a+\frac{1}{a}. 
  
  (77)    
 
 Note that
\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime}\|P_{X})=\frac{1}{Q(X\in A)}\mathbb{E}_{P}[(\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}})^{2}{\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{X\in A}\right\}}}]-1. By dominated convergence theorem,
\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime}\|P_{X})\to\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X}) as a\to\infty.
On the other hand,
since Q^{\prime}_{Y}\to Q_{Y} pointwise, the weak lower-semicontinuity of \chi^{2}-divergence yields
\liminf_{a\to\infty}\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}^{\prime}\|P_{Y})\geq\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y}).
Furthermore, using the simple fact that \chi^{2}(\epsilon P+(1-\epsilon)Q\|P)=(1-\epsilon)^{2}\chi^{2}(Q\|P), we have \frac{\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{X})}{\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{Y})}=\frac{\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime}\|P_{X})}{\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}^{\prime}\|P_{Y})}.
Therefore, to prove (  76  ), it suffices to show for each fixed a, for any \delta>0, there exists \tilde{P}_{X} such that
\frac{D_{f}(\tilde{P}_{X}\|\tilde{P}_{Y})}{D_{f}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})}\geq\frac{\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{X})}{\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{Y})}-\delta. 
 
 
 For 0<\epsilon<1, let \tilde{P}_{X}=\bar{\epsilon}P_{X}+\epsilon Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}, which induces \tilde{P}_{Y}=P_{Y|X}\circ\tilde{P}_{X}=\bar{\epsilon}P_{Y}+\epsilon Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}. Then
D_{f}(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}[f(1+\epsilon(\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}-1))]. Recall from (  77  ) that
\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\in[\frac{1}{a},\frac{1}{a}+2a]. Since f^{\prime\prime} is continuous and f^{\prime\prime}(1)=1, by Taylor’s theorem and dominated convergence theorem, we have
D_{f}(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\chi^{2}(Q_{X}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{X})(1+o(1)). Analogously, D_{f}(\tilde{P}_{Y}\|P_{Y})=\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}^{\prime\prime}\|P_{Y})(1+o(1)).
This completes the proof of \eta_{f}(P_{X})\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{X}). 
 
 
   Remark 7   .  
 
 In the special case of KL divergence, we can circumvent the step of approximating by bounded likelihood ratio:
By  [ PW15 , Lemma 4.2] , since \chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})\leq\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})<\infty, we have
D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})=\epsilon^{2}\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})/2+o(\epsilon^{2}) and D(\tilde{P}_{Y}\|P_{Y})=\epsilon^{2}\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})/2+o(\epsilon^{2}), as \epsilon\to 0. Therefore \frac{\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X})}\leq\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{D(\tilde{P}_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})}\leq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X}). Therefore \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X})\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}}(P_{X}) 
 
 
 
 
  B.2  Proof of Theorem   3   

 
 We prove 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}=\eta_{\chi^{2}}. 
  
  (78)    
 
 First of all, \eta_{\rm KL}\geq\eta_{\chi^{2}} follows from Theorem   2  .
For the other direction we closely follow the argument of  [ CRS94 , Theorem 1] . Below we prove the following integral representation: 
 

  
  
 D(Q\|P)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi^{2}(Q\|P^{t}){\rm d}t, 
  
  (79)    
 
 where P^{t}\triangleq\frac{tQ+P}{1+t}. Then 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})= 
 \displaystyle~\int_{0}^{\infty}\chi^{2}(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y}^{t}){\rm d}t 
    
  
  
 \displaystyle\leq 
 \displaystyle~\int_{0}^{\infty}\eta_{\chi^{2}}\cdot\chi^{2}(Q_{X}\|P_{X}^{t}){\rm d}t=\eta_{\chi^{2}}D(Q_{X}\|P_{X}). 
    
 
 where we used P_{Y}^{t}=P_{Y|X}\circ P_{X}^{t}.
It remains to check (  79  ). Note that 
 

  
  
 -\log x=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1-x}{(x+t)(1+t)}{\rm d}t 
    
 
 Therefore 
 

  
  
 D(Q\|P)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+t}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\frac{{\rm d}Q-{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}P+t{\rm d}Q}\right]{\rm d}t 
    
 
 Note that t\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\frac{{\rm d}Q-{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}P+t{\rm d}Q}\right]=-\mathbb{E}_{P}\left[\frac{{\rm d}Q-{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}P+t{\rm d}Q}\right]. Therefore
\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\frac{{\rm d}Q-{\rm d}P}{{\rm d}P+t{\rm d}Q}\right]=\frac{1}{1+t}\int\frac{({\rm d}Q-{\rm d}P)^{2}}{{\rm d}P+t{\rm d}Q}=(1+t)\chi^{2}(Q\|P^{t}), completing the proof of (  79  ). 
 
 
 
  B.3  Proof of Theorem   4   

 
 We shall assume that P_{X} is not a point mass, namely, there exists a measurable set E such that P_{X}(E)\in(0,1).
Define 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X})=\sup_{Q_{X}}\frac{D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})} 
    
 
 where the supremum is over all Q_{X} such that 0<D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})<\infty.
It is clear that such Q_{X} always exists (e.g., Q_{X}=P_{X|X\in E} and D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})=\log\frac{1}{P_{X}(E)}\in(0,\infty)). Let 
 

  
  
 \eta_{I}(P_{X})=\sup\frac{I(U;Y)}{I(U;X)} 
    
 
 where the supremum is over all Markov chains U\to X\to Y with fixed P_{XY} such that 0<I(U;X)<\infty. Such Markov chains always exist, e.g., U={\mathbf{1}_{\left\{{X\in E}\right\}}} and then I(U;X)=h(P_{X}(E))\in(0,\log 2).
The goal of this appendix is to prove (  18  ), namely 
 

  
  
 \eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X})=\eta_{I}(P_{X})\,. 
    
 
 
 
 The inequality \eta_{I}(P_{X})\leq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X}) follows trivially: 
 

  
  
 I(U;Y)=D(P_{Y|U}\|P_{Y}|P_{U})\leq\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X})D(P_{X|U}\|P_{X}|P_{U})=\eta_{\rm KL}(P_{X})I(X;U)\,. 
    
 
 For the other direction, fix Q_{X} such that 0<D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})<\infty. First, consider the case where \frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}
is bounded, namely, \frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\leq a for some a>0 Q_{X}-a.s.
For any \epsilon\leq\frac{1}{2a}, let U\sim\text{Bern}(\epsilon) and define the probability measure \tilde{P}_{X}=\frac{P_{X}-\epsilon Q_{X}}{1-\epsilon}. Let P_{X|U=0}=\tilde{P}_{X} and P_{X|U=1}=Q_{X}, which defines a Markov chain U\to X\to Y such that X,Y is distributed as the desired P_{XY}. Note that 
 

  
  
 \frac{I(U;Y)}{I(U;X)}=\frac{\bar{\epsilon}D(\tilde{P}_{Y}\|P_{Y})+\epsilon D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{\bar{\epsilon}D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})+\epsilon D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})} 
    
 
 where \tilde{P}_{Y}=P_{Y|X}\circ\tilde{P}_{X}.
We claim that 
 

  
  
 D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})=o(\epsilon), 
  
  (80)    
 
 which, in view of the data processing inequality D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})\leq D(\tilde{P}_{Y}\|P_{Y}), implies \frac{I(U;Y)}{I(U;X)}\xrightarrow{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})}{D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})} as desired.
To establish (  80  ), define the function 
 

  
  
 f(x,\epsilon)\triangleq\begin{cases}{1-\epsilon x\over\epsilon(1-\epsilon)}\log{1-\epsilon x\over 1-\epsilon}\,,&\epsilon>0\\
(x-1)\log e,&\epsilon=0\,.\end{cases} 
    
 
 One easily notices that f is continuous on [0,a]\times[0,{1\over 2a}] and thus bounded. So we get, by
bounded convergence theorem, 
 

  
  
 \frac{1}{\epsilon}D(\tilde{P}_{X}\|P_{X})=\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[f\left(\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}},\epsilon\right)\right]\to\mathbb{E}_{P_{X}}\left[\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}-1\right]\log e=0\,. 
    
 
 
 
 To drop the boundedness assumption on \frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}} we simply consider the conditional distribution Q_{X}^{\prime}\triangleq Q_{X|X\in A}
where A=\{x:\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}(x)<a\} and a>0 is sufficiently large so that Q_{X}(A)>0. Clearly, as a\to\infty, we have Q_{X}^{\prime}\to Q_{X} and Q^{\prime}_{Y}\to Q_{Y}
pointwise, where Q^{\prime}_{Y}\triangleq P_{Y|X}\circ Q^{\prime}_{X}. Hence the lower-semicontinuity of divergence yields 
 

  
  
 \liminf_{a\to\infty}D(Q_{Y}^{\prime}\|P_{Y})\geq D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})\,. 
    
 
 Furthermore, since \frac{{\rm d}Q^{\prime}_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}=\frac{1}{Q_{X}(A)}\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\mathbf{1}_{A}, we have 
 

  
  
 \displaystyle D(Q^{\prime}_{X}\|P_{X})= 
 \displaystyle~\log\frac{1}{Q_{X}(A)}+\frac{1}{Q_{X}(A)}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\log\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}\leq a\right\}\right]. 
  
  (81)    
 
 Since Q_{X}(A)\to 1, by dominated convergence (note: \mathbb{E}_{Q}[|\log\frac{{\rm d}Q_{X}}{{\rm d}P_{X}}|]<\infty) we have
D(Q^{\prime}_{X}\|P_{X})\to D(Q_{X}\|P_{X}).
Therefore, 
 

  
  
 \liminf_{a\to\infty}{D(Q_{Y}^{\prime}\|P_{Y})\over D(Q_{X}^{\prime}\|P_{X})}\geq{D(Q_{Y}\|P_{Y})\over D(Q_{X}\|P_{X})}\,, 
    
 
 completing the proof.
