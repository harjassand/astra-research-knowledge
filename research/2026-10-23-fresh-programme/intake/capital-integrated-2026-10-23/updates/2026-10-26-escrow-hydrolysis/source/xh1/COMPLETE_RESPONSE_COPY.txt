## Astra Chemistry — A stronger chemical mechanism

### 1. An experimentally demonstrated protective switch

The previous DNA-escort design had a difficult requirement: an activated donor must be able to dock and redock repeatedly without reacting incorrectly while temporarily undocked.

A particularly relevant experiment by Frommer and colleagues (2024) provides a stronger physical starting point.

They demonstrated that a DNA duplex containing an abasic site can shelter an attached thioester. Changing the DNA pairing arrangement by strand displacement can then expose the donor to a nearby amine for acyl transfer.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

Wiley Online Library



Two geometries were observed:

| DNA arrangement                                          | Experimental effect                                        |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Protective abasic-site geometry, approximately offset −3 | More than 100-fold protection against thioester hydrolysis |
| Productive geometry, approximately offset +1 or +2       | Strongly favored DNA-templated amide formation             |

The authors also demonstrated strand-displacement activation after prolonged storage of the protected donor.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

Wiley Online Library



This suggests a two-register chemical escrow:

1. Keep the donor's acyl group sheltered in a DNA structure while it waits or temporarily loses its short PNA recognition contact.
2. Only a correctly positioned molecular complex changes the DNA arrangement into the acyl-transfer geometry.
3. Correct amide formation consumes the donor linkage, while an unsuccessful parent retains its amino terminus and can try again.

This is more chemically specific than assuming that a DNA escort somehow prevents all unwanted reactions.

There are critical limitations. Frommer's experiment used a TAMRA-linked thioester, not Astra's activated PNA tetramer. The protection depended on the attached molecule: a biotin-containing analogue did not exhibit the same protective effect. Moreover, the demonstrated protection concerns hydrolysis, not universal suppression of reactions with other amines.

The original experiments also used alkaline conditions, including pH 11, rather than an established common operating environment for the proposed long-program PNA machine. The two-register operation is a demonstrated chemical precedent, but its integration into Astra's intended synthesis remains an invention to be tested.

### 2. New theorem: protecting donors from hydrolysis does not automatically improve chemical fidelity

I modeled two reversible DNA-controlled states:

- \\(S\\): a protected donor, held by its escort.
- \\(A\\): an authorized donor, positioned for acyl transfer.

The donor changes from \\(S\\) to \\(A\\) at rate \\(q\\), and can return to protection at rate \\(r\\). Correct amide formation occurs from \\(A\\) at rate \\(k\\).

The model also charges incorrect covalent reactions in both states, hydrolysis and escape into solution.

Let \\(w_S\\) denote irreversible wrong-reaction leakage while protected, \\(w_A\\) the corresponding hazard while active, \\(e\\) the escape rate from the active state, \\(\chi\\) the probability that escape causes a wrong bond, and \\(h_A\\) the active-state hydrolysis rate.

Then the exact wrong-to-correct product ratio is

\\[ \boxed{ \rho= \frac{w_A+e\chi}{k} + \frac{w_S}{q} \frac{k+w_A+h_A+e+r}{k}. } \\]

I independently verified the expression by solving the absorbing continuous-time Markov chain and testing it against stochastic trajectories.

The surprising result is that the protected-state hydrolysis rate does not appear in this ratio.

Under the stated assumptions, lowering hydrolysis improves the fraction of donors that survive an individual attempt, but it does not change the odds between correct and incorrect permanent bonds. Indeed, if failed donors can be replaced instantly and harmlessly, the expected time to the next permanent outcome is also independent of the protected-state hydrolysis rate.

That conclusion changes when donor replacement takes time, consumes material, creates template-binding waste, or damages the parent. Under those realistic complications, protection can be valuable.

The distinction matters for Astra: the experimentally demonstrated 100-fold hydrolysis protection cannot be interpreted as 100-fold chemical proofreading.

### 3. A minimum standard for chemical shielding

For a 1,000-instruction program to achieve 99% full-chain correctness from these reaction channels alone, the wrong-to-correct ratio must satisfy

\\[ \rho\leq 0.99^{-1/1000}-1 \approx1.00504\times10^{-5}. \\]

This immediately yields two restrictions:

\\[ w_A+e\chi \leq 1.00504\times10^{-5}k, \\]

and, even with no active-state errors,

\\[ w_S\leq 1.00504\times10^{-5} \frac{qk}{k+w_A+h_A+e+r}. \\]

Thus the protected thioester must not merely survive water exposure. It must also be sufficiently inaccessible to unwanted nucleophiles.

There is another consequence: frequent switches back into protection increase the number of opportunities for protected-state leakage. Reversible switching is beneficial for rescuing temporarily undocked donors only if its added leakage exposure is sufficiently small.

This makes unwanted aminolysis in the protected configuration a more important physical measurement than protected-state hydrolysis alone.

### 4. A finite optimum exists for DNA proofreading

The next calculation challenges the earlier assumption that adding more DNA recognition checkpoints always improves fidelity.

I modeled a donor passing \\(n\\) checkpoints. At each checkpoint it can advance, dissociate harmlessly, or undergo a premature irreversible reaction. Correct and incorrect donors have different dissociation rates.

When premature chemistry is impossible, additional checkpoints can progressively suppress mismatched donors, although the reaction becomes slower.

When premature chemistry has any fixed nonzero hazard, that conclusion changes. As more checkpoints are added, correct donors increasingly fail to reach the reaction, but incorrectly reacting donors still have opportunities to produce permanent errors.

There is an optimal finite number of checkpoints. Beyond it, more proofreading makes eventual chemical fidelity worse.

For a hypothetical system with checkpoint progression \\(0.15\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\), matched-donor dissociation \\(0.05\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\), mismatched-donor dissociation \\(0.5\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\), and premature-coupling hazard \\(10^{-8}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\):

| Effective wrong-to-correct donor arrival ratio | Optimal checkpoints | Predicted 1,000-step correctness |
| ---------------------------------------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| 1:1                                            | 11                  | 99.24%                           |
| 255:1                                          | 13                  | 74.79%                           |

These are verified calculations in an explicit checkpoint Markov model, not experimentally acquired kinetics.

The 255:1 case is deliberately severe: it treats all 255 incorrect members of a 256-building-block repertoire as equally able to enter the checkpoint process. Effective DNA recognition should reduce that competition, but the reduction must be measured.

The model also reveals a throughput problem. In the 255:1 case, the 13-checkpoint optimum requires approximately 11,300 donor encounters per irreversible incorporation decision, with a modeled mean of 6.5 hours per instruction before loading and cleanup overhead.

A high-fidelity molecular assembly system therefore needs both selective recruitment of the intended donor and reliable rejection before chemical commitment. Deep proofreading alone is unlikely to be an economical solution.

### 5. Why hydrolysis protection can still make a major difference

I then added a realistic failure mode missing from the ideal theorem: every time a donor hydrolyzes and must be replaced, the recovery operation has a small chance of damaging the parent or losing its positional registration.

The protected and unprotected systems can have identical intrinsic correct-to-wrong amide selectivity, yet substantially different long-chain survival.

For one deliberately hypothetical chemical system, I varied only the hydrolysis rate in the protected state. I assumed a \\(10^{-5}\\) irreversible parent-loss probability per otherwise harmless donor-replacement event.

| Protected-state hydrolysis rate | Hydrolyzed fraction per donor encounter | Correct 1,000-step completion |
| ------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 0.1 s⁻¹                         | 92.9%                                   | 87.57%                        |
| 0.01 s⁻¹                        | 58.4%                                   | 98.45%                        |
| 0.001 s⁻¹                       | 18.8%                                   | 99.61%                        |
| 0.0001 s⁻¹                      | 10.2%                                   | 99.72%                        |

Without replacement-induced damage, these cases have the same modeled full-chain correctness of approximately 99.84%.

This establishes a more defensible use of Frommer's protective chemistry: it can preserve scarce reactive building blocks and reduce the number of potentially damaging repair operations, even if it does not inherently improve correct-versus-wrong covalent-bond discrimination.

The exact model also shows that protection becomes more valuable when donor replacement takes time. With an illustrative 10-second recovery overhead, the most hydrolysis-prone configuration requires approximately 311 seconds per terminal reaction outcome, compared with approximately 181 seconds in the more protected configuration.

The new constraint is that recovery is only harmless when the original rooted parent remains chemically competent and correctly registered. Hydrolyzed donors that stay attached to the template invalidate the ideal renewal assumption, as established in our earlier waste-poisoning analysis.

### 6. A concrete 256-donor addressing construction

To reduce the number of incorrect donors that reach the chemical checkpoint, I constructed a 256-entry DNA address family.

Each of the 256 possible PNA four-base building blocks receives a separate 16-base DNA escort barcode. I used an affine linear code over \\(\mathrm{GF}(4)\\), then independently verified all pairwise sequence distances.

The construction also has a minimum of five mismatches in full-length reverse-complement comparisons across the barcode set.

Under a simplified equilibrium model with independent mismatch energy penalties, eight substitutions would require a minimum penalty of approximately 1.26 kcal/mol per mismatch at 298 K to suppress aggregate wrong-donor binding below \\(10^{-5}\\), even with 255 competing species at equal concentration.

This is a useful theoretical address-separation target, not proof that the molecules meet it. The current set still contains 37 barcodes with homopolymer runs of four or more nucleotides, two extreme-GC cases, and several shorter complementary subsequences. It therefore needs sequence refinement and physical hybridization testing.

There is also an unavoidable geometry issue: 16 bases occupy about 5.44 nm along straight B-form DNA. A rooted polymer cannot generally advance by that distance after inserting one small chemical building block. This address system would require a moving or locally folded DNA instruction interface, rather than the previously rejected straight-template arrangement.

### 7. The strongest competing chemistry is now more substantial

A July 2026 Nature Catalysis paper by Schaub and colleagues demonstrated an engineered enzyme cascade combining CoA ligases and N-acyltransferases. It produced more than 120 different on-DNA amide products, with average conversion above 91% in an enzymatically constructed DNA-encoded library, while preserving the DNA barcode.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://www.nature.com\&sz=32)

Nature Catalysis



This is an important comparator because enzymes provide chemically defined active sites and can control reactive intermediates more directly than an exposed synthetic thioester.

It does not establish autonomous, independently advancing 256-monomer polymer synthesis. But Astra cannot assume its proposed nonenzymatic machine would outperform the enzymatic approach simply because it uses programmable DNA circuitry.

The comparison now favors different mechanisms for different objectives. For near-term chemical diversity and mild DNA-compatible reactions, the enzymatic system is experimentally stronger. For independently progressing molecular programs with potentially arbitrary building-block identities, the two-register DNA escrow remains an interesting but unacquired capability. For directly demonstrated repeatable PNA chain growth, Joshi's protected NHS-PNA chemistry remains the more established starting point.

### 8. Selected construction and next scientific obstacle

The strongest surviving construction is a DNA-addressed, two-register acyl-donor escrow.

The crucial molecular operation would be:

\\[ \text{Protected donor} \\;\rightleftharpoons\\; \text{Identity-qualified reactive donor} \\;\longrightarrow\\; \text{Correct rooted amide}. \\]

An unsuccessful donor must return to the protected state or be harmlessly removed. An incorporated product must remain masked against a second addition until its molecular instruction has advanced.

This construction has a sharper physical requirement than the previous escort:

The same chemically reactive group must be protected against unwanted amines while stored, exposed efficiently only in the intended rooted complex, and prevented from causing incorrect chemistry if activation is followed by donor escape.

The first discriminating system should have two independently programmed roots and two chemical instructions each. It should measure protected-state unwanted aminolysis, matched versus mismatched transfer, hydrolysis, recovery of failed parents, and chemically verified advancement. The product assay must distinguish the intended amide site from a merely attached incorrect product.

The principal new analytical finding is that increasing chemical protection or DNA proofreading can fail to improve eventual fidelity—and may worsen it—unless the physical mechanism actually changes the competition between correct and irreversible wrong reactions.
