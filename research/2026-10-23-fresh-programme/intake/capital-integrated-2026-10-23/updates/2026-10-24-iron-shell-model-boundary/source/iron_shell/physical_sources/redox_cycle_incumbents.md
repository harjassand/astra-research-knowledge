# Redox-strain pulse: strongest experimental incumbents

## Assessment

Redox cycling of iron/iron oxide is established in chemical-looping and thermochemical-storage experiments. The strongest pertinent incumbents show that repeated oxidation/reduction can change pore structure and reaction kinetics, including in pellet-scale lab specimens. This waives novelty for the broad idea of redox-driven morphological activation. The narrower operation—command a shallow Fe → FeO → Fe pulse in an already dense iron shell, then re-reduce to the same metal fraction to reopen a connected path—is not directly instantiated by the two experiments below. Their cycles use large gas-composition swings and generally cover a wider phase range; neither measures local redox strain or gas-path connectivity across an Fe shell.

## Two decisive primary studies

### 1. Lab-scale iron-oxide pellets across repeated steam/H2 redox cycles

Lea Huber, Melanie Heindl, Marc Schlosser, Arno Pfitzner, and Belal Dawoud, “On the Cycle Stability and Macroscopic Structure of Iron Oxide Pellets for Thermochemical Hydrogen Storage: Influence of Water Content during the Pelletizing Process,” *Applied Sciences* 13 (2023), 6408, [DOI 10.3390/app13116408](https://doi.org/10.3390/app13116408), [full article PDF](https://epub.uni-regensburg.de/58689/1/217_On%20the%20Cycle%20Stability%20and%20Macroscopic.pdf).

**Material/control/readout.** The team made gram-scale lab-pelletized composites of 90 wt.% Fe2O3 and 10 wt.% cement, varying water in green pellets from 18 to 33 wt.%, then sintered at 950 °C. At 800 °C they cycled reduction in 50% H2/50% N2 and oxidation in 50% steam. Under these conditions the expected oxygen-carrier sequence is Fe3O4 ⇌ 3FeO ⇌ 3Fe; hematite is not stable. Each stage was tracked by thermogravimetric mass change, normalized to Fe3O4 and Fe endpoints, and samples were scanned by micro-CT before/after cycling. The apparatus used mass-flow controllers for H2/N2 and a steam generator; the paper reports ±0.5% MFC accuracy, ±1% steam-rate accuracy, ±0.3 °C temperature tolerance, and ±0.1 µg thermobalance accuracy.

**What it establishes.** Cycle history and starting pore architecture matter. By the 6th cycle, the low-water P1 pellet needed about 26 min (reduction) and 19 min (oxidation) to reach conversion α=0.8; the high-water P10 pellet reached the same threshold in about 18 and 13 min by cycle 9. The low-water samples’ kinetics deteriorated more strongly. After cycling, reported porosity was about 23% for P1 (six cycles) versus 45–46% for high-water samples P8/P10 (nine cycles). Tomography showed internal cracks, a dense outer shell, and compaction/shrinkage; P2 volume fell from 630 to 387 mm³ after six cycles, while P5 fell from 1064 to 452 mm³ and P8 from 1180 to 571 mm³ after nine cycles. This is direct evidence that cyclic redox coexists with persistent kinetic differences and large structural evolution in pellets.

**Limits.** The variable being compared is water content during pelletization, not a controlled partial-oxidation pulse. The cycle uses broad reducing/oxidizing gas steps, and the mass-conversion calculation assumes Fe3O4/Fe endpoints; phase amounts after cycling were left for later work. The reported “open porosity” is estimated using µCT total volume and He-pycnometer density; the voxel size is about 8 µm. This gives a bulk He-accessible open-void fraction, but does not quantify a continuous inlet-to-core-to-outlet path, sub-8-µm throats, or permeability. Cracks are seen, but their cause is not attributed to trapped-water pressure; some shell-shaped cracking is attributed to layered agglomeration during pelletizing. No fines/attrition or bulk mechanical-strength test was performed; the authors explicitly defer mechanical stability to future work. The specimens are gram-scale synthetic Fe2O3/cement pellets, not industrial ore pellets.

### 2. Recycled micron-scale iron powder

J. Kuhn et al., “Iron as Recyclable Metal Fuel: Unraveling Oxidation Behavior and Cyclization Effects Through Thermogravimetric Analysis, Wide-Angle X-Ray Scattering and Mössbauer Spectroscopy,” *ChemSusChem* (2024), [DOI 10.1002/cssc.202400351](https://doi.org/10.1002/cssc.202400351), [publisher page](https://chemistry-europe.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cssc.202400351).

**Material/control/readout.** Iron powder batches with mean diameters 23, 70, 119, and 185 µm were studied; the cycling samples used 25 ± 3 mg iron diluted with SiO2 at 1:4 mass ratio to mitigate sintering/agglomeration. At 700 °C a cycle used 40 min synthetic-air oxidation, 20 min N2 purge, 30 min reduction in 5 or 10 vol.% H2/N2, then another N2 purge. The Fe23 powder was cycled up to ten times; a 10 vol.% H2 condition and larger Fe70 particles were tested for fewer cycles. TGA measured mass/oxygen conversion; SEM examined particle morphology; WAXS and post-cycle Mössbauer quantified phases.

**What it establishes.** Oxidation/reduction cycling is itself a morphology-changing actuator in iron particles. SEM showed a porous surface after cycling: many pores smaller than 1 µm after one cycle, and fewer but larger/deeper pores after ten. Oxidation rate increased with cycle count; the authors conclude that cycling enhanced reactivity overall, with a stronger effect on oxidation. However, reduction remained strongly staged and incomplete. After five cycles Mössbauer found Fe23 at about 50% Fe, 33% Fe3O4, and 17% FeO; Fe70 was about 33% Fe, 47% Fe3O4, and 21% FeO. The article explicitly says no complete reduction was reached under its conditions and calls for further intermediate-phase/in-situ work. Hence the measured morphology/oxidation benefit does not demonstrate a clean, fully re-reduced Fe shell or persistent improvement of the rate-limiting reduction step.

**Limits.** The iron particles are not ore pellets. Silica dilution is a laboratory device to limit sintering, not a pellet formulation; no 3D pore connectivity, crack statistics, gas permeability, or fines/attrition were measured. The oxidizing half-cycle is synthetic air, reaching iron oxides beyond FeO, not a shallow Fe/FeO pulse. This is strong incumbent evidence for cyclic morphological change, but not for the specific proposed partial-redox control.

## Boundary for the proposed operation

These two studies already demonstrate broad cycle-driven restructuring and show the practical tradeoff: more porosity can accompany faster reactions, while sintering, compaction, shell formation, incomplete re-reduction, and unmeasured mechanical damage remain. They do not establish that a prescribed shallow Fe → FeO → Fe excursion opens connected pathways through a dense Fe layer or that such gains survive repeated return to the same Fe/O endpoint. Nor do they attribute observed cracks to water pressure. For an eventual claim, the useful observable would be post-reduction gas permeability or connected-pore change at a matched measured Fe/O phase fraction, with mass conversion and a fines/strength balance; a rate increase alone is not diagnostic of pore reopening.

**Sharp falsifier:** after the partial Fe → FeO oxidation and re-reduction, the sample reaches the same Fe/O endpoint but has no increased connected permeability or reduction rate versus a thermally/gas-history-matched no-pulse control—or the gain vanishes after re-reduction, or requires damaging fines/strength loss. Any of those rejects the proposed useful persistent morphology operation for that material and cycle window.
