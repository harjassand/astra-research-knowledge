# Iron-shell / trapped-water pressure premise audit

## Finding

The available primary evidence supports (i) deuterium-bearing water-like clusters at the moving Fe/FeO interface of a reduced, needle-shaped APT specimen and (ii) oxide rims at internal pores in a separately reduced single-crystal specimen. It does **not** directly measure pressure inside those pores, water phase at reaction temperature, leakage rate, or pressure-driven shell fracture. The cited 4–5 atm pore-water pressure is a phase-field-model output, not an instrument reading. Both material studies deliberately use single-crystal reference material; neither validates the proposed total-pressure-pulse mechanism in an industrial iron-ore pellet.

## El-Zoka et al. (2023): direct observation versus inference

Primary: A. A. El-Zoka et al., “The Fate of Water in Hydrogen-Based Iron Oxide Reduction,” *Advanced Science* 10 (2023), 2300626, [DOI 10.1002/advs.202300626](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202300626), [publisher full text](https://advanced.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/advs.202300626), [PMC record](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10460863/).

- **Material and scale:** Czochralski-grown, [100]-oriented single-crystal wüstite (Fe1-xO), site-specifically FIB/PFIB-prepared as needle-shaped atom-probe specimens. El-Zoka et al. note this reference has substantially lower impurity content than mineral samples but still contains Ca, Al and Na. This is a nanoscale specimen with a strongly varying needle cross-section, not a pellet or representative grain. At 700 °C the Fe shell is reported as roughly 5–10 nm thick at different positions along the shank; reduction is faster near the apex because of its higher surface-to-volume ratio. The 10-s specimen is Fe-covered with a remaining oxide core. (The explicit motivation to avoid inherited sintering porosity applies to the separate single-crystal reference used by Zhou et al. below.)
- **Controlled input:** 50 mbar D2, 700 °C, interrupted specimens at 5, 10, 20, 30, 60 and 120 s. D2 was selected to distinguish reactant-derived deuterium from hydrogen introduced during preparation/analysis. The samples were held cryogenic before and after reaction, then transferred under UHV for APT; residual gas mass spectrometry checked that the reaction chamber contained D2. These are quenched snapshots, not operando gas or pressure traces.
- **Directly observed:** APT chemical maps/profiles show D enrichment at the reduction interface (up to about 40 at.% along a selected enriched path), a 12–14 nm Fe–O gradient, and very little D in the oxide core within the experiment’s resolution. The authors report a 20 Da signal and post hoc deconvolution of the 20–21–22 Da peaks: about 1000 ions could not be assigned to Ca and were **probably** D2O. A Ca/D2O-bearing interfacial layer appears after 5 s and clusters by 10 s; a selected cluster is about 10 nm and also enriched in Na (reported 7.14 ± 2.2 at.%). At 60 s, the tip is fully reduced and larger water/impurity clusters are segregated at its surface.
- **Not directly observed:** The paper infers that D2O clusters occupy reduction-created nanopores, but APT maps elemental/molecular-ion signals after cryogenic quench; they do not directly resolve a high-temperature gas cavity, its pressure, a pore’s connection to the free surface, a leak flux, or a fracture event. The authors explicitly say it remains unknown whether the water clusters/layers were liquid or vapor during reaction and that the confinement/pressure conditions are not understood (section 2.3). They note the 20 Da ambiguity with Ca; the deconvolution strengthens a D2O assignment but does not turn the signal into a pressure measurement.

## One close primary: Zhou et al. (2023)

X. Zhou et al., “Effect of Pore Formation on Redox-Driven Phase Transformation,” *Physical Review Letters* 130 (2023), 168001, [DOI 10.1103/PhysRevLett.130.168001](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.130.168001), [open article PDF](https://sustainable-metallurgy.com/papers/2023/2023_Re_oxidation_PRL.pdf).

The team reduced a single-crystalline cubic Fe1-xO reference at 700 °C for 2 h under 30 L/h H2, then used SEM, FIB-lamella STEM/EDS, electron tomography and 4D-STEM to map pores and local phase. They observed pores from tens of nanometers to a few micrometers, both isolated pores and larger connected channels, plus cubic Fe1-xO rims at pore surfaces embedded in adjacent regions that had become bcc Fe. The reoxidized fraction was estimated at about 5%. These are substantial structural observations of pore topology and local phase, but they are post-reduction microscopy on a single-crystal reference—not a pellet-scale, in-situ pressure or leak measurement.

Their phase-field model uses the mapped pore topology and a modeled local gas composition. It predicts about 15% water fraction in closed pores at equilibrium and pore water-vapor pressure up to 4–5 atm (about 0.5 MPa). That pressure is **inferred by simulation**. The paper compares it with phase-transformation stresses around 1 GPa and attributes crack propagation during reduction/oxidation to transformation stress rather than trapped-water pressure. Thus it supports water-mediated local reoxidation as a model consistent with observed oxide pore rims, but it does not support a claim that water pressure itself mechanically opens paths.

## Consequences for a fixed-ratio total-pressure pulse

For Fe1-xO + H2 ⇌ Fe + H2O, scaling both gas partial pressures while keeping p(H2O)/p(H2) fixed leaves the ideal-gas reaction quotient, and hence the gas-composition contribution to the equilibrium driving force, unchanged. A total-pressure pulse can still change gas density, external mass transfer, and potentially pore mechanics; those are alternative effects that must be separated from local trapped-water release. In particular, an increase in external pressure does not by itself establish a larger outward pressure gradient from an isolated pore, while a pressure decrease would require a measured pore-to-bulk differential and a connected or newly opened path to produce water release.

**Current obstruction:** neither cited experiment measures the internal pressure or its transient response, and neither couples a calibrated total-pressure pulse at fixed H2O/H2 ratio to pore connectivity, outlet-water flux and phase/fracture change in the same pellet. The proposed operation is not instantiated by these measurements. The model’s own stress comparison makes pressure-driven fracture a particularly weak premise unless a direct differential-pressure pathway is established.

**Sharp falsifier for the pressure-reopening mechanism:** under a predeclared isothermal pressure waveform at constant inlet H2O/H2 ratio, a pellet shows no pressure-correlated increase in outlet H2O release, pore connectivity, fracture or reduction/metallization rate beyond the response of an open-pore transport control, despite verified sample gas-pressure waveform and a trapped-water tracer signal. That outcome would reject pressure-driven reopening under those conditions. Conversely, a rate change alone would not establish the mechanism; it could arise from ordinary gas transport or reaction kinetics. D2O/other tracer presence alone establishes neither pressure nor mechanical actuation.
