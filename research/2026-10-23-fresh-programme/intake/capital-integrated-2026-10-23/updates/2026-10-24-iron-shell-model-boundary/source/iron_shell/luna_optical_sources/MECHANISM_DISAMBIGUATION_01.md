# Dense Fe-shell transport: pressure cracking versus redox-strain seed

**Scope:** Two-source primary-paper audit of the mechanism distinction and the closest measured conventional morphology control. This is a literature note, not an experimental result or a priority search.

## Finding

Fruehan et al.'s proposed shell fracture is a **local gas-pressure/overpressure hypothesis**: their abstract says that when equilibrium gas pressure at the Fe/FeO interface exceeds 1 atm, the dense iron shell may fracture and permit faster gaseous reduction. Hessling et al. (2024, supplied pointer, ref. 18) paraphrase this in terms of increased interfacial pH₂O, sometimes reached by higher temperature or higher external pH₂. Neither wording is evidence that cycling the Fe/FeO phase boundary creates transformation strain or cracks. Deliberately crossing that boundary at roughly fixed total pressure is a distinct redox-strain hypothesis, still untested here.

## What the two primary sources establish

- **Fruehan et al. (2005), “Final stage of reduction of iron ores by hydrogen.”** Late-stage rate loss after roughly 70–90% reduction is attributed to dense iron around wüstite grains, which makes oxygen transport through iron rate-limiting. Their analysis contrasts roughly 1 μm and 2 μm wüstite grains: the smaller grains can remain gas-reaction controlled to at least about 95% reduction, while the larger grains become diffusion controlled earlier. The abstract proposes fracture above 1 atm equilibrium gas pressure at the Fe/FeO interface. Its scale is the individual wüstite grain in ore fines, not a macroscopic shell around an entire pellet. In the accessible publisher record, no shell-thickness value, fracture-strength measurement, or directly observed pressure-induced crack event is reported. The publisher full-text link was inaccessible in this audit, so this is an evidence-access limit, not a claim that the full article contains no further measurements.

- **Fogelström & Martinsson (2025), “Effect of Heating Rate on the Non-Isothermal Hydrogen Reduction of Hematite Pellets.”** This is a measured conventional control at commercial-pellet scale: 10–12 mm pellets, reduced in pure H₂ from 450 to 900°C at 1.9, 2.5, 3.8, or 7.7°C/min. Weight loss tracks reduction, and SEM/EDS on interrupted pellets resolves morphology. The slowest ramp yielded porous iron without the dense-iron layers around residual oxide grains seen at faster ramps. At 668°C, their semi-quantitative center-grain counts of dense-iron-enclosed grains were 0, 55, 189, and 427, respectively, across increasing ramps; the residual reduction extents there were 0.9%, 2.8%, 14.7%, and 42.3%. Faster ramps increased the overall reduction rate in this protocol, so slower heating is a morphology lever with a throughput tradeoff, not an established universal optimum. This is the strongest directly measured incumbent for controlling dense-versus-porous product morphology in the two-source scope; it does not test crack creation.

## Discriminating logic and boundary

A future mechanism test would need to separate two controls: vary total pressure while holding H₂:H₂O ratio fixed to test a pressure contribution; vary H₂:H₂O across the Fe/FeO equilibrium at fixed total pressure to test phase/redox cycling. These are proposed controls, not experiments performed in the cited work. A redox waveform would then have to beat a heating schedule selected for the same reduction endpoint on net late-stage rate or H₂ use, while directly showing connected shell cracks and accounting for reoxidation, fines, and product strength.

**No advantage established:** pressure-associated fracture is already proposed by Fruehan, and thermal-history control of iron morphology is experimentally demonstrated by Fogelström & Martinsson. No evidence in these two sources establishes that redox cycling improves on either route. They provide no basis for a broad novelty or priority claim. The narrow unresolved question is whether fixed-total-pressure Fe/FeO cycling produces useful connected cracks beyond pressure and optimized thermal-history controls.
