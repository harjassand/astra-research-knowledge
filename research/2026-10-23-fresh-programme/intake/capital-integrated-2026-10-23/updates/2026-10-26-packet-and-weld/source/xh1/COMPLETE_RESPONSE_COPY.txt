## Astra Chemistry — Continuing the parallel-repair construction

### 1. The most credible starting chemistry is already experimentally established

The strongest route is to build on Niu, Hili and Liu's 2013 DNA-programmed polymerization rather than introduce another speculative reaction.

Their system uses PNA recognition adapters to position synthetic building blocks along a DNA template, followed by copper-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) to join neighboring blocks. It produced sequence-defined polymers incorporating PEG and peptide-like structures, including products formed through 16 consecutive couplings. Importantly, the researchers found that alternating AA/BB building blocks outperformed their AB alternatives, which suffered from unwanted cyclization.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PMC



This already provides three features Astra needs: a DNA-stored program, an established way to join multiple positioned building blocks, and stable triazole connections that consume the reacting azide and alkyne handles.

The new opportunity is more specific:

Can unfinished connections be repaired independently while completed connections remain inert, without permitting a wrong connection to become permanent?

Successful CuAAC bonds provide part of that operation naturally. However, the historical experiments did not establish independently addressable repeated repair of arbitrary missing junctions across long mixed-program populations.

### 2. Exact result: cross-linking creates a quadratic fidelity problem

I modeled a set of \\(L\\) intended azide–alkyne junctions. Each correct pair reacts at rate \\(k\\), while each chemically compatible but incorrect pair reacts at rate \\(\beta\\). Every reactive handle can form only one permanent bond.

If all pairs can encounter one another, the probability that every permanent bond is correct is exactly

\\[ \boxed{ P_L=\prod\_{j=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{1+j\beta/k}. } \\]

For small \\(\beta/k\\),

\\[ \log P_L\approx-\frac{\beta}{k}\frac{L(L-1)}2. \\]

This is more restrictive than ordinary independent per-step errors: the number of possible wrong partners grows with the size of the available reaction network.

Required selectivity for 99% entirely correct assembly

| Intended junctions | Maximum \\(\beta/k\\)   |
| ------------------ | ----------------------- |
| 16                 | \\(8.38\times10^{-5}\\) |
| 100                | \\(2.03\times10^{-6}\\) |
| 1,000              | \\(2.01\times10^{-8}\\) |

Exact kinetic-race calculation under complete mixing and equal rates within each class. None of these rate ratios are measured CuAAC selectivities.

This explains why achieving excellent yields on short templates would not alone establish scalability.

But this is not a universal impossibility theorem. The 2013 experiment specifically used DNA templating and macrocyclic geometry to favor adjacent partners.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PMC

&#x20;If local geometry restricts each reactive handle to a bounded number of wrong partners, the cumulative fidelity penalty can return to approximately linear scaling.



That shifts the most important chemical-design variable from reaction speed alone to the number of incorrect partners that remain physically accessible to each reactive site.

### 3. The strongest alternative: locally confined packets

I modeled assembly in isolated packets of \\(b\\) junctions, followed by joining completed packets.

This reduces incorrect partners but introduces a new failure opportunity whenever packets must be connected.

In a simplified model where each packet has all-to-all internal competition and its connector fails with probability \\(q\\),

\\[ P\_{\mathrm{total}}= \left[ \prod\_{j=0}^{b-1}(1+j\beta/k)^{-1} \right]^{L/b} (1-q)^{L/b-1}. \\]

The competing error terms behave approximately like

\\[ -\log P\_{\mathrm{total}} \approx \frac{L(b-1)}2\frac{\beta}{k} + \frac{L}{b}q. \\]

The first term grows with packet size; the second shrinks. Consequently, an intermediate packet size can outperform both individually joining every monomer and exposing the entire polymer to unrestricted reactions.

The important finding is not the specific optimum. It is that modular confinement can relax chemical-selectivity requirements, but only if the connectors between modules are themselves sufficiently reliable.

### 4. A new physical obstruction: successful downstream fragments may become harder to remove

A partially successful parallel reaction does not simply leave isolated unreacted donors.

Suppose 1,000 intended links each form with probability 60% in one reaction period. The resulting structure contains covalently connected islands separated by missing junctions.

The exact Bernoulli-run calculation gives:

| Connected run                   | Probability of at least one such run |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------ |
| 10 consecutive successful bonds | 91.5%                                |
| 12 consecutive successful bonds | 58.2%                                |
| 14 consecutive successful bonds | 26.7%                                |
| 16 consecutive successful bonds | 10.6%                                |

These are predicted structural distributions, not measured polymerization outcomes.

Why this matters: each island can retain many PNA adapters hybridized to the DNA template. An island disconnected from the desired product can nevertheless remain very strongly attached to the template.

I derived an exact clearance model for an island with \\(m\\) independent DNA-binding arms. Let each bound arm detach at rate \\(u\\), and each detached arm rebind at rate \\(a\\). The entire island escapes only when all arms are detached simultaneously.

When rebinding is impossible,

\\[ \mathbb E[T\_{\rm escape}]=\frac{H_m}{u}, \\]

where \\(H_m\\) is the \\(m\\)-th harmonic number. Escape time grows only logarithmically with island size.

With rebinding, an exact expression for the mean escape time is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[T\_{\rm escape}] = \sum\_{n=1}^{m}\frac{1}{nu} \sum\_{j=n}^{m} \frac{\binom mj}{\binom mn} \left(\frac au\right)^{j-n}. } \\]

This can grow exponentially with the number of arms.

Effect of multivalent rebinding

Hypothetical \\(u=0.3\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\); \\(a=0\\) means no rebinding, while \\(a=0.3\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\) means equal per-arm detachment and rebinding rates.

| Binding arms | No rebinding | With rebinding |
| ------------ | ------------ | -------------- |
| 2            | 5.0 s        | 6.7 s          |
| 5            | 7.6 s        | 28.4 s         |
| 10           | 9.8 s        | 6.6 min        |
| 14           | 10.8 s       | 71.3 min       |
| 20           | 12.0 s       | 2.14 days      |

I independently verified the formula against the finite-state Markov generator.

This identifies a failure that the earlier four-base single-donor clearance model could not capture: the better the system becomes at forming neighboring covalent bonds, the harder its unsuccessful disconnected products may be to remove.

It suggests that an effective reset must actively prevent DNA–PNA rebinding or physically separate the DNA scaffold, rather than relying solely on spontaneous dissociation.

A relevant precedent already exists: Brudno and colleagues demonstrated polymerase-mediated displacement of a PNA polymer from its DNA template while preserving a covalent genotype–product connection. That supports the underlying kind of operation, although not repeated clearance of the proposed macrocyclic, multi-adapter synthetic-polymer islands.&#x20;

### 5. A stronger result: resetting the entire polymer can waste quadratically more chemistry

I considered an alternative that avoids a molecular bond detector entirely.

After each parallel coupling period, retain only the fragment that is permanently connected to the original root. Clear everything beyond its first missing junction, refill the remaining positions, and repeat.

This is a form of covalent-continuity ratchet: chemistry itself determines how far the product has advanced.

It has an attractive property: a failed link cannot promote a disconnected downstream fragment into the rooted product merely because that fragment has the expected mass or sequence.

But I derived a substantial cost.

Suppose every unresolved link succeeds independently with probability \\(p\\), and every disconnected downstream island is discarded at each reset. For a chain of \\(L\\) links, the expected number of rounds until completion is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[R]=1+\frac{L(1-p)}p. } \\]

More importantly, the expected number of successful chemical bonds formed in downstream fragments that are subsequently discarded is

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[W]=\frac{(1-p)L(L-1)}2. } \\]

So the price of insisting on a single continuous rooted prefix grows quadratically in chain length.

# 199,800

By contrast, keeping all correctly formed edges in place requires only 13 independent coupling opportunities to exceed 99% complete-bond probability in the ideal no-wrong-bond model, but it lacks the same automatic rooted-continuity reset.

The important next attack was to divide the problem into independently rooted packets. For packets of \\(b\\) links, the expected wasted bonds become

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[W\_{\rm packetized}] =\frac{(1-p)L(b-1)}2. } \\]

At \\(L=1000\\), \\(p=0.6\\), packets of 20 links reduce this modeled waste from 199,800 bonds to 3,800—a 52.6-fold reduction.

The new requirement is reliable connection and eventual release of the temporary packet roots. This has not yet been physically implemented.

### 6. Two proposed reset mechanisms, and why neither can yet be assumed reliable

I pursued two ways to remove disconnected fragments while preserving the original covalent product.

Active PNA displacement is the preferred candidate. A DNA strand-displacement operation, potentially assisted by polymerase, would suppress rebinding of the temporary PNA recognition arms. Unconnected fragments could then be removed, while a separately rooted product survives and its template is restored. The Brudno precedent supports the physical direction, but not repeated recovery under Niu's CuAAC conditions.

Sacrificial DNA scaffolds offer a more radical alternative. Photocleavable DNA linkers are experimentally established, including controlled splitting of DNA strands.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed Central (PMC)

&#x20;A scaffold designed to fragment between coding positions could release whole unrooted molecular islands without requiring those islands to dissociate arm by arm.



But I found a reliability obstruction.

If every one of \\(N\\) temporary scaffold attachments must be removed, and individual cleavage succeeds independently with probability \\(c\\), then

\\[ P\_{\rm complete\ clearance}=c^N. \\]

For 1,000 attachments, even 99.9% successful cleavage per attachment yields only 36.8% probability of complete clearance under this stringent model. Achieving 99% requires per-attachment cleavage reliability above 99.99899%.

This does not rule out repeated cleavage, spontaneous release or a different scaffold topology. It does rule out assuming that demonstrated cleavage of a short DNA construct automatically provides reliable thousand-site reset.

Repeated exposure also introduces damage. For a construction requiring 668 resets, independent irreversible damage probability would need to remain below approximately \\(1.50\times10^{-5}\\) per reset to preserve 99% survival from that cause alone.

### 7. A useful negative result: catalyst pulsing is not itself repair

Suppose a chemically intact junction reacts correctly with hazard \\(kc(t)\\), and incorrectly with hazard \\(wc(t)\\), where \\(c(t)\\) is the time-varying catalyst activity.

Writing \\(A=\int c(t)\\,dt\\), the correct-product yield is exactly

\\[ Y(A)=\frac{k}{k+w}\left(1-e^{-(k+w)A}\right). \\]

Only cumulative catalyst exposure matters.

I verified numerically that dividing the same exposure into six pulses gives exactly the same outcomes as one continuous exposure in this model.

Therefore, repeated CuAAC catalyst additions establish genuine fault recovery only if something relevant changes between them: damaged substrates are replaced, trapped intermediates are repositioned, inappropriate contacts are removed, or reaction selectivity improves.

Without such a change, the simpler uninterrupted synthesis is equally informed and preferable.

### 8. Selected physical construction

The strongest research candidate is a locally confined, independently rooted packet assembler, rather than an unrestricted thousand-building-block machine or a complicated autonomous motor at every junction.

Candidate chemical architecture

1. Addressed assembly. PNA-coded macrocyclic synthetic building blocks occupy their prescribed positions on a DNA track, retaining a stable proximal product root.
2. Localized covalent commitment. CuAAC joins adjacent azide/alkyne ends, consuming successful junction handles. DNA-localized copper catalysis is one candidate for increasing intended-versus-unintended reaction selectivity; this extension is not yet demonstrated.
3. Topology-preserving reset. Actively remove disconnected islands while keeping the correctly rooted fragment chemically competent and able to regain its original positional register.
4. Independent retry. Replenish missing building blocks and repeat without undoing stable, correctly placed triazole bonds.
5. Packet fusion. Join completed packets using a separately qualified connection, then remove the temporary coding adapters and roots without altering the synthetic backbone.

The DNA-localized copper-ligand strategy has experimental support from Nian and colleagues' 2025 work, but no measured selectivity improvement has yet been established for this packet architecture.&#x20;

There is another major restriction: covalent continuity is not sequence correctness.

For 20 individually distinct monomers with alternating AA/BB connectivity and even with fixed identified endpoints, there are

\\[ (9!)^2=131{,}681{,}894{,}400 \\]

possible alternating full-length path orders. Only one is the designated order. Geometry may prohibit almost all alternatives, but length, exact mass and endpoint identification alone cannot certify the correct connectivity. This combinatorial counterexample is verified in the working model.

The decisive comparison remains Niu's direct template synthesis and He and Liu's autonomous DNA walker, which already achieved programmed multistep amine-acylation synthesis in one isothermal mixture.&#x20;

### What survives

The most substantial new results are the exact quadratic wrong-partner fidelity law, the multivalent-island clearance obstruction, and the quadratic material-waste theorem for globally rooted retries. Together they establish why unrestricted parallel reaction, whole-chain reset and ordinary catalyst replenishment each fail to solve the complete problem.

The next physically decisive interface is now narrowed to a small system: two independently programmed DNA tracks, each containing three or four chemical building blocks, with a deliberate missed middle junction. The candidate succeeds only if that missed bond can subsequently form while earlier correct junctions survive, disconnected products are removed, the rooted product retains correct registration, and cross-program wrong connections remain distinguishable and sufficiently rare.

I preserved the full mathematical derivations and executable checks:

- Scientific construction and proofs
- Executable reaction-network and clearance model

All model assertions passed, including independent finite-state and Monte Carlo checks. The mathematical findings are conditional but substantive. The proposed fault-tolerant packet assembler remains an untested chemical construction, with multivalent clearance, correct re-registration and packet fusion as its decisive physical obstacles.
