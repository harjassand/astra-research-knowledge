## What the OQE paper actually supplies

Its open quantum evolution (OQE) model is a system `S` coupled to a latent memory `M` by a time-independent joint unitary `U`. Eq. (1) interleaves `U` with a system operation `G_j` at every time step; the paper explicitly allows a unitary or measurement. The induced process tensor is defined as the map from any sequence of such system operations to the final system state. The paper says an identified OQE gives the full observable process tensor and can predict future dynamics for any operation sequence. In principle, therefore, an exact OQE can propagate a mid-circuit measurement branch while retaining `M`; a finite classical controller can select its next `G_j` from the recorded outcome. An OQE-plus-controller simulation can also preserve the memory state across a success or abort label if the specified operation leaves that memory in the continuation output.

The acquisition and validation in this paper are substantially narrower than that formal model. It trains from randomized-benchmarking records: prepare `S` in `|0>`, apply a random single-qubit Clifford sequence, append its inverse, and measure `|0>` only at the end. The experiment uses the 24 single-qubit Cliffords, with native `±X/2`, `±Y/2`, `±X`, `±Y`, and identity gates. The loss in Eq. (2) is mean-squared error on the final measured outcome for each sampled sequence and length. It is not a count table of intermediate action/outcome branches. The tested data have no mid-circuit measurement feedback, controller state, success/abort event, or loop that continues with the same hidden memory after a terminal label.

Their device is a pair of capacitive-coupled transmons used as system and environment, with exchange coupling `J ≈ 11.3 MHz`; an environment-qubit voltage bias tunes the effective coupling. The model memory dimension `χ` is selected and the `(2χ)×(2χ)` unitary is fit by BFGS from random initializations (up to 200 iterations and five restarts). For each coupling, they collect separate length ranges `k=2…40` and `k=2…60`; each length has 200 random-sequence records, with each final expectation estimated from 1,024 measurement shots. Sixty percent of the first range is training; the remainder validates, and the second range tests extrapolation. The reported example generalizes from training through length 40 to test through 60. Accuracy is much higher in the near-Markovian regime; strongly non-Markovian settings need larger `χ`, and their memory/non-Markovianity curves do not converge over `χ=1…6` for `V_bias>0.23`. At large lengths they also report a systematic measurement bias. Native Clifford duration varies (20 ns times one to three native gates, plus 100 ns idle), so their nominal time-independent `U` assumption is imperfect in the hardware run; the authors note this and report that the predictor remains useful.

The fitted OQE is physically parameterized: `U` is unitary and the memory starts in a pure state (the paper fixes a basis choice). This is an advantage if an accurate small OQE is recovered: it gives a quantum-state simulator and physical branch updates, not merely probabilities. But the paper uses nonconvex supervised fitting with a predefined `χ`; it gives no identifiability theorem, finite-shot recovery bound, or branchwise error guarantee. A low RB loss only demonstrates fit on sampled final-return observables; it is not a certificate for all measurement instruments or adaptive policies.

## Same-access comparison with controlled Hankel and PTT

The candidate controlled Hankel learner has a different measured object: action/outcome episode counts for legal intervention words, including joint history/future event probabilities. A finite full-rank Hankel core gives an observable linear realization up to similarity, without receiving latent memory states, Kraus operators, or an OQE. For a hidden quantum memory of dimension `χ`, the action/outcome predictive operators have a Liouville-space rank upper bound of `χ²`; the learned minimal rank can be smaller. This is a probability predictor, not automatically a CP or quantum-state realization. The prior controlled-PSR result (Boots, Siddiqi & Gordon) supplies this controlled Hankel/spectral ancestry, with asymptotic consistency under its rank and invertibility assumptions, not a finite-sample arbitrary-policy certificate.

A finite-state classical controller can be composed with the learned action/outcome operators. For a controller that may end in success or abort, but otherwise continues while retaining the predictive state, the completed terminal law is a finite matrix resolvent. The already written adaptive bound in `SUPPORT_ADAPTIVE_BOUND.txt` makes its boundary explicit: a fixed paid abort coin `δ`, exact abort retention, and a *joint branch-instrument* error `e` in a compatible complete quotient norm yield `||Ô−O|| ≤ (1−δ)e/[δ−(1−δ)e]` when the denominator is positive. This is conditional on acquiring that norm error; count estimates, rare success histories, rank conditioning, and process resets still cost. Terminal conditional predictions can scale as `1/p_success`. The compiled Hankel model may be signed/non-CP, so it certifies a terminal probability law only under the stated acquisition/error contract, not a physical state trajectory.

An exact identified OQE is a strong equal-access alternative for the same controller: compose its measurement CP branches with the controller and carry the `χ`-dimensional `M` through each branch; for a repeated finite-state controller, lift the OQE Liouville state together with controller state and solve the corresponding terminal/continuation linear equations. This retains physicality and memory and yields a natural comparator to the count-only resolvent. The difference is acquisition: the cited OQE paper fitted a stationary unitary from open-loop RB endpoints, not from controlled branch frequencies, and supplies no theorem that the model is recovered for the richer feedback operation class. If an OQE learner is given the same measurement/controller records and achieves the same predictive error, the controller-resolvent output is not a distinct capability of the Hankel representation.

White et al.’s PTT is the operation-complete finite-horizon comparator: it reconstructs a process tensor from informationally complete CP interventions and output-state tomography, so arbitrary outcome-dependent interventions are represented in the acquired horizon. Its full intervention basis scales exponentially with horizon (`O(d^(4k)` for `k` system slots); MPS/MPDO or finite-memory/OQE structure can reduce the representation/acquisition burden under added structure. PTT supplies a sharper finite-horizon operation model than RB-only OQE, but not by itself a stationary infinite-horizon loop compiler. Under the candidate’s actual feedback access, the fair comparison is controlled Hankel versus a fitted stationary OQE and a process-tensor learner, each trained on the same action/outcome experiment budget and tested on the same adaptive controller.

Adhikary et al.’s AISTATS 2020 HQMM learner ([primary paper](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v108/adhikary20a.html)) enforces CP outcome maps and a TP sum through Kraus/Stiefel constraints and learns from sequence likelihood. As published, its maps are indexed by outputs, not external actions. A matched action-controlled HQMM would require separate Kraus families for each action and intervention coverage; positivity is a benefit, while fitting is constrained/nonconvex and supplies no finite-sample global-recovery guarantee. Thus the strongest same-task comparisons are (i) a stationary finite-memory OQE fitted on the same branch data if its unitary model class fits, (ii) an action-controlled CP/HQMM fit on those data if physical realizability is required, and (iii) PTT for a finite horizon. None receives latent memory labels.

## Decisive matched test and status

Use a hidden-memory qubit process with a finite set of visible actions and mid-circuit outcomes. On training runs, expose only legal action/outcome episodes and known initial/re-preparation conditions; withhold the memory model from all learners. Fit (i) the count-only controlled Hankel realization and finite-controller terminal resolvent, (ii) the same-data stationary OQE fit, and (iii) finite-horizon PTT if its intervention budget fits. Evaluate on held-out finite-state controllers that select actions from observed outcomes, can terminate as success or abort, and continue with the memory preserved after abort when specified. Include a paid geometric abort coin for looping policies. Score both the unconditioned terminal law and memory-sensitive continuation following each terminal label; record success probabilities so conditional error and sample cost are explicit.

The OQE paper already establishes the formal stationary-memory simulation and RB-to-longer-sequence forecasting pieces. It does not establish acquisition of branch-conditioned, reusable success/abort controllers from its RB experiment. But an exact OQE, or PTT over the required horizon, can already support the same controller composition once acquired; ordinary finite-state-controller and resolvent composition is not a novelty claim. The unresolved value of the count-only route is whether it acquires the needed branch operators more reliably or cheaply from the exact same legal intervention data, with a validated primitive error and without losing the controller’s terminal law. This support check performed no new experiment and establishes no advantage.
