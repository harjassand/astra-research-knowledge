## Astra Chemistry — Continuing the construction

### 1. A more consequential problem than catalyst lifetime

The primary literature changes the balance between the two surviving architectures.

Joshi, Romanens and Winssinger's 2025 study already demonstrated two consecutive cycles of protected PNA extension, including experiments using a mixture of all 256 four-base PNA sequences. Its chemical mechanism provides something the speculative DNA-addressed photo-RAFT system does not yet provide: a successful incorporation consumes the previous reactive amine and introduces a protected new terminus.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed Central (PMC)



However, the same study revealed an important limitation:

- Certain terminal single-base mismatches produced approximately equal mass-spectral peak intensities for matched and mismatched products.
- In a subsequent extension experiment, the previously matched product was extended preferentially, with a reported product ratio of approximately 6:1.
- Some surface-bound primers remained persistently unreactive, with bead crowding proposed as a possible explanation.

These observations are more informative than a generic error-rate model. They show that making the correct bond and continuing to make further bonds have different selectivities. They also show that unsuccessful reactions cannot all be treated as independent, harmless retries.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.acs.org\&sz=32)

ACS Publications



This makes a more focused research target attractive: a molecular checkpoint that distinguishes a correctly extended rooted chain from an unreacted parent, a mismatched covalent product, and a hydrolyzed donor—without destroying the competent parent.

### 2. An important 2026 precedent for the checkpoint

Mitra, Mukherjee, Jurinović and Ouldridge's July 2026 work demonstrates an associative toehold: two DNA components become joined in a way that allows a fuel strand to engage an internal recognition site and release the assembled product.

The researchers explicitly identify covalent joining as a possible alternative means of creating such a recognition geometry. Their demonstrated implementation, however, involves DNA strand-displacement assembly, not an organic amide bond.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.acs.org\&sz=32)

ACS Publications



This suggests a concrete extension of Astra's current chemistry:

Use successful organic bond formation to create a durable molecular connection that can reconstruct a DNA release/advancement signal after unjoined reactants have been removed.

The distinction is that a DNA controller should not be allowed to accept mere temporary proximity as chemical success.

### 3. A strict limitation on post-reaction proofreading

I modeled a molecule undergoing repeated recognition attempts after an organic bond has formed.

Suppose a wrong covalent product has a small probability \\(a>0\\) of falsely passing a reader on each attempt. If unsuccessful readings leave the wrong product intact, and it is repeatedly tested under independent, identical conditions, its eventual probability of being accepted is

\\[ P\_{\mathrm{eventual\ false\ acceptance}} =\lim\_{n\to\infty}[1-(1-a)^n]=1. \\]

A reader that sometimes refuses a wrong product but never removes, repairs or permanently quarantines it therefore cannot provide indefinite error protection.

More importantly, even a perfect reader cannot recover an original parent after an incorrect irreversible amide has formed.

If each fresh coupling opportunity produces a correct amide with probability \\(p\\), a permanent wrong amide with probability \\(w\\), and otherwise leaves a retriable parent, the maximum proportion of original molecules capable of completing an entirely correct \\(L\\)-step synthesis is

\\[ \boxed{ Y\_{\max}=\left(\frac{p}{p+w}\right)^L. } \\]

For a hypothetical \\(p=0.6\\), \\(w=0.003\\), and \\(L=1000\\), this ceiling is only 0.682%, even with perfect downstream rejection of every incorrect product.

I verified the result using an absorbing-state model, including independent simulations of repeated molecular readouts.

This establishes a distinction between two objectives:

- Product purity: remove incorrect products from the final output.
- Manufacturing yield: preserve enough original molecules to obtain correct full-length products.

A sophisticated post-reaction reader can improve purity substantially while doing little to rescue manufacturing yield.

Consequently, the main fidelity intervention must occur before the irreversible organic bond is formed, unless Astra acquires a way to reverse or excise an incorrect bond.

### 4. Stronger construction: proofread the donor before committing the amide

I found an experimentally established mechanism that is particularly relevant.

In 2024, Mukherjee, Sengar, Cabello-García and Ouldridge demonstrated fuel-driven kinetic proofreading using nonenzymatic DNA strand displacement. The system improved discrimination between similar DNA recognition sequences during templated DNA dimerization.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed



This is a better foundation than inventing a bond detector from scratch.

The proposed Astra extension is to make the chemical coupling the final event after several reversible donor-recognition checkpoints:

Competent, unextended parent

Correct or incorrect donor binds

Fuel-driven recognition checkpoints

A donor must remain correctly docked through successive recognition steps. Dissociation returns the parent to the starting state.

Only a completed checkpoint sequence permits irreversible coupling

Recognition failure

Donor departs. Parent retains its chemically competent terminus.

Chemical commitment

An amide forms, consuming the old amine and leaving a protected new terminus.

The crucial improvement is that incorrect molecules are rejected before they permanently damage the growing chain.

### 5. Exact pre-commitment proofreading result

Suppose correct and wrong donors arrive at rates \\(\lambda_C,\lambda_W\\). Each bound donor must survive \\(n\\) forward chemical-control steps of rate \\(k\\), competing with dissociation rates \\(u_C,u_W\\).

Assume every dissociation completely resets the checkpoint while preserving the competent parent.

Then the eventual wrong-to-correct incorporation odds are exactly

\\[ \boxed{ \frac{P_W}{P_C} = \frac{\lambda_W}{\lambda_C} \left(\frac{k+u_C}{k+u_W}\right)^n. } \\]

The amplification is exponential in checkpoint count when \\(u_W>u_C\\).

I verified this against an independent continuous-time Markov model and stochastic simulation.

For an illustrative equal-arrival case with \\(k=0.15\\), \\(u_C=0.05\\), and \\(u_W=0.5\\), in s⁻¹:

| Checkpoints | Probability of entirely correct 1,000-step synthesis | Mean time per incorporation |
| ----------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| 4           | 0.0133%                                              | 1.86 min                    |
| 6           | 42.8%                                                | 3.60 min                    |
| 8           | 92.3%                                                | 6.66 min                    |
| 10          | 99.24%                                               | 12.10 min                   |
| 12          | 99.93%                                               | 21.77 min                   |

These rates are hypothetical, not measured PNA proofreading kinetics. The model excludes permanent chain damage in this comparison.

The significance is that discrimination can become strong before the wrong bond forms, without requiring an incorrect amide to be repaired afterward.

This is an application of established kinetic-proofreading theory to the proposed organic synthesis interface, not a claim to have invented kinetic proofreading.

### 6. New obstruction: the controller must forget progress when a donor leaves

There is a hidden failure in the ideal proofreading mechanism.

Suppose the correct donor passes several DNA recognition checkpoints, then dissociates before forming its amide. If the DNA controller remains in an advanced state, a subsequently arriving incorrect donor could use that progress and bypass the earlier checks.

I extended the model to include a probability \\(m\\) that each donor-dissociation event leaves the controller's current checkpoint state intact.

For the same hypothetical ten-checkpoint system:

| Checkpoint progress retained after dissociation | Full-chain correct-completion probability, 1,000 steps |
| ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| 0                                               | 99.24%                                                 |
| 0.001%                                          | 99.19%                                                 |
| 0.01%                                           | 98.69%                                                 |
| 0.1%                                            | 93.88%                                                 |
| 1%                                              | 56.78%                                                 |
| 10%                                             | 0.291%                                                 |

To maintain at least 99% full-chain correctness, the allowed progress-retention probability is approximately

\\[ \boxed{m\leq4.40\times10^{-5}} \\]

for these model parameters.

This is a result of the explicitly constructed Markov network, not a universal numerical threshold.

The new design requirement is a donor-owned checkpoint state: all progress toward chemical authorization must be lost, consumed or invalidated when the participating donor departs.

Simply attaching a multistep DNA circuit to the template is not sufficient. Its advanced state must remain coupled to the identity and continued presence of the actual reactant.

### 7. The most decisive failure: chemistry occurring before authorization

I then allowed the donor to form an irreversible amide at a small rate \\(\ell\\) before it completed all the proofreading checkpoints.

This directly challenges combining the DNA proofreader with Joshi's already activated NHS-PNA donor.

Let \\(u_i\\) be donor dissociation, \\(k\\) checkpoint progression, and \\(n\\) the number of required steps. Under complete reset after dissociation, the probability that one donor-binding event ultimately creates a bond is

\\[ q_i(n)= \frac{\ell}{u_i+\ell} \left[ 1-\left(\frac{k}{k+u_i+\ell}\right)^{n-1} \right] + \left(\frac{k}{k+u_i+\ell}\right)^{n-1} \frac{k}{k+u_i}. \\]

The first term counts premature amide formation; the second counts legitimate completion of the proofreading sequence.

An important limiting result follows:

\\[ \boxed{ \lim\_{n\rightarrow\infty} \frac{q_W(n)}{q_C(n)} = \frac{u_C+\ell}{u_W+\ell} \qquad(\ell>0). } \\]

Thus even with arbitrarily many checkpoints, the error discrimination eventually collapses toward that of the premature-coupling pathway.

Ten-checkpoint sensitivity to premature coupling

Same hypothetical rates as before; \\(\ell\\) is the unauthorized-amide hazard while a donor is bound.

| Premature coupling rate         | 1,000-step correctness |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| 0                               | 99.24%                 |
| \\(10^{-8}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\) | 99.21%                 |
| \\(10^{-7}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\) | 98.89%                 |
| \\(10^{-6}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\) | 95.78%                 |
| \\(10^{-5}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\) | 69.65%                 |

For the ten-checkpoint case, the largest permitted premature-coupling hazard to retain 99% full-chain correctness is approximately

\\[ \boxed{\ell\leq6.88\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s}^{-1}}. \\]

This is extraordinarily demanding. It is not evidence that real activated PNA esters satisfy the bound.

The important chemical conclusion is that adding a DNA proofreading circuit beside a continuously reactive NHS ester does not automatically provide proofreading of the amide bond.

The organic reactants must remain genuinely noncommittal until the necessary recognition checks are complete.

### 8. New result: a DNA reader cannot certify chemistry it cannot distinguish

I examined the strongest version of the proposed post-bond reader: it observes a DNA signal generated by material that remains attached after washing.

There is a fundamental distinction between two unsuccessful products:

1. An unjoined donor, which can leave during clearance.
2. A wrong-site covalent product, which may remain attached to the same rooted molecule as a correct product.

Washing can distinguish the first from a covalently rooted product. It cannot necessarily distinguish the second.

Let \\(P_C\\) and \\(P_W\\) denote the probability distributions of all observations available to the controller for correct and wrong covalent products. For any acceptance rule,

\\[ \boxed{ P(\text{accept}\mid C)-P(\text{accept}\mid W) \leq \operatorname{TV}(P_C,P_W), } \\]

where \\(\operatorname{TV}\\) is the total-variation distance.

If both products generate identical DNA observations, their total-variation distance is zero. No amount of downstream DNA logic or repeated testing can then distinguish them. The bond must change something chemically observable by the controller.

This is a general information-theoretic limitation, rather than a claim that all actual PNA products have identical observations.

For a hypothetical per-instruction correct-to-wrong product ratio of \\(p:w=0.6:0.003\\), achieving 99% correct sequence fidelity across 1,000 accepted instructions requires

\\[ \frac{P(\text{accept}\mid W)} {P(\text{accept}\mid C)} \leq 0.0020101. \\]

If the reader accepts 99% of correct products, it must accept fewer than 0.199% of incorrect products. That demands an observable total-variation distance of at least 0.9880 between these molecular states.

I verified the bound computationally and constructed an indistinguishable-observation counterexample.

The consequence is that an associative DNA toehold indicating covalent continuity is not automatically a certificate of correct molecular connectivity. The latter needs a separate chemical or sequence-sensitive discrimination mechanism.

### 9. Experimental chemistry supplies a revealing counterexample

The 2020 light-triggered PNA ligation study provides an important observation.

The researchers used initially unreactive furan-modified PNA, then activated it using visible-light-generated singlet oxygen. Under their tested conditions, a correctly matched PNA pair formed a covalent ligation product, while mismatched and scrambled probes could become chemically oxidized without forming the desired bond. The researchers also needed analytical methods that distinguished stable PNA duplexes from true covalent products.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.rsc.org\&sz=32)

Royal Society of Chemistry Publications



This demonstrates three different molecular states in a real chemical system:

- Recognized and correctly covalently joined.
- Chemically activated but not joined.
- Noncovalently associated without correct ligation.

A DNA controller that advances upon photoactivation would incorrectly accept the second state. A controller that advances merely because two probes remain nearby could accept the third.

The potential opportunity is to couple the fuel-driven DNA proofreading demonstrated in 2024 to localized latent chemical activation, followed by a separate covalent-continuity test.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.acs.org\&sz=32)

ACS Publications



But activated, unjoined donors must also be neutralized or removed. Once activated, they can dissociate and potentially react elsewhere. This is a second chemical-failure pathway that the DNA proofreading signal does not detect.

### 10. Exact constraint on the post-reaction clearance operation

I derived a coupled bound on clearance and product preservation.

Let an unjoined donor disappear at rate \\(u\\), and let a correctly rooted product lose its spatial registration at rate \\(v\\). For a wash lasting \\(t\\),

\\[ P(\text{donor remains})=e^{-ut}, \qquad P(\text{root retained})=e^{-vt}. \\]

A longer wash improves rejection of the unjoined donor but risks destroying positional information needed for the next instruction.

Allocating a combined 99% reliability target over 1,000 instructions to just these two independent mechanisms, using the earlier hypothetical correct/wrong production ratio of 200:1 and \\(u=0.3\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\), yields the boundary:

Required clearance interval

# 23.0 s

Maximum registration-loss rate

# 2.18 × 10⁻⁷

s⁻¹

Both figures are conditional model requirements, not measured PNA kinetics. This calculation only discriminates unjoined donors from attached products; it does not solve wrong stable covalent products, multivalent rebinding, or other chemical errors.

The result reinforces the division of labor between two mechanisms: pre-bond recognition must prevent wrong irreversible chemistry; post-bond clearance can prevent an unjoined donor from masquerading as an incorporated product.

### 11. Revised construction choice

The strongest research candidate is now a three-interlock system:

Interlock A — Before bond formation

Use fuel-driven DNA checkpoints to discriminate incoming donors. The organic bond precursor remains chemically latent, and dissociation invalidates all acquired checkpoint progress.

Interlock B — Chemical commitment

Only a qualified donor gains access to bond activation. The reaction must consume the old growing terminus or otherwise prevent repeated incorporation. Activated but unjoined material must be cleared.

Interlock C — After bond formation

Only retained, correctly identified covalent product permits the next instruction to become usable. The next reactive terminus remains protected until the previous site is closed.

The 2026 fuel-driven DNA-templating system provides a precedent for product-dependent strand-displacement control, but its authors' proposed extension to covalent joining is not an experimental demonstration of this organic-bond interlock.&#x20;

The main competing system remains ordinary staged NHS-PNA extension. It already uses real protected-terminus chemistry, avoids some duplicate-incorporation problems and has fewer speculative interfaces.&#x20;

Astra's more elaborate architecture becomes preferable only if it establishes a measurable improvement in correct full-length products per starting molecule, independent local progress, or a broader synthetic repertoire.

The decisive unresolved physical operation is therefore not another DNA circuit. It is chemical activation that remains conditional on the same correctly identified donor throughout the recognition-to-bond transition, without allowing a departing activated donor to react elsewhere.

I preserved the new indistinguishability proof, combined reliability calculation and verified model with the existing kinetic work:

The substantive advance is a tighter, experimentally informed specification of chemical fault tolerance: recognition, activation, covalent commitment and instruction advancement cannot be treated as interchangeable signals. An integrated molecular implementation remains unproven.
