## Result

The exact conjunction requested is **not acquired in these primary sources**: I found (i) electrolyte-wet separators whose through-plane ionic resistance is measured under pressure, (ii) isolated axial glass-capillary channels with measured metal deposition and varying fixed geometry, and (iii) separator mechanical/contact models. I did not find a single system that combines isolated through-plane channels, suppressed in-plane ionic bypass, a continuous ion-conducting metal-side skin, and reversible axial compression with simultaneous pore-area/conductance/deposition readout. Do not splice the separate studies into a demonstrated material.

The closest actual channel operation is Lee, Ma & Bai, “Concentration Polarization and Metal Dendrite Initiation in Isolated Electrolyte Microchannels,” *Energy & Environmental Science* (2020), DOI [10.1039/D0EE01874K](https://doi.org/10.1039/D0EE01874K); [primary arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.09523) and [full PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.09523). Glass capillaries confine the liquid path to an axial lumen, so sidewalls isolate each path from lateral electrolyte bypass. The pulled glass lumen has measured nonuniform geometry: approximately 90–180 µm diameter along a 4.5–5 mm electrode spacing; a short straight comparator is about 2 mm. With 1 M LiPF6/EC-DMC or LiPF6/triglyme, they measured galvanostatic Li deposition and voltage/Sand-time behavior. Expanding lumen geometry delayed depletion; depletion in the expanding case was seen only above 40 mA cm⁻² and the experimental Sand-time exponent was about −4.19, versus about −1.94 in the straight channel. Their coupled simulation includes an advancing metal front and cross-sectional-area variation. This is a powerful fixed-taper geometric incumbent for area-controlled local flux. The glass channel is rigid: its cross-section is fabricated, not reversibly changed by axial compression, and there is no spreading skin at the metal interface.

The closest direct pressure-to-ionic-resistance operation is Kücher, Schabenberger, Zonta & Jossen (2025), “Performance and Properties of Laboratory and Commercial Separators under Compression and Varying Temperature,” *J. Electrochem. Soc.* 172, 070512, DOI [10.1149/1945-7111/ade47b](https://doi.org/10.1149/1945-7111/ade47b); [TUM record with primary abstract](https://portal.fis.tum.de/en/publications/performance-and-properties-of-laboratory-and-commercial-separators-under-compression-and-varying-temperature/). They apply pressure to a symmetric, single-layer pouch cell and use EIS to determine ionic resistance through different separators. The abstract reports resistance decreasing under pressure for the tested glass-fiber separator, unlike microporous separators; a wet-processed separator shows a larger temperature-dependent MacMullin-number increase than tested dry-processed separators. This establishes a real, material-dependent pressure response in through-plane separator resistance. The accessible record does not provide the pressure cycle, numerical strain/area curves, in-plane conductivity, pore-mouth map, or concurrent Li-metal morphology, so it does not establish reversible channel-area control.

Cannarella et al. (2014), “Mechanical Properties of a Battery Separator under Compression and Tension,” *J. Electrochem. Soc.* 161, F3117–F3122, DOI [10.1149/2.0191411jes](https://doi.org/10.1149/2.0191411jes); [open-access article record](https://doi.org/10.1149/2.0191411jes). Their material is Celgard 3501, a 25-µm dry-stretch microporous polypropylene separator. Compression tests compare dry and fluid-immersed material; the primary abstract and body text report viscoelastic strain-rate dependence and additional poroelastic stiffening in fluid-filled pores. For their ~1-cm sample and electrolyte viscosity 4.2 mPa·s, poroelastic effects become noticeable at strain rates around or above 10⁻³ s⁻¹; slow compression lets electrolyte drain, while faster compression traps/compresses pore fluid. Electrolyte/DMC immersion softens the separator versus dry samples. This is an actual wet-compliant material and constrains the mechanism: the prescribed solid-volume porosity law cannot be assumed without drainage, rate, and creep measurements. It does not co-measure through-plane ionic conductance, channel area, or electrodeposition during compression. Direct PDF retrieval was unauthorized in this check; the publisher-indexed primary abstract and full-text extract were available.

Zhang et al. (2019), “Rethinking How External Pressure Can Suppress Dendrites in Lithium Metal Batteries,” *J. Electrochem. Soc.* 166(15), A3639–A3652, DOI [10.1149/2.0701914jes](https://doi.org/10.1149/2.0701914jes); [full primary PDF](https://escholarship.org/content/qt25q4d8zh/qt25q4d8zh.pdf). This is a mechanics calculation for a realistic rough pouch-cell pair, not a transport/area-conductance experiment. The modeled separator is 13.5 µm polypropylene; the roughness domain is 400 × 400 µm sampled at 1 µm; the starting Li film is 2 µm and grows to 18 µm. The paper uses measured load/thickness cases around 744, 1,205, and 1,585 kPa. It treats electrolyte-filled porous regions as having zero mechanical modulus, assumes liquid remains in pores, and explicitly does not solve electrical, chemical, or transport effects. Its proposed stress/creep explanations are hypotheses for pressure-suppressed plating, not an acquired microchannel conductance law.

## Interface consequence and decisive gate

A continuous ion-conducting skin is not automatically a metal-side “short circuit.” It would be a lateral ionic shunt/equalizer only if it remains continuous and hydrated across pore mouths. That creates a parallel sheet-conduction path: an opening/narrowing local channel could then draw compensating lateral flux from adjacent mouths, smoothing or relocating the pore-level current response. This is a network consequence, not a measured effect in the sources above. The metal itself is electronically conducting; it does not provide an ion-conducting lateral path through an electrolyte skin.

The decisive source/acquisition gap is therefore concrete: measure, on the same membrane/interface, (1) axial pore area under controlled reversible compression and after relaxation, (2) through-plane ionic conductance, (3) in-plane ionic sheet conductance of any skin, and (4) current/deposition at individual pore mouths under a sinusoidal or patterned constriction. Compare with a matched-mean-resistance layer without the skin. If a skin is used, its sheet resistance relative to axial pore resistance must be reported; otherwise observed leveling could be lateral ionic redistribution rather than the intended local compaction feedback. Include wetting, electrolyte drainage, load rate, creep/hysteresis, and pore-edge closure. No such matched test or material performance is acquired here.

The previous independent additive-leveling and 3D-host comparators remain preserved in `SUPPORT_PRIMARY.txt` and are not re-scored by this narrow interface check.
