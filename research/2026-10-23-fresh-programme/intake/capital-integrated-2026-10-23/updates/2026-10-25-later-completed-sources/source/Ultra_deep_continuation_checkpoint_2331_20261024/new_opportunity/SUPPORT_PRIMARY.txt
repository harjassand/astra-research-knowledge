## Bounded result

The useful positive mechanism to test is a *constitutive* conductivity response to local compaction, not merely a pre-set vertical conductivity gradient. Put an electrolyte-filled porous interlayer between a metal surface and a separator held approximately fixed. For a local column of initial thickness \(d_0\), porosity \(\epsilon_0\), and incompressible solid skeleton, shrinking the gap to \(d\) gives

\[
 \epsilon(d)=1-(1-\epsilon_0)d_0/d,\qquad
 j(d)=\Delta V\,\kappa(\epsilon(d))/d.
\]

In this local-column, fixed-voltage approximation a thinner region has *lower* current only if

\[
 \frac{d\ln\kappa}{d\ln d}=(1-\epsilon)\frac{d\ln\kappa}{d\epsilon}>1.
\]

For a Bruggeman closure \(\kappa\propto\epsilon^\alpha\), this is \(\alpha(1-\epsilon)/\epsilon>1\), or \(\epsilon<\alpha/(1+\alpha)\). Using the separator exponent \(\alpha=1.59\) and initial porosity \(\epsilon=0.508\) reported for the adjacent commercial-cell model below gives a toy sensitivity of 1.54, above threshold. That is a *calculated implication of a constitutive closure*, not an observed negative-current-feedback effect. The real test must include lateral transport, evolving pores, interfacial kinetics, concentration, and finite-wave-number perturbations. A vertical column derivative is necessary for this proposed local mechanism, not sufficient for full morphological stability.

The closest direct conventional stability analysis is Tikekar, Archer & Koch (2014), “Stability Analysis of Electrodeposition across a Structured Electrolyte with Immobilized Anions,” *J. Electrochem. Soc.* 161, A847–A855, DOI [10.1149/2.085405jes](https://doi.org/10.1149/2.085405jes); [author-posted full text](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271206319_Stability_Analysis_of_Electrodeposition_across_a_Structured_Electrolyte_with_Immobilized_Anions). Their fixed-anion fraction is uniform through a stationary separator. They assume quasi-steady deposition “i.e. the growth of the metal electrode surface does not affect the transport” (model assumptions, around p. A849), so this is not a conductivity profile that follows a moving metal boundary. In the high-current two-region model, their Eq. 38 gives

\[
\sigma/J=\frac{v_m k}{F\tanh(kL)}\left(1-\frac{\gamma v_m\mu_c C_a^f C_0 k^2}{J}\right),\quad
k_{cr}=\sqrt{\frac{J}{\gamma v_m\mu_c C_a^f C_0}}.
\]

Thus fixed anions improve stability at high overpotential and capillarity removes short-wave growth, but modes \(k<k_{cr}\) remain unstable on an unbounded interface. A finite cell can exclude these modes only if its allowed minimum wave number exceeds \(k_{cr}\); that is a size/operating-point condition, not a general all-wavelength stabilization. For their stated example, \(D_c=10^{-7}\,\mathrm{cm^2/s}\), total cation concentration 1 M, separator length 1 mm, and 10% fixed anions, the migration region begins near 0.35 mA cm\(^{-2}\) and 72 mV. This is analysis, not an experimental stability demonstration. At low overpotential they report tethered anions make little difference because mobile ions carry most current (discussion p. A854).

The closest experimental mechanical/porosity comparator is Spingler et al. (2021), “The Effects of Non-Uniform Mechanical Compression of Lithium-Ion Cells on Local Current Densities and Lithium Plating,” *J. Electrochem. Soc.* 168, 110515, DOI [10.1149/1945-7111/ac33e1](https://doi.org/10.1149/1945-7111/ac33e1); [open article record and primary abstract/methods](https://doi.org/10.1149/1945-7111/ac33e1). They measured compression-pressure relationships for harvested component stacks from a 2.1 Ah Kokam pouch cell (DEC-soaked for safety), with 1, 3, 6, and 10 MPa six-hour holds, then coupled measured compression curves to a pseudo-3D electrochemical-mechanical model. Their model feeds component porosity into effective mass transport using a Bruggeman-type relation. Reported starting separator parameters are 20 µm thickness, 50.8% porosity and exponent 1.59. Under their 1C local-compression validation, lithium deposits appeared in the compressed area; at 3 MPa they were most evident near the inner edge, while at 6 MPa deposits covered the compressed region. The model predicts local plating propensity but does not simulate deposited-lithium thickness. It is graphite-cell plating, not metal-anode growth in a separate porous interlayer. It shows that a porosity-dependent transport law is already a conventional ingredient, while it does not test the proposed fixed-skeleton \(\epsilon(d)\) or measure a reversed local current response.

A sharper pore-redistribution warning comes from Gao et al. (2025), “Impact of localized compression on lithium plating and capacity degradation in lithium-ion pouch cells,” *J. Power Sources* 652, 237657, DOI [10.1016/j.jpowsour.2025.237657](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpowsour.2025.237657); [publisher page](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0378775325014934). Their reported operation is 3C cycling with 6 MPa local compression on commercial pouch cells. The primary abstract and graphical-abstract text describe separator pore closure and lateral ion migration into open-pore regions, with a ring-shaped high-lithium/plating zone at the compressed boundary that later expands inward. This is directly relevant to whether a homogenized local \(\kappa(\epsilon)\) feedback survives spatially: pore closure can redirect flux to the boundary. Publisher full-text retrieval returned 403 in this check; methods and figures were not independently opened. This is still a graphite-cell result, not direct metal-anode/interlayer evidence.

For the stress/capillarity incumbent at a true solid-solid metal interface, Ahmad & Viswanathan (2017), “Stability of Electrodeposition at Solid-Solid Interfaces and Implications for Metal Anodes,” *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 119, 056003, [arXiv:1702.08406](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08406), derives the deformed-interface kinetic relation \(i_d/i_0=\exp[(1-\alpha_a)\Delta\mu_{e^-}/RT]\) (Eq. 4) and an electrochemical-potential shift containing curvature, deviatoric stress, and hydrostatic pressure (Eq. 5). Their continuum isotropic-elastic linear analysis shows stability depends on coupled modulus and partial-molar-volume ratios; stress can be stabilizing or destabilizing. It supplies a serious incumbent for solid interlayers, but neither an experimentally measured porous-layer constitutive law nor a universal “stiffness alone stabilizes” result.

The strongest direct experimental spatial-gradient incumbent is Pu et al. (2019), “Conductivity and lithiophilicity gradients guide lithium deposition to mitigate short circuits,” *Nature Communications* 10, 1896, DOI [10.1038/s41467-019-09932-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-09932-1); [full primary article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6478682/). Their 90.4%-porous, roughly 70-µm Ni scaffold has an Al2O3-passivated top and Au-activated bottom. The operation combines a conductivity gradient and a charge-transfer/nucleation gradient to move Li growth bottom-up inside the scaffold, away from the separator; cross-sectional microscopy shows the bottom fills first and the top remains open at early loading. They report operation across severe conditions, including cycling at 40 mAh cm⁻² and 5 mA cm⁻², and 130 cycles at 10 mA cm⁻². This is a real morphology-control incumbent, stronger than a continuum-only proposal. It is not an isolated test of a scalar conductivity gradient: Al2O3 passivation, Au lithiophilicity, three-dimensional surface area, and pore-filling geometry all change together. The gradient is fabricated in the stationary scaffold and the growing metal front advances through it; they do not claim a conductivity profile that continuously follows a planar moving interface or a universal linear-stability theorem. Their own resistance account separates electron-path resistance, Li-ion transport resistance, and charge-transfer resistance, so a conductivity-only transfer claim would omit a demonstrated competing pathway.

## What the comparison does and does not settle

An imposed positive scalar conductivity profile is not by itself a demonstrated stabilizer: the decisive object is the perturbation-to-current transfer function after the conductivity field, transport, and moving boundary are coupled. The Tikekar model changes electrochemical transport through fixed charge and concentration fields, includes capillary curvature, and leaves long waves unstable; it does not establish that a static \(\kappa(z)\) gradient alone reverses protrusion feedback. Conversely, the proposed compaction route changes \(\kappa\) *with the local gap*, so it is not reduced to that fixed-profile model.

The key unresolved positive gate is measurable and sharp: for the actual candidate electrolyte-filled interlayer, obtain \(\kappa(\epsilon, P, t)\) under sustained operating compression; then calculate or measure \(\delta j_k/\delta h_k\) for sinusoidal metal-interface perturbations across relevant \(k\), including lateral ion transport and reaction kinetics, against a matched-mean-resistance fixed-\(\kappa\) layer. The proposed mechanism survives only if the relevant current response is negative where protrusions would otherwise grow, and no pore-edge, crack, or closure-induced hot spot replaces the uniform-column instability. Compare actual metal-anode deposition morphology, not only graphite plating or conductivity. No such experiment or performance benefit is acquired here.
