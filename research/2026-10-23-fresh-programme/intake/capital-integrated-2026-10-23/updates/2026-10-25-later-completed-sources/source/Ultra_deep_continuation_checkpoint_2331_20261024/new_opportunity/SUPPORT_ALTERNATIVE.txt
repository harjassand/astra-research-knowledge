# Roughness feedback at fixed deposition: capillarity as a sign-changing countermechanism

## Status

A closed early-time linear stability criterion is derived below from the primary moving-boundary electrodeposition model of Haataja, Srolovitz, and Bocarsly. The familiar Gibbs–Thomson surface-energy term changes the sign of a Fourier roughness mode at a calculable cutoff while the mean deposition flux is held fixed. This is a primary-literature reconstruction and a challenge to transport-only stabilization, not a new mechanism, Li-battery validation, or a claim of fracture-free cycling.

## Model and derived sign criterion

Consider a single-crystal metal/electrolyte interface, with small height perturbation
\[
h(x,t)=\bar v t+A_k e^{ikx+\nu(k)t},\qquad |A_k k|\ll1.
\]
The mean cation deposition flux is fixed at a positive dimensionless value \(j\), \(0<j<1\). The dimensionless wavenumber is \(k=qL\), where \(q\) is physical wavenumber and \(L\) is the mass-transfer-layer thickness. In the fully supported electrolyte limit, bulk electric-field perturbations are screened and the cation perturbation away from the thin charge layer decays as \(e^{-kz}\).

The primary model couples Nernst–Planck ionic transport, Poisson electrostatics, Butler–Volmer charge transfer, capillarity in the interfacial overpotential, and the interfacial mass balance. Its curvature term is the Gibbs–Thomson contribution. In the paper's high-growth, thin-double-layer asymptotic regime, Eq. 14 gives
\[
\frac{\nu(k)}{V'}=
\frac{j k\left[j(1+\alpha_1)-(1-j)\widehat\gamma\,\alpha_1 k^2\right]}
     {j(1+\alpha_1)+(1-j)k},
\]
where \(V'>0\) is the growth-velocity scale, \(\alpha_1>0\) is a Butler–Volmer symmetry factor, and \(\widehat\gamma>0\) is dimensionless surface tension. With molar volume \(\Omega_m\), interfacial energy \(\gamma\), cation valence \(z\), temperature \(T\), and gas constant \(R\), the paper's capillary scale is \(\widehat\gamma=\Omega_m\gamma/(LzRT)\), subject to its stated volume convention.

For every \(k>0\), the prefactor and denominator are positive. Therefore
\[
k_0=\sqrt{\frac{j(1+\alpha_1)}
                  {(1-j)\widehat\gamma\alpha_1}}
\]
is the exact sign boundary of this reduced dispersion relation: \(0<k<k_0\) grows, \(k>k_0\) decays. The mean \(j\) is unchanged; the sinusoidal mode has zero spatial mean to first order. As \(k\to0^+\), \(\nu/V'\sim jk>0\). Thus capillarity reverses the roughness feedback at short wavelengths but cannot eliminate the long-wave instability on an unbounded interface at any finite \(\widehat\gamma\) and positive deposition flux.

A concrete dimensionless replay uses the primary paper's plotted values \(j=0.2,\alpha_1=0.5,\widehat\gamma=0.001\). They give \(k_0=27.3861\). Evaluating the same formula gives \(\nu/V'=+0.05475\) at \(k=k_0/2\), and \(-0.22347\) at \(k=2k_0\). This is a check of the source model's sign change at fixed \(j\), not a lithium parameterization.

For a finite lateral width \(W\), the permitted modes are \(k_m=2\pi mL/W\), \(m=1,2,\ldots\). Within the model's validity band, mode \(m\) is linearly damped exactly when
\[
\widehat\gamma>
\widehat\gamma_{\rm crit}(m)=
\frac{j(1+\alpha_1)}{(1-j)\alpha_1 k_m^2}.
\]
This is the finite-domain criterion that a transport-only design must face. It is not a battery-wide stability theorem: the largest resolved length, defects, grain boundaries, SEI, elastic contact, plastic flow, and fracture may lie outside the linear model.

## Reproducible sign calculation

This short calculation reproduces the dimensionless replay above.

    from math import sqrt
    j, a, g = 0.2, 0.5, 0.001
    k0 = sqrt(j*(1+a)/((1-j)*g*a))
    def growth(k):
        return j*k*(j*(1+a)-(1-j)*g*a*k*k)/(j*(1+a)+(1-j)*k)
    print(k0, growth(k0/2), growth(2*k0))

## Physical cost and decisive challenge

The passive mechanism is paid as interfacial free energy, not a sensor or feedback controller. A convex Li protrusion has the curvature chemical-potential increment \(\Delta\mu_\gamma=\Omega_m\gamma\kappa\) per mole, equivalent to an extra local equilibrium-potential magnitude \(|\Delta\eta_\gamma|=\Omega_m\gamma|\kappa|/(zF)\). At fixed current this is a local reaction penalty; its finite-amplitude mean-voltage cost is not calculated here. Required inputs are measured \(\gamma,\Omega_m,z,T,L\), the operating \(j\), and the charge-transfer slope \(\alpha_1\). The dimensionless threshold needs no nanoscale sensor. None of the Li/SEI values needed to instantiate this threshold was acquired in this task.

A decisive falsifier for this mechanism in its stated regime is a seeded small sinusoidal interface at fixed mean current, with early-time amplitude tracked for one wavelength below and one above the independently measured \(k_0\). The model predicts opposite signs of \(d\log|A_k|/dt\). Failure to see the sign reversal rejects at least one of the assumed capillary coefficient, supported-electrolyte transport regime, Butler–Volmer linearization, or isotropic single-crystal interface assumptions. Passing this early-time test would establish only the local linear mechanism, not long-cycle capacity retention or freedom from short circuits.

## Adversarial comparison

Transport alone is not a sufficient stability certificate in the classical liquid-electrolyte model. Part I explicitly includes migration, diffusion, supporting ions, charge-transfer kinetics, and curvature; it reports long-wave instability for every finite deposition flux in both supported and unsupported limits, while surface tension damps short scales. Changing the spectator-ion concentration changes the growth rate and unstable wavelength band, but does not remove the arbitrarily long unstable modes on an unbounded interface.

The strongest conventional competing mechanism is already more capable than capillarity alone. In Part II, Haataja et al. model an adsorbing additive that accumulates near protrusions and blocks metal-growth sites; their abstract reports a linearly stable growth regime over a wide deposition-flux range at sufficiently high additive concentration. This is established electrodeposition leveling prior art, not an invention here. Whether a particular additive can do this at a Li/SEI interface without parasitic chemistry or loss of coulombic efficiency is not supplied by that metal-film model.

For solid polymer electrolytes, Monroe and Newman add elastic stress to the reaction kinetics. Their linear-elastic model predicts suppression for a separator shear modulus about twice lithium's under a PEO-like Poisson ratio. That is a distinct solid/solid mechanism and a stronger Li-specific incumbent; its Hookean constitutive assumptions and the real interface's contact, yielding, creep, and fracture remain separate physical questions. This capillary calculation neither replaces nor validates that route.
