# Dynamic plating/stripping: what the scalar stability model can and cannot buy

Scope: a bounded support calculation for Li-metal surface roughening. This is not a claim that a new pulse protocol has been invented or that a material-specific stability advantage is acquired. The live question is whether a dynamic waveform changes the roughness amplification per *useful net Li* after its reversal charge, time, and polarization work are counted.

## Strong conventional dynamic incumbent

Pulse/reverse current is established. Yang et al. systematically compared pulse plating and reverse-pulse plating of Li and reported improved morphology/efficiency for short, widely separated plating pulses in diffusion-controlled conditions; adding a high-current anodic pulse improved cycling efficiency but gave no obvious morphology improvement in their tested conditions ([J. Power Sources 272 (2014), 900–908, DOI 10.1016/j.jpowsour.2014.09.026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpowsour.2014.09.026)). Wang et al.'s asymmetrical bidirectional-current (ABCM) study claimed that a short reverse-current segment can remove fresh, mild dendrites, while their in-situ visual test found large dendrites could not be dissolved; they explicitly note the visual-cell geometry differs from their coin cells. Their same-rate/same-capacity comparisons cost additional time or require increased current, and the paper identifies the reverse-current energy loss as a practical drawback ([iScience 23 (2020), 100781, DOI 10.1016/j.isci.2019.100781](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2019.100781)). A 2026 operando/cryogenic microscopy study further found that the spatial stripping path changes with electrolyte-dependent SEI thickness: some conditions strip throughout deposits, others preferentially near the current collector ([Hwang et al., Small Methods (2026), DOI 10.1002/smtd.202600002](https://doi.org/10.1002/smtd.202600002)). This makes “reverse current removes tips” a chemistry- and history-dependent premise. These are the incumbent mechanism and limitations, not a novel proposal.

Independent in-situ optical work finds a strong inherited-state effect: the initial Li plating current sets nuclei number and positions; subsequent current changes mainly alter cluster growth rate and mode. A high-current nucleation step followed by lower-current growth raised reported Li||Cu CE from 84% to 93.53% in the tested protocol, but that is a nucleation/initial-condition intervention, not a proof that arbitrary later waveform modulation erases roughness ([Guo et al., Advanced Science (2025), DOI 10.1002/advs.202414396](https://doi.org/10.1002/advs.202414396)). This makes nucleation density, local concentration, and interphase state live variables; a height-only ODE cannot adjudicate the established protocol.

## Minimal moving-boundary model with finite kinetics and capillarity

Use a metal/electrolyte interface `z=h(x,t)` with a small Fourier mode `h_k(t)e^{ikx}`. Let `j>0` denote deposition current density. For a flat electrolyte gap `L`, conductivity κ_e, and fixed remote potential, the quasistatic Laplace solution gives the mode's solution resistance per area

\[
R_{\Omega,k}=\frac{\tanh(kL)}{\kappa_e k}.
\]

This is finite for `k>0`, tends to `L/κ_e` as `kL→0`, and to `1/(κ_e k)` for `kL≫1`. A protrusion shortens the local ohmic path and creates the first-order drive `j h_k/κ_e` volts. A convex peak's capillary chemical-potential shift is represented by `η_{γ,k}=γΩ_m k²h_k/F`, with surface energy `γ` and Li molar volume `Ω_m`.

For symmetric Butler–Volmer kinetics

\[
j=2j_0\sinh\!\left(\frac{F\eta}{2RT}\right),\qquad
R_{ct}(j)=\left(\frac{\partial j}{\partial\eta}\right)^{-1}
=\frac{2RT}{F\sqrt{j^2+4j_0^2}},
\]

linearizing the kinetic and ohmic drops gives

\[
\delta j_k=\frac{j h_k/\kappa_e-\gamma\Omega_m k^2h_k/F}
{R_{ct}(j)+R_{\Omega,k}},\qquad
\dot h_k=\frac{\Omega_m}{F}\delta j_k=\sigma_k(j)h_k.
\]

Thus finite charge-transfer resistance and capillarity are present explicitly. This deliberately isolates an instantaneous, reaction/ohmic model: no concentration, SEI, or nucleation memory. It is a reduced moving-boundary stability calculation, not a full porous-electrode or solid-electrolyte model; Pritzker and Fahidy's Cu/Cu2+ linear stability analysis includes diffusion, migration, Butler–Volmer kinetics, capillary and ohmic overpotentials, and shows why those omitted fields matter ([Electrochimica Acta 37 (1992), 103–112, DOI 10.1016/0013-4686(92)80018-H](https://doi.org/10.1016/0013-4686(92)80018-H)).

The exact scalar solution is

\[
\log\frac{|h_k(T)|}{|h_k(0)|}=\int_0^T\sigma_k(j(t))\,dt.
\]

All scalar generators commute. Consequently, rearranging the same time spent at each current cannot change this model's final mode amplitude. If `R_ct` is treated as constant over a small operating window, `σ_k(j)=a_k j-b_k`, so every waveform with the same signed charge `Q_net=∫j dt` and elapsed time `T` gives exactly `log(|h_f/h_0|)=a_kQ_net-b_kT`. Pulse ordering then has zero predicted advantage. With the full BV slope, a waveform can alter the integral through its amplitude-dependent `R_ct(j)`, but that is an instantaneous nonlinear-response effect, not memory or a noncommuting control effect. It must be computed from the matched current/overpotential record; one cannot label every pulse effect “concentration recovery.”

For symmetric reverse pulses \(+J\) for \(t_+\), then \(-J\) for \(t_-\), BV has the same differential \(R_{ct}(J)\) in both phases. Define \(T=t_++t_-\), \(Q_{net}=J(t_+-t_-)\), and \(C_k=\gamma\Omega_m k^2/F\). Then the height-only model predicts exactly

\[
\log\frac{|h_f|}{|h_0|}=
\frac{\Omega_m/F}{R_{ct}(J)+R_{\Omega,k}}
\left(\frac{Q_{net}}{\kappa_e}-C_kT\right).
\]

At fixed \(Q_{net},T\), pulse order has no effect. If the parenthesis is positive (unstable mode), using \(J>|Q_{net}|/T\) lowers the BV resistance and can *increase* the positive growth exponent; if it is negative, the same kinetic change strengthens damping. Reversal is therefore not a universal roughness suppressor even before adding concentration polarization. For constant \(R_{ct}\), the waveform dependence vanishes entirely at fixed \(Q_{net},T\).

## Count the reverse charge and dissipation

With ideal Faradaic yield, useful net Li thickness is \(\Delta H_{net}=\Omega_m Q_{net}/F\), not \(\Omega_m Q_+/F\). If `ε_+` is the fraction of `Q_+` that becomes connected Li before programmed stripping, and `Q_-^Li` is the measured reverse charge that actually removes that Li, then `ΔH_useful=Ω_m(ε_+Q_+-Q_-^Li)/F`. The idealized pulse-reverse calculation below sets `ε_+=1` and `Q_-^Li=Q_-`; parasitic consumption, inactive Li, and incomplete stripping make the useful increment smaller and must be measured for the exact waveform.

Set \(r=t_-/t_+\). For a given net deposit at fixed peak \(J\), reverse current multiplies cycle time by

\[
\frac{T_{rev}}{T_{DC}}=\frac{Q_++Q_-}{Q_+-Q_-}=\frac{1+r}{1-r},
\]

and gross charge throughput per net charge by the same factor. Example: \(r=0.10\) costs 22.2% more time and 22.2% more absolute charge at the same peak current. If instead the same total time and net charge are required, the pulse magnitude must rise by \(1/\delta=1.222\), where \(\delta=(1-r)/(1+r)=0.8182\). The ohmic loss \(R_\Omega\int j^2dt\) then rises by \(1/\delta^2=1.494\), about 49%. BV activation loss also rises: for \(j=2j_0\), symmetric BV gives \(j\eta_{ct}\propto j\,\mathrm{asinh}(j/(2j_0))\), and changing to \(J=2.444j_0\) increases this term by about 43%. These costs precede any added SEI loss or heat from a real cell.

The energy comparison must use measured cell voltage: \(W/A=\int j(t)\eta_{cell}(t)dt\), separating reversible chemical storage from dissipative activation/ohmic polarization and including double-layer transients if the pulse edge is fast enough to charge them. For the symmetric BV + resistor model, the polarization work density is \(j\eta_{ct}(j)+R_\Omega j^2\), positive in either polarity. A reverse-current segment cannot be treated as free smoothing; it consumes Li inventory, has finite power and time, and may return less Li than it stripped.

## Minimum state that lets pulse order matter

To represent the experimentally invoked “replenish the interface” mechanism, at least one history variable is required. Let \(u_k\) be the concentration polarization of a Fourier mode, expressed in equivalent volts, with diffusion relaxation time \(\tau_k=(Dk^2+s g)^{-1}\). A minimal small-signal closure is

\[
\delta j_k=g\big[(\alpha_k j-\Gamma_k)h_k-u_k\big],\quad
\dot h_k=v_m\delta j_k,\quad
\dot u_k=-Dk^2u_k+s\delta j_k,
\]

where \(g=1/(R_{ct}+R_{\Omega,k})\) is finite, \(v_m=\Omega_m/F\), \(\Gamma_k=\gamma\Omega_m k^2/F\) is capillary stabilization, \(\alpha_k j h_k\) is the local field-focusing term, and \(s>0\) converts an interfacial flux perturbation into concentration polarization. For the flat-slab model above, \(\alpha_k=1/\kappa_e\); a diffusion-resolving model replaces it with its mode-dependent transfer coefficient. The state vector obeys \(\dot x=A(j)x\), with

\[
A(j)=\begin{pmatrix}
v_mg(\alpha_kj-\Gamma_k)&-v_mg\\
sg(\alpha_kj-\Gamma_k)&-Dk^2-sg
\end{pmatrix}=A_0+jA_1.
\]

For symmetric \(+J/-J\) pulses the BV slope \(g\) is equal in the two phases. Direct multiplication gives \([A_0,A_1]_{11}=-\alpha_ksv_mg^2\). Starting at \((h_0,u_0)=(h_0,0)\), plating-first then stripping versus stripping-first then plating, with phase times \(t_+,t_-\), therefore differ by

\[
\left[ e^{A(-J)t_-}e^{A(+J)t_+}-e^{A(+J)t_+}e^{A(-J)t_-}\right]_{11}
=-2J\alpha_ksv_mg^2 t_+t_-+O((t_++t_-)^3).
\]

For \(t_-=t_+/2\), equal-charge/equal-time order swap has leading roughness-per-net-thickness difference \(-2\alpha_k s g^2 t_+\). This is a concrete noncommuting, state-memory effect. It vanishes when transport polarization is absent (\(s=0\)) or becomes fast enough to eliminate \(u_k\) as an independent state; it is not licensed unless the interfacial relaxation time and sign/magnitude of this mode coupling are physically present. It is also not yet a prediction for Li: SEI heterogeneity, nucleation, stress, and 3-D morphology can dominate or change the coefficients.

A decisive same-cell falsifier is to compare the two orderings at matched \(Q_+,Q_-,T\), identical initial interface and electrolyte, then read out the same Fourier roughness spectrum and CE. Record voltage/current throughout, include the recovery interval needed to return \(u_k\) near its initial value, and compare \(\Delta\ln|h_k|/\Delta H_{useful}\) and \(W/\Delta H_{useful}\). The scalar model predicts no order difference; a reproducible one, beyond measurement error and after matched recovery, demonstrates a real internal state and supplies its operational value. The test does not require a nanoscale-feedback controller or knowledge of latent parameters in advance.

## Current conclusion

The dynamic pathway is a known, sometimes useful alternative to static interfacial stabilization. The strongest conventional explanation is concentration-polarization relaxation plus timely stripping of only young, still-reversible protrusions; primary Li experiments support these mechanisms while also showing inherited nucleation, large-dendrite irreversibility, and electrolyte-dependent stripping paths. In the finite-kinetic, capillary scalar model, a matched waveform has no ordering advantage; pulse-amplitude effects come only from the BV slope and can worsen unstable growth. A noncommuting benefit needs an acquired state (concentration polarization is the minimum candidate) and must survive the explicit gross-charge, time, CE, and polarization-work penalties. No material-specific parameters or advantage over a static layered/solid-electrolyte design are established here.
