SUPPORT TRANSPORT — electrodeposition on a rough metal/electrolyte interface

Bounded result

A fixed passive depth profile of positive ionic conductivity cannot change the sign of the linear current-focusing instability in the electroneutral, constant-concentration, quasi-static ohmic model below. It changes the positive Dirichlet-to-Neumann mobility, and therefore the growth rate, but the sign is set by the local base field minus the capillary overpotential. A different, explicitly state-responsive mechanism can reverse the sign: if interface advance compresses a porous layer and reduces its normal conductivity enough that its area-specific resistance rises, the induced conductivity perturbation adds a negative current term. I derive that term for finite layer thickness and finite Fourier wavenumber. It can stabilize long waves under a threshold, but that low-wavenumber condition alone does not certify stability at every wavelength.

1. Static passive anisotropic/layered ohmic conductor

Take a metal/electrolyte interface y=ζ(x,t), with y increasing from the metal into the electrolyte. The unperturbed interface is y=0; a fixed-potential boundary is at y=L. A positive deposition current magnitude J flows toward the metal, so define the base electrolyte potential by φ̄′(y)>0 and J=σ_n(y)φ̄′(y), where σ_n is the conductivity normal to the interface. Perturb one nonzero Fourier mode ζ=ζ_k exp(ikx+ωt). Assume electroneutrality, fixed concentrations, quasi-static Ohmic conduction, a diagonal positive conductivity tensor with normal component σ_n(y)>0 and in-plane component σ_t(y)>0, and no convection or field-dependent coefficients. The perturbation u_k(y) obeys

  (σ_n u′)′ − k² σ_t u = 0,       u(L)=0.

Define the positive surface Dirichlet-to-Neumann admittance

  Y(k) = −σ_n(0)u′(0)/u(0) > 0,

so the perturbation of the upward deposition-current magnitude is δj=σ_n(0)u′(0)=−Y(k)u(0). Its units are S m⁻². Positivity is exact: multiplying the field equation by u* and integrating gives

  Y(k)|u(0)|² = ∫₀ᴸ [σ_n|u′|² + k²σ_t|u|²]dy > 0

for a nonzero boundary value. The same proof works for a positive-definite anisotropic tensor after replacing the tangential term by its positive quadratic form.

For a homogeneous anisotropic slab, let q=|k|√(σ_t/σ_n) and p=σ_n q=|k|√(σ_nσ_t). Then

  Y(k)=p coth(qL).

In the isotropic limit this is σ|k|coth(|k|L). The long-wave limit is σ_n/L; at wavelengths short compared with the slab thickness, Y≈|k|√(σ_nσ_t). For piecewise-constant layers indexed from the electrode to the top, each of thickness d_i, define q_i=|k|√(σ_ti/σ_ni), p_i=σ_ni q_i. If Y_top is the admittance looking upward from the top of layer i, the input admittance at its bottom is

  Y_i = p_i [Y_top + p_i tanh(q_i d_i)]/[p_i + Y_top tanh(q_i d_i)].

Start with Y_top→∞ at a fixed-potential top and recurse down. At k→0 this gives the ordinary series value Y(0)=[Σ_i d_i/σ_ni]⁻¹. This is a distributed field result, not a lumped approximation.

Let r_ct>0 be the differential charge-transfer resistance per area (Ω m²). Define the deposition-driving overpotential convention so that an increase in electrolyte potential at the interface increases deposition, and write its local linear kinetic law as j=(φ_ℓ−φ_m−E_eq)/r_ct. Let Γ=γV_m/(zF)>0, where γ is interfacial energy, V_m is metal molar volume, z is charge number and F is Faraday’s constant. A convex crest ζ_k>0 has curvature +k²ζ_k and raises the equilibrium potential by Γk²ζ_k. Since φ̄′(0)=G=J/σ_n(0), the displaced-interface potential perturbation is Gζ+u(0). Therefore

  δj_k = [G−Γk²]ζ_k/[r_ct + Y(k)⁻¹].

The interface speed is V_mj/(zF), so

  ω(k) = (V_m/zF)[G−Γk²]/[r_ct+Y(k)⁻¹].

The denominator is positive. Thus the fixed-profile sign threshold is k_c=√(G/Γ): modes with 0<|k|<k_c grow, and modes above it decay. A fixed positive depth grade, tensor anisotropy, or positive r_ct can change Y and the rate, but cannot flip this sign at a fixed operating-point G. The magnitude need not always decrease under grading because grading can also change J, G, and the operating voltage; the sign statement is the robust conclusion.

Units and operating cost: [σ]=S m⁻¹, [Y]=S m⁻², [r_ct]=Ω m², [G]=V m⁻¹, [Γ]=V m, and [k_c]=m⁻¹. The dimensionless kinetic/transport crossover is r_ctY(k); it is reaction-limited for r_ctY≫1 and transport-limited for r_ctY≪1. A depth profile has area-specific ohmic resistance R_Ω=∫₀ᴸdy/σ_n(y), so at imposed mean J it costs ΔV_Ω=JR_Ω and dissipates J²R_Ω per unit area, in addition to the reaction drop Jr_ct and loss J²r_ct. Lowering conductivity is therefore a rate tradeoff, not a free stabilizer.

2. Responsive porous interlayer: finite-qd calculation

Now separate a mechanically responsive porous interlayer of base thickness d from the rest of the electrolyte. Its base normal/tangential conductivities are κ_n,κ_t>0. The rest of the stack, including its top boundary, is represented exactly by its positive admittance Y_b(k) at the upper face of this layer. The metal advances by h(x); its local remaining layer thickness is d−h. Assume, as a constitutive model, that this advance compresses a porous skeleton whose solid volume per unit projected area is conserved and that porosity changes uniformly through each local column. Let ε_p be liquid porosity and κ_n=κ_n(ε_p). The volume constraint is

  (d−h)[1−ε_p(h)] = d(1−ε_p(0)),
  dε_p/dh|₀ = −(1−ε_p)/d,
  κ_{n,h} := dκ_n/dh = −(1−ε_p)κ_{n,ε}/d.

The layer’s area-specific resistance R_h=(d−h)/κ_n(ε_p(h)) consequently has derivative

  R_h′ = [(1−ε_p)κ_{n,ε}/κ_n − 1]/κ_n.

If κ_{n,ε}>0, compression increases resistance when (1−ε_p)κ_{n,ε}/κ_n>1. This is a mechanical/constitutive feedback; it is not a fixed depth grading. It must be measured or justified for an actual material. No such law is inferred here from a generic porous-electrolyte scaling.

For a Fourier mode, q=|k|√(κ_t/κ_n), p=κ_nq, and let

  A(k)=p cosh(qd)+Y_b(k)sinh(qd),
  Y_in(k)=p[p sinh(qd)+Y_b(k)cosh(qd)]/A(k).

This is the ordinary layer input admittance from the distributed field equation κ_nu″−k²κ_tu=0. The conductivity changes by δκ_n=κ_{n,h}h throughout the layer. Since the base field is normal and uniform, this distributed coefficient perturbation contributes the flux source Gκ_{n,h}h at each face; the perturbation u still solves the homogeneous Fourier ODE in the layer. At the top, continuity of total normal flux gives κ_nu′(d)+Gκ_{n,h}h=−Y_bu(d). Solving the two-end transfer problem yields bottom flux

  δj = −Y_in u(0) + Gκ_{n,h}h[1−p/A].

The second term is the distributed conductivity-response term. It is not obtained by simply substituting a perturbed lumped resistance at arbitrary qd. With S(k)=G−Γk², the moving-interface kinetic condition is δj=[S(k)h+u(0)]/r_ct. Eliminating u(0) gives the finite-wavelength dispersion relation

  δj/h = {Y_in(k)[G−Γk²] + Gκ_{n,h}[1−p/A(k)]}/{1+r_ctY_in(k)},
  ω(k) = (V_m/zF)(δj/h).

Every denominator and Y_in is positive. For positive pore conductivity slope κ_{n,ε}>0, compression gives κ_{n,h}<0; because 0<p/A<1, the additional term is negative at every k. It can oppose the usual positive current-focusing term, unlike static grading.

An algebra-only finite-q check used dimensionless values κ_n=2, κ_t=0.5, d=0.7, Y_b=1.3, G=0.9, κ_{n,h}=−0.8, r_ct=0.4, Γ=0.02, k=1.2. Evaluating the transfer formula and independently solving the two linear top-boundary/kinetic equations gave δj/h=0.550690603128 in both routes (absolute difference 1.1×10⁻¹⁶). These are synthetic values only, not a material fit.

Check the k→0 limit. Here Y_in→[d/κ_n+Y_b(0)⁻¹]⁻¹ and [1−p/A]/Y_in→d/κ_n. Therefore

  δj/h → [−J R_h′]/[r_ct + d/κ_n + Y_b(0)⁻¹]

when capillarity is omitted at k=0. This matches differentiating the ordinary series current J=ΔV/[r_ct+R_h+Y_b(0)⁻¹]. A positive R_h′ makes this long-wave geometric response negative. Under galvanostatic operation, the global k=0 mode is removed by the current constraint; the same local formula applies to the limit of nonzero mean-zero modes, while the spatial mean adjusts separately.

For finite k, the exact sign test is

  N(k)=Y_in(k)[G−Γk²]+Gκ_{n,h}[1−p/A(k)].

A mode is stable iff N(k)<0. The small-k condition R_h′>0 only stabilizes the long-wave limit; it does not by itself prove that the entire band 0<|k|<√(G/Γ) is stable. Full linear stability requires N(k)<0 over every accessible nonzero k (or the discrete allowed set in a finite cell). At high k the capillary term dominates for Γ>0; the compaction contribution also remains nonpositive for κ_{n,h}<0. This supplies a concrete discriminating test: measure ε_p(h), κ_n(ε_p), r_ct, Y_b(k), J, and Γ at matched operating conditions and evaluate N(k) across the unstable band. Compare the required voltage and areal power with the homogeneous layer at the same J. A material whose measured R_h′≤0 fails even the long-wave gate; one with R_h′>0 but N(k)≥0 at any accessible k has not achieved robust suppression.

This candidate has explicit cost and uncertainty. The added mean ohmic loss is J²R_h per area; the voltage drop is JR_h. The mechanical compression work per area would be ∫σ_yy(h)dh, requiring a measured stress–compression law and not included in this electrochemical calculation. The model assumes that metal advance compresses a supported porous layer rather than simply displacing it, that the conductivity response is local and uniform through its thickness, that r_ct and Γ do not change with porosity, and that the layer remains connected and does not crack or lose contact. If these fail, the computed negative feedback is not a physical prediction.

A sharp all-wavelength mathematical window exists in a simple limit. Take an isotropic responsive layer with its top directly fixed-potential (Y_b→∞), so Y_in=κk coth(kd) and the distributed compaction term is Gκ_h. Define the capillary length-squared ℓ_γ=Γ/G and choose the dimensionless case ℓ_γ=d². With x=|k|d, the sign numerator divided by G is

  (κ/d) x coth(x)(1−x²) + κ_h.

For 0<x≤1, the partial-fraction identity coth(x)=1/x+2xΣ_{m≥1}(x²+π²m²)⁻¹ gives x coth(x)≤1+x²/3, hence x coth(x)(1−x²)≤(1+x²/3)(1−x²)≤1. For x≥1 the first term is already nonpositive. Therefore the sufficient condition −κ_h>κ/d makes every nonzero mode decay. This is exactly the low-k resistance condition R_h′>0. Under the skeleton-volume relation it reads (1−ε_p)κ_{n,ε}/κ_n>1. For example, an assumed constitutive law κ_n∝ε_p^m satisfies it when m>ε_p/(1−ε_p); this is only an algebraic witness, not a measured porous-electrolyte law or material recommendation. At other ℓ_γ/d² or finite Y_b, use N(k)<0 directly; do not infer all-k stability from R_h′>0 alone.

3. Governing comparators and scope

The conventional homogeneous-electrolyte comparison is not only Laplace conduction: Sundström and Bark solve a linear stability problem for a stagnant binary electrolyte with simultaneous electrode perturbations and time-dependent concentration perturbations; their analysis reports growing modes over the applied-current range considered. It is the appropriate warning against treating the fixed-concentration ohmic calculation as a full battery model. Tikekar, Archer and Koch analyze a structured electrolyte with immobilized anions and find transport changes can improve stability, through changed concentration/electric-field profiles rather than merely a positive scalar input impedance. Li et al. use Poisson–Nernst–Planck transport, retain non-electroneutrality and find anisotropic cation diffusion/local-field control can suppress dendritic growth by lowering maximum growth rates. Those are stronger anisotropic comparators and are outside the sign theorem above: their concentration and field couplings change the numerator, so this calculation does not challenge their result. For solid-solid interfaces, Chatterjee et al. show that mechanical and electrical overpotentials compete and that the mechanics contribution can materially change reaction distribution and stability. That is the closest conventional comparison for a compaction-mediated feedback; this note claims no novelty over chemo-mechanical stabilization.
