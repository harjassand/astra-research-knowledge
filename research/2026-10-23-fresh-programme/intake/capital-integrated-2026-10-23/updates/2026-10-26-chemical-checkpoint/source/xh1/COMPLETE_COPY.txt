## Astra Chemistry — Continuing construction

### A stronger historical precedent changes the research target

The literature search revealed an important predecessor that needs to be incorporated into Astra's chemistry programme.

In 2003, Rosenbaum and Liu demonstrated DNA-templated polymerization of PNA aldehyde building blocks, producing polymers containing 40 PNA units through 10 consecutive coupling reactions. Their chemistry used reductive amination rather than the activated-esters central to our recent construction.&#x20;

These results rule out treating long, sequence-directed PNA synthesis, mixed-donor polymerization, or synthetic-polymer translation as new capabilities by themselves.

The more consequential unsolved target is reliable chemical error handling: preserving a usable parent after unsuccessful reactions, suppressing irreversible incorrect incorporation, and ensuring that long programmed products remain correct rather than merely reaching the desired length.

The 2025 masked-PNA chemistry remains useful for isolating individual coupling attempts. The older reductive-amination system offers a potentially stronger starting point for reversible chemical selection.

### A new chemical obstruction: reductive amination can break the single-incorporation barrier

The reversible-imine strategy offers a way to correct errors before commitment, but it introduces a problem absent from the original NHS-ester design.

After conventional reductive amination, the original terminal primary amine becomes a secondary amine, not an amide. That secondary amine can remain chemically reactive toward aldehydes. Consequently, protecting only the incoming building block's terminal amine does not automatically prevent additional alkylation at the previous coupling site.

This is a serious distinction:

| Reaction                              | Bond after successful incorporation | Previous reactive nitrogen |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------- |
| NHS-ester amidation                   | Amide                               | Strongly deactivated       |
| Imine followed by reduction           | Secondary amine                     | Potentially reactive       |
| Imine followed by Ugi-type commitment | N-acylated amide                    | Strongly deactivated       |

Conventional reductive amination is experimentally established for DNA-templated PNA polymerization, but the proposed masked, repeated-checkpoint implementation would need to measure secondary-amine side reactions separately.&#x20;

This suggests a more powerful target: a reversible recognition stage followed by an irreversible bond-forming reaction that also consumes the old reactive terminus.

A Ugi multicomponent reaction is one possible chemical route. It can convert an imine into a stable bis-amide product while incorporating additional chemical components. Ugi chemistry has been demonstrated on DNA-conjugated substrates and proteins, including some aqueous conditions. However, those experiments do not demonstrate DNA-template-directed PNA chain growth under the necessary dilute, hybridization-compatible conditions.&#x20;

The Ugi route remains a candidate rather than the selected mechanism. Its main attraction is structural: it could combine reversible error discrimination with the same post-incorporation protection that made the NHS-ester approach fault tolerant.

### A decisive analytical limit on reversible proofreading

I constructed and executed a kinetic model with four molecular states: a template-bound imine, a detached but still covalent imine, a hydrolyzed donor still bound to the template, and a recovered parent whose donor has been removed.

The model includes imine hydrolysis, re-formation, DNA dissociation and rebinding, followed by a separate reduction pulse.

In a simplified two-state limit, let \\(r\\) be the imine-hydrolysis rate, \\(c\\) its re-formation rate while the donor remains correctly positioned, and \\(u_i\\) the donor dissociation rate for sequence class \\(i\\). When \\(r\\) is small relative to \\(c+u_i\\), the effective irreversible loss rate is

\\[ \gamma_i \approx \frac{r u_i}{c+u_i}. \\]

The resulting discrimination has a strict limit:

\\[ \boxed{\frac{\gamma\_{\rm wrong}}{\gamma\_{\rm right}} \leq \frac{u\_{\rm wrong}}{u\_{\rm right}}.} \\]

Thus reversible hydrolysis and re-formation can amplify the consequences of DNA recognition, but cannot create arbitrarily strong discrimination from weak binding differences in this model.

There is also an exact fidelity–retention trade-off for exponential effective loss rates. If a checkpoint must reduce the wrong/right product ratio by a factor \\(E\\), while retaining fraction \\(Y\\) of correct intermediates, then

\\[ \boxed{ Y=E^{-1/(m-1)}, \qquad m=\gamma\_{\rm wrong}/\gamma\_{\rm right}. } \\]

For a 1,000-fold purification:

| Loss-rate discrimination \\(m\\) | Correct intermediate retained |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| 2×                               | 0.1%                          |
| 5×                               | 17.8%                         |
| 10×                              | 46.4%                         |
| 20×                              | 69.5%                         |
| 100×                             | 93.3%                         |

This identifies a concrete requirement. To achieve roughly 1,000-fold purification while retaining at least half the correct intermediates, the wrong intermediates must disappear approximately 11 times faster than the correct ones.

The result is a conditional kinetic bound, not a claim that such discrimination has already been obtained experimentally.

### A new optimum: proofreading cannot be arbitrarily prolonged

I next included irreversible damage to the parent during repeated attempts.

If correct and incorrect imines survive a waiting period \\(t\\) with rates \\(\gamma_c\\) and \\(\gamma_w\\), while each attempt has an irreversible damage probability \\(d\\), the eventual correct-completion probability for one instruction is

\\[ F(t)= \frac{\eta q_c e^{-\gamma_c t}} {\eta q_c e^{-\gamma_c t}+\eta q_w e^{-\gamma_w t}+d}, \\]

where \\(q_c,q_w\\) are the initial correct and wrong intermediate probabilities and \\(\eta\\) is the reduction efficiency.

For \\(\gamma_w>\gamma_c>0\\), the optimal proofreading interval is

\\[ \boxed{ t^\*=\max\left( 0,\frac{1}{\gamma_w} \ln\frac{(\gamma_w-\gamma_c)\eta q_w}{\gamma_c d} \right). } \\]

This gives a physical trade-off: insufficient waiting commits incorrect products, while excessive waiting sacrifices recoverable material and exposes parents to additional irreversible failures.

I independently checked the optimum numerically.

In a more detailed four-state simulation using deliberately favorable, unmeasured binding and hydrolysis kinetics, the optimized checkpoint reaches approximately 99.64% modeled fidelity over 100 instructions, assuming indefinite retries. However, the expected time from the checkpoint and reduction phases alone is approximately 25 hours per 100-step program. With substantially faster correct-donor dissociation, the modeled fidelity collapses.

Therefore, this result identifies a potentially powerful operating regime, but does not establish that real PNA substrates occupy it.

### Another decisive failure: mass-correct does not imply sequence-correct

I also enumerated all 256 canonical four-base PNA sequences.

They fall into just 35 different base-composition classes. In particular, the 24 permutations containing one A, C, G and T have identical molecular masses.

Consequently, even a perfectly measured full-length polymer mass cannot distinguish all incorrect building-block sequences. This is important for Astra's quality criterion: confirming that the right amount of material was incorporated is not equivalent to confirming its molecular order.

The experimental comparator uses additional sequence-information strategies, so this does not invalidate that work. It does mean Astra must retain sequence-resolving and bond-site-resolving assays instead of relying on full-product mass alone.&#x20;

### Strongest construction retained

There are now two defensible directions, with different evidential strengths.

The immediate construction remains protected NHS-PNA growth with nondestructive retries. It retains the demonstrated amide chemistry and prevents repeated terminal incorporation during a donor feed, subject to the original assumptions. The central unresolved issues are permanently inactive parents, wrong stable amides, register preservation and reactor-wide waste accumulation.

The more ambitious chemical invention is reversible recognition followed by amide-forming commitment. Unlike conventional reductive amination, its final reaction must chemically deactivate the old terminus. Ugi chemistry offers one possible route, but its extra stereocentres, competing reactions and uncertain compatibility with templated PNA growth are currently major barriers. An oxidative imine-to-amide route avoids some structural problems but has no established compatible implementation here.

The next decisive chemical question is whether a genuinely matched intermediate can be retained or regenerated much more efficiently than an incorrect one while still producing a non-reactive amide linkage and preserving unsuccessful parents. That is a sharper target than simply increasing polymer length or adding another DNA motor.
