## ASTRA — New result: a collective aperture–bandwidth law

The moderate-index emitter array has a structural limitation that cannot be removed by simply increasing the coupling strength.

For \\(N\\) equally spaced emitters, the photon couples to the normalized collective excitation

\\[ u(q)=\frac1{\sqrt N}(1,e^{iq},\ldots,e^{i(N-1)q})^\mathsf T, \\]

where \\(q=\beta(\omega)a\\) changes with photon frequency.

Suppose a proposed broadband device relies on a fixed \\(r\\)-dimensional internal subspace, and demands that this subspace capture \\(u(q)\\) with amplitude error at most \\(\epsilon\\) throughout a phase interval of width \\(W\\).

I proved the necessary condition

\\[ \boxed{r\ge(1-\epsilon^2)\frac{NW}{2\pi}.} \\]

More powerfully, the exact covariance matrix

\\[ C\_{jl}=\frac{e^{i(j-l)q_c}}{N} \operatorname{sinc}\left(\frac{(j-l)W}{2}\right) \\]

gives a sharper necessary condition:

\\[ \boxed{\sum\_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_j(C)\ge1-\epsilon^2,} \\]

where the eigenvalues are ordered from largest to smallest.

This establishes a modal-complexity requirement for broadband collective coupling. It applies regardless of how the fixed internal modes are mixed or controlled, but it does not rule out a full frequency-adaptive many-mode Hamiltonian.

The sufficient counts come from an explicit fixed modal subspace, not an unconstrained numerical optimizer. I checked the error across the full phase interval using the exact covariance eigenvectors and a derivative bound between sampled frequencies.

For 33 emitters, five collective modes are both necessary and sufficient to approximate the moving phase-matched excitation to 1% in amplitude. A single collective resonance is fundamentally insufficient under this fixed-subspace model.

This identifies the next constructive requirement: a passive internal Hamiltonian that accesses several collective modes with the correct frequency dependence while keeping their free-space radiation suppressed.

## 2. A passive band-matching mechanism—and its limit

At the Bragg phase \\(q=\pi\\), a smooth nondegenerate reciprocal Bloch band must have zero slope. Therefore it cannot reproduce a photon dispersion with nonzero \\(dq/dE\\) to first order.

Moving the emitter spacing to \\(\lambda_0/6\\) gives \\(q_0=2\pi/3\\), where the reciprocal nearest-neighbour band

\\[ E\_{\rm int}(q)=\varepsilon+2J\cos q \\]

can match the Maxwell-derived guided dispersion. The explicit parameters are

\\[ J=-0.27411038B,\qquad \varepsilon=1.22588962B. \\]

Its isolated band remains positive, with minimum \\(0.67767B\\). On the tested photon interval, the maximum mismatch between this internal band and the guided photon energy is approximately \\(0.001286B\\).

However, this is not sufficient for a coherent gate. An executed optimization of the full finite-band Fano model—including all unwanted photon outputs—gave a worst-case error of approximately 5.01% over \\(E/B\in[1.495,1.505]\\). That is a failure of the tested three-control architecture, not a general impossibility theorem.

One exact obstruction is also established: at \\(q=\pi\\), the forward and backward guided coupling columns coincide. An antisymmetric two-sided incoming photon is completely decoupled. Thus no such Bragg array can implement a conditional sign on all two-sided photon inputs; it must use an encoded input channel or a different coupling geometry.

## 3. A constructive five-mode radiation-safe subspace

The next attack tested whether a static set of collective modes can simultaneously approximate the frequency-changing guided excitation and avoid free-space radiation.

For the original 33-emitter slab geometry, projecting onto the five leading Slepian modes gives:

| Property                                                  | Continuous-band upper bound |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| Guided-mode projection error                              | 0.731%                      |
| Ideal isolated-resonance radiation-induced coherent error | 0.569%                      |

The bounds use the exact angular radiation kernel and conservative analytic variation estimates between calculated frequencies. They are not yet formal interval-arithmetic certifications, and they are not photon-gate error bounds.

This establishes a useful separation: five collective modes can satisfy the modal and radiation-geometric requirements simultaneously. The remaining obstacle lies in constructing a passive Hamiltonian whose full off-shell response accesses those modes with the correct phases and amplitudes. The numerical checks show that ordinary nearest-neighbour hopping plus three scalar controls does not yet accomplish that.

## 4. All-particle stability survives even when the gate fails

For the optimized 42-emitter finite-band Hamiltonian, I derived an independent analytic lower bound on its one-particle Schur operator:

\\[ H\_{\mathrm{Schur}}\succeq1.60737B\\,I. \\]

This proves positivity without relying on a numerical eigenvalue calculation.

Consequently, combining it with the specified positive-interaction memory preserves

\\[ \boxed{\inf\operatorname{spec}H_N=-B\min(N,m)} \\]

for every finite particle number, provided each required memory excitation sector contains a zero-interaction reference state and the photon continuum contains arbitrarily soft escaping modes.

This makes the failure especially informative: exact collective storage capacity does not imply broadband coherent controllability. The bottleneck is in the frequency-dependent scattering response, not the many-body spectral lower bound.

## 5. Independent alternative: cavity-assisted scattering

I also derived the complete one-photon error requirements for a conventional single-sided cavity with one lossy conditional emitter.

For a 1% full-state coherent-error target, the simple resonant cavity architecture requires approximately

\\[ \boxed{ \frac{\kappa\_{\rm intrinsic}}{\kappa\_{\rm total}}<2.5\times10^{-5}, \qquad \frac{4g^2}{\kappa\gamma}\ge39{,}999. } \\]

The cavity eliminates the distributed-array mode-tracking problem, but replaces it with demanding intrinsic-loss and cooperativity requirements. Neither architecture currently establishes a practical advantage.

The array challenge is therefore more specific than before: realize the five-mode, radiation-safe collective subspace as a passive, frequency-adaptive scattering response, while retaining its positive spectrum and completing the Maxwell Green tensor. Selective radiance and discrete prolate concentration are established antecedents; the new results here are scoped mathematical applications and resource obstructions, not an established new law of nature.&#x20;
