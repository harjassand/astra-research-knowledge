# Closed-void powder: obtainable observations and the missing certificate interface

## Scope pinned to the Capital record

I read `technology/records/T-powder-closed-void/v0001.json` from Capital commit `0451552424b4679fa9a73faf5849ea5a4af21cf6`. The target is a **per-particle**, one-sided certificate of closed shielded void fraction (design threshold 1%, nominal 30-µm conductive particles) under per-particle composition/density/coating premises. The record says the fixed-body signed-field identity is ideal-only; no instrument, physical measurements, independent bound review, or useful-yield result exists. Its next obligation explicitly includes actual field/trajectory/material acquisition and independent void ground truth.

The missing observation is not just a scalar density for a powder lot. It is a traceable upper bound for a retained particle linking its physical mass, the relevant volume/response, closed versus accessible voids, composition/oxide/coating, and measurement uncertainty. No source below reports that full joint certificate or directly measures `Tr(alpha_phys)/m` for moving 30-µm metal powder.

## Three primary experimental interfaces

**Direct microCT of powder.** Du Plessis et al. describe and demonstrate a Ti-6Al-4V powder workflow in [MethodsX (2018), “Standard method for microCT-based additive manufacturing quality control 4: Metal powder analysis”](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mex.2018.10.021). Powder is loaded into a plastic cup (larger particles) or thin plastic tube (smaller particles), attached to a glass rod close to the X-ray source. The reported settings are 100 kV; 100 μA, 2.5-mm field and 1.5-µm voxels for the larger powder, or 280 μA with apertures, 0.7-mm field and 0.7-µm voxels for the <40-µm powder; the latter scan takes about 3 h. Strong beam-hardening correction and image segmentation are used. Their method explicitly separates closed porosity from open porosity by comparing automatic surface determinations with and without void filling; they quantitatively analyze a smaller powder with mean diameter 14 µm. This is a real route to per-particle 3D pore and shape observations, but it reports neither a traceable known-void reference series nor a one-sided error bound for 1% void certification. It also cannot report features below a voxel, and it does not measure oxygen content.

For scale only, an ideal spherical 1% void in a 30-µm sphere has equivalent diameter `30*(0.01)^(1/3) = 6.46 µm`, roughly 9 voxels across at 0.7-µm voxel pitch. That makes a single such spherical cavity a plausible CT target; it is an inference from pitch, not a validated 1%-coverage result. A 1% total void split among sub-voxel pores, thin cracks, or low-contrast inclusions is outside what this source certifies without a calibrated partial-volume/error model and a declared lower feature-size bound.

**Radiography as a possible fast screen.** Jaenisch et al. experimentally validate digital radiography against XCT in [Metals (2020), “Radiographic Visibility Limit of Pores in Metal Powder for Additive Manufacturing”](https://doi.org/10.3390/met10121634). Their MnFePSi gas-atomized powder is 100–150 µm. They put particles on double-sided tape over a stiff polymer substrate as a near-monolayer, then isolated one porous particle for a second test. A GE nanotom m 180 with an unfiltered 130-kV microfocus source (1.3-µm nominal spot), 200× magnification, and DXR500L digital detector array yielded 0.5-µm effective pixels; XCT used hundreds of projections. The paper derives a contrast-to-noise visibility criterion and notes DDA calibration is needed to suppress fixed-pattern/bad-pixel noise. Its simulations predict roughly 3–4.4-µm minimum centered spherical pores over 10–150-µm particle sizes. Physical validation, however, is on about 70 particles in the 100–150-µm range and one 137-µm particle with nine pores. At the chosen angle a 5-µm pore overlapped another pore and was missed; the authors say particle/pore orientation affects detectability. A 30-µm, 1%-void target may exceed the modeled size threshold for a single centered cavity, but that size/shape class was not experimentally validated and a single projection is not a 3D closed-porosity measurement. Their automatic feeder/conveyor and throughput are proposed future operations, not demonstrated.

**Two-fluid buoyant mass/volume.** Oskui et al. demonstrate a same-object buoyant-mass method in [PLOS ONE (2017), “Measuring the mass, volume, and density of microgram-sized objects in fluid”](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0174068). A vibrating U-shaped glass tube (1.5-mm inner diameter) moves a sample past its high-amplitude tip; a photointerrupter detects tube resonance, and the frequency excursion is calibrated to buoyant mass using a known bead. They calibrate fluid density using known NaCl solutions. A 770-µm glass bead is measured in water and salt water, giving mass `180 ± 4.4 µg`, volume `176.3 ± 4.3 nL`, and density `1.0210 ± 0.0005 g/mL`; their measured single-bead buoyant-mass spread is 120–160 ng. The same tube's servomotor-driven reservoirs switch fluid and carry the sample back and forth. This establishes a real identity-preserving two-fluid mass/volume principle for a large bead, not for conductive 30-µm powder. A 30-µm Al-density sphere is about 38 ng and its 1% mass deficit is about 0.38 ng; both are below this specific apparatus's 120-ng resolution (the comparison does not rule out a redesigned SMR).

Crucially, two-fluid weighing returns volume **excluded from the fluid that is present**, not a topology label. A connected cavity that wets fills with fluid and ceases to contribute to excluded volume. A sealed void and an open but non-wetting/trapped-gas void can give the same buoyancy signal. Changing fluid density also changes surface tension, wetting, trapped-gas stability, oxide chemistry, and possibly the coating; if those change between passes, the two equations no longer refer to the same particle state. For reactive metals the paper itself shows that fluid exposure can change sample mass over time (magnesium dissolution), so non-change for the target alloy must be measured rather than assumed.

## Exact gap / decisive test

Direct XCT currently appears the closest already demonstrated route to the named measurand, but the source evidence supports imaging and segmentation—not the record's one-sided certificate. The decisive missing operation is a **same-particle, blinded, calibrated coverage demonstration** at the target size and threshold: known closed-cavity references at and around 1%, open-pore and non-wetting/trapped-gas controls, irregular/off-center pores, oxide/coating variations, and held-out ground truth by registered higher-resolution XCT or destructive sectioning. The estimator must return a conservative upper bound or explicitly reject particles whose pore-scale/composition/contrast lies outside its validated domain. It must also report yield and failed/ambiguous particles. No such test is reported in the cited sources.

If the proposed electric response is retained as an alternative, the instrument still needs a separately validated signed-field operation that converts per-particle `Tr(alpha_phys)/m` into an observable: controlled field and gradient reversals on the same fixed or tracked particle, resolved force/trajectory sensing, and calibrated bounds for charge, position, rotation, electrode images/higher harmonics, oxide response, and mass. Existing powder CT/radiography do not establish that operation, and fluid density methods do not measure polarizability.

The two-fluid route has a more specific obstruction: without a verified operation that forces all connected pores to fill (while not changing mass, coating, or particle identity), buoyant volume cannot distinguish closed void from open-but-unwetted void. XCT/destructive sectioning on matched indexed particles is the decisive way to validate the fluid-access state; merely measuring in two liquids is insufficient.
