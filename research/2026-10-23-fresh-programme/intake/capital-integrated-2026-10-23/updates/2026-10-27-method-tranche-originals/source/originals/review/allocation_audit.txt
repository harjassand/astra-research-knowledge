INDEPENDENT ALLOCATION MODEL AUDIT

Scope: Read studies/allocation_model.py and studies/results.json only for this review. Did not inspect experiment labels, other study interpretations, or other reviews. Did not modify the model or results. This is a synthetic, finite-horizon, known-model allocation example, not an estimated Astra effect or validated research strategy.

Verdict: No material transition, posterior, hidden-state-information, or action-credit asymmetry was found for the supplied cases. The example supports a conditional demonstration that maximizing successful component gates can conflict with maximizing completed conjunctions. It does not establish practical superiority of a policy, a general interface-first rule, or an empirical gain from a named research method. Optimality on each controller's own objective is largely definitional. The heuristic's near-optimal base performance is not general.

1. Objective comparison: valid demonstration, tautological dominance.

The two dynamic programs optimize lexicographic terminal objectives (lines 79-95): gate_optimal maximizes expected successful T+I gates, breaking ties by programs; program_optimal maximizes expected completed T-and-I programs, breaking ties by gates. Therefore program_optimal cannot lose its own primary metric to an admissible same-information policy, and gate_optimal cannot lose its primary metric. The assertions at lines 196-199 are useful consistency checks against the evaluated policies, not independent scientific evidence that an allocation principle improves research. They do not independently prove global Bellman optimality.

The nontrivial model-specific content is the size and structure of the cross-objective tradeoff. In the base case, gate_optimal yields 3.447402444 successful gates and 0.447405525 completed programs; program_optimal yields 2.135629370 gates and 0.617411025 programs. Thus 0.170005500 more expected programs costs 1.311773074 expected successful gates at the same declared action budget. The bottleneck index yields 0.617325030 programs, only 0.000085995 below the program optimum in this case. These are consequences of the chosen task model and terminal utilities, not observations about Astra.

The program optimum is not an optimum for probability of any completion. For example, in one_maturation the index has probability 0.645003788625 versus 0.644747071500 for program_optimal, while the latter has more expected completed programs. Do not silently substitute these objectives.

2. Information and hidden-state parity: passes within this model.

All policies use the same known q, p_t, p_i, maturation cost, horizon, and observable candidate summaries. Policies do not receive latent Z. The posterior at lines 32-36 is the correct Bernoulli viability posterior after independent conditional failures. Theory outcomes reveal no Z information by assumption. Candidate sorting and duplicate-action removal are legitimate symmetry reductions for iid, exchangeable candidates; these summaries retain the relevant information under the stated model. This would need reconsideration for heterogeneous candidates, shared latent causes, or history-dependent action effects.

The explicit-hidden replay selects actions from observable state only, keeps latent identities aligned when sorting, and generates interface success with p_i*Z (lines 156-191). It does not supply hidden viability to the controller. This is a useful separate generative check of posterior evaluation, although it reuses the policy solver and several modeling conventions and cannot independently certify that solver's optimum or validate the world model.

3. Baseline fairness: controlled internally, limited externally.

The gate baseline is not a weak greedy strawman: it is fully adaptive, knows the same model, and its secondary objective favors completed programs. This is a charitable baseline among policies that optimize that particular gate-count utility. Both objective-optimal policies have access to the same actions and transitions. The comparison does not exploit privileged information for program_optimal.

However, gate_optimal is an explicitly different utility function, not a measured model of conventional research practice, the current Astra workflow, a proof-first rule, or a realistic competitor sharing the programme objective. Its inferiority on program count cannot establish inferiority of any such external baseline. A practical superiority claim would require credible same-objective deployable competitors and measured costs. The named gate metric counts successful gates only; failures and useful negative information are not counted as resolved research. Calling it general research productivity or resolution would overstate what the code measures.

4. Cost accounting: action parity passes; total computational-cost parity is absent.

Every preparation increment, theory attempt, interface attempt, and fallback consumes a horizon step. Preparation persists and is charged identically. No policy receives free maturation or free extra interface retries. The index's impossible-completion fallback does not stop the credit clock.

Nevertheless, paid_credits is assigned the budget unconditionally (line 141), including terminal idle steps once all gates are done. It reports a fixed charged horizon, not measured useful calls or an independently measured total resource bill. In all_easy_ample_budget, only six successful gate calls are needed but the reported paid budget is nine, with three idle steps. This is coherent if idle reservations are charged, but not evidence that all policies incur nine real research calls.

Policy computation, optimization, calibration/model acquisition, memory, coordination, implementation, and real experiment costs are absent. In particular, the supposedly cheap bottleneck index calls the exact gate-optimal DP as a fallback (line 118). It is a hybrid, not a demonstrated computationally cheap independent controller. Its formula and usual decisions may be cheap; end-to-end computational advantage is unmeasured. The same action-credit budget should not be described as equal total cost without this qualification.

5. Failure regions and limits already visible in the supplied results.

- No complete program possible: budget 3 with maturation 2 requires at least four steps for T plus preparation plus one I attempt. All policies have zero completions and tie on gates. No universal completion advantage exists.
- All easy with ample budget: all policies complete three programs and six gates. There is no strict advantage.
- Hard-theory index failure: program_optimal starts with T and obtains 0.169802500 programs; the index starts with I and obtains 0.121127500, a gap of 0.048675000, about 28.7% below the optimum. Its interface-gate count is much higher without translating into matched completions.
- Long-preparation index failure: program_optimal starts with T and obtains 0.963900000 programs; the index starts with I and obtains 0.899991000, a gap of 0.063909000, about 6.6% below the optimum.
- q_certain and short_horizon also have a T initial action for the program optimum. Initial actions alone should not be read as unique strict preferences because rounded lexicographic ties can select among equally good sequences. In particular, q_certain and short_horizon have the same expected program count for the index despite different first actions.

These cases rule out a universal interface-first heuristic and demonstrate that the base index gap is not a general guarantee. The two explicit index stress cases are honestly disclosed in code as selected after a coarse scan. The sensitivity list has no sampling denominator, representative parameter distribution, or frequency interpretation. It cannot establish how often a policy helps or fails in real work.

6. Modeling and numerical boundaries.

The assumed conjunction is central: T has no programme reward unless paired with I, except through the separately reported gate utility. All candidates have iid viability, T is independent of viability, nonviable interfaces never succeed, rates are known and stationary, preparation is deterministic and persistent, and there is no cross-candidate transfer or reusable discovery. These assumptions deliberately allow easy, individually successful theory work to be stranded. Real theory can reveal viability, have standalone value, transfer across candidates, or change future probabilities; none of those possibilities is tested here. Finite-horizon terminal scoring also discards continuation value beyond the horizon and does not value remaining partial preparation. The example illustrates proxy-objective risk under these premises rather than settling which utility real research should optimize.

The results' description of finite enumeration with binary64 arithmetic is appropriate. It is not exact rational arithmetic. Rounding each objective to 12 decimal places at policy comparisons (line 93) can select an action whose unrounded primary expectation is slightly lower; the assertion tolerance is 1e-9. This is a minor numerical qualification, not an observed material error in the supplied values. General use would also need parameter validation: the heuristic divides by p_t and p_i, so zero success rates are outside its currently robust domain. No such zero-rate case is supplied.

The correlated-repetition calculation is correct for repeated attempts on the same latent candidate: success probability approaches q, whereas independent marginal resampling would approach one. This is a useful counterexample to an iid assumption, not an estimate of research-task correlations. Independent fresh candidates have a different law and should not be pooled with repeated attempts on one candidate.
