# Astra XH2 — A new mechanism for cellulose reconstruction

## 1. Replacing two-hit molecular welding with a preassembled interface

The strongest new direction is to change how the old and newly synthesized cellulose are joined.

The previous model required a multivalent xyloglucan linker to undergo two successful enzymatic grafting reactions—one connecting it to the old structure and another to the new structure. Under a shared, randomly ending reaction window, this created a severe reliability problem.

Primary research supports an alternative that could avoid the second enzymatic reaction.

- Preassembled cellulose–xyloglucan composites: Whitney and colleagues demonstrated that high-molecular-weight xyloglucan can become incorporated into bacterial cellulose during its biosynthesis. The resulting composites contain molecular associations between cellulose and xyloglucan.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://bsapubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

  Botanical Society of America

- Polymer-scale covalent grafting: Herburger and colleagues demonstrated that Equisetum hetero-trans-β-glucanase (HTG) can covalently transfer cellulose-derived chains onto high-molecular-weight xyloglucan acceptors—not just short synthetic XXXG molecules.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

  PubMed Central (PMC)

- A directly relevant interfacial measurement: Atomic-force microscopy experiments found that xyloglucan increased the measured adhesion energy between hydrated bacterial-cellulose nanofibres by a factor of approximately 4.3. That is not a 4.3-fold increase in bulk tensile strength.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

  PubMed



These findings suggest a new candidate operation:

New cellulose containing retained xyloglucan

Biological fibre synthesis creates an associated composite before welding

Establish overlap with old fibres

One HTG-catalysed graft to old cellulose

The xyloglucan polymer is already associated with the new network

Retain the covalent graft and the original polymer association

Proposed old-to-new mechanical connection

One covalent attachment plus a mechanically effective pre-existing anchor

This is not yet a demonstrated cellulose weld. The xyloglucan association may slip, detach, or fail to transmit sustained loads. The donor cellulose may also be weakened by enzymatic cutting.

A further warning from the 2006 primary study is important: incorporating xyloglucan into bacterial cellulose affected its mechanical behaviour, including weakening under uniaxial tension in a previously studied composite. Increased microscopic adhesion therefore cannot be equated with improved bulk strength.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://bsapubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

Botanical Society of America



The advantage being investigated is precise: could pre-assembling one side of the molecular connection convert an unreliable two-reaction repair into a more reliable single-reaction operation?

## 2. New exact result: preassembly changes the scaling of repair reliability

I constructed and executed a stochastic model comparing the two chemistries.

Each small repair region has a random catalytic lifetime \\(T\\), ending when access to the repair interface disappears. All candidate links in that region share the same shutdown event.

In the old four-arm mechanism, a linker needs successful grafts to both old and new cellulose. At short reaction times, completing the necessary two reactions is a second-order event:

\\[ P\_{\rm two-hit}(T)\sim3(\eta kT)^2, \\]

where \\(k\\) is the reaction rate and \\(\eta\\) is the probability that a graft remains mechanically associated with its original donor network.

In the proposed preassembled mechanism, a candidate xyloglucan chain already has a useful mechanical association with new cellulose, with probability \\(a\\). It needs only one additional graft to old cellulose:

\\[ P\_{\rm one-hit}(T)=a\eta(1-e^{-kT}). \\]

This second formula temporarily assumes all catalytic encounters target the old cellulose; I examine that restriction below.

For an exponentially distributed shutdown time with rate \\(d=k\\), and Poisson-distributed candidate links with mean \\(N\\), the one-hit model has an exact zero-weld probability:

\\[ \boxed{ P\_{\rm fail}=\frac{1-e^{-Na\eta}}{Na\eta}. } \\]

For large \\(N\\), the failure probability decreases as \\(N^{-1}\\), compared with the \\(N^{-1/2}\\) scaling of the earlier two-hit mechanism under shared early shutdown.

This is a substantial improvement in mathematical reliability. It does not depend on assuming that cells know when welding has succeeded.

### Quantitative comparison

I retained the earlier illustrative parameters: donor retention \\(\eta=0.8\\), preassociation \\(a=0.5\\), reaction-to-shutdown rate ratio \\(k/d=1\\), and a 90% probability that all 20 independently assessed repair regions form sufficient links.

| Required successful links per region | Preassembled, one-hit candidates | Earlier four-arm, two-hit linkers |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| At least 1                           | 476                              | 14,898                            |
| At least 3                           | 1,428                            | 52,333                            |
| At least 5                           | 2,380                            | 90,137                            |

These are exact-model thresholds rounded upward, not experimental molecule requirements. Multiple links in a region are also not necessarily independent structural load paths.

The new interface reduces the candidate requirement by approximately 31–38-fold in the matched illustrative reaction-rate model.

The comparison is not automatically equal-cost: the one-hit system requires preassociated high-molecular-weight xyloglucan, which may have lower catalytic accessibility, while the older system uses synthetic multivalent acceptors.

That cost must be tested rather than ignored.

## 3. The competing-reaction problem: HTG must modify the correct cellulose

The improved construction still has a significant weakness.

The new xyloglucan polymer is initially associated with newly synthesized cellulose. But HTG does not intrinsically recognize which cellulose is old and which is new.

If HTG grafts an acceptor polymer to another fibre within the new material, that reaction may reinforce the new network without connecting it to the damaged host.

Let \\(\theta\\) denote the fraction of relevant catalytic encounters that target old cellulose. Then

\\[ P\_{\rm useful}(T) =a\eta\theta(1-e^{-kT}). \\]

The chemistry therefore needs either preferential access to the old fracture surface or enough reaction opportunities to tolerate substantial misdirection.

For the same 20-region, 90%-reliability target, \\(a=0.5\\), \\(\eta=0.8\\), and \\(k/d=1\\):

| Fraction targeting old cellulose | Candidates for at least one weld | Candidates for at least five welds |
| -------------------------------- | -------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| 100%                             | 476                              | 2,380                              |
| 75%                              | 635                              | 3,173                              |
| 50%                              | 952                              | 4,759                              |
| 25%                              | 1,904                            | 9,517                              |
| 10%                              | 4,759                            | 23,791                             |
| 1%                               | 47,582                           | 237,907                            |

The result is exact under the stated competing-reaction model.

The one-hit advantage survives considerable misdirection, but only while the other requirements remain favourable. In particular, slowing the effective reaction rate of the large preassociated xyloglucan acceptor can erase the advantage.

Against the earlier four-arm chemistry operating at \\(k/d=1\\), the one-hit mechanism with 50% preassociation and 80% donor retention loses its lower candidate-molecule requirement if its effective reaction rate falls below approximately 3.18% of the comparison rate, even with perfect old-side targeting. This threshold is model-specific and does not compare the mass or manufacture costs of the two molecular species.

This identifies a concrete interface-design choice: the old cellulose must remain sufficiently accessible to HTG while the new cellulose retains the acceptor polymer mechanically.

### A correction to the previous cellulose-accessibility estimate

The earlier research used a roughly 9% cellulose-I-versus-cellulose-II activity comparison as an adverse scenario.

That should not be treated as a measured penalty for native bacterial cellulose.

Franková and colleagues established HTG-mediated covalent modification of bacterial cellulose and found that mercerization affected bacterial cellulose Iα differently from plant-derived cellulose Iβ. The native crystalline form and processing history therefore matter.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed



The rate on the actual old–new, wet bacterial-cellulose interface remains unknown. It must be measured rather than supplied by the previous cotton-cellulose comparison.

## 4. A surprising result: two different welding chemistries can outperform either alone

The one-hit mechanism is fast, but its weakness is potentially correlated mechanical failure.

Many covalent grafts could depend on the same xyloglucan-rich region remaining attached to newly synthesized cellulose. If that entire region delaminates, adding more grafts to it will not help.

For 20 independent repair regions, a shared delamination probability of just 1% per region limits the probability that all regions retain their one-hit connections to approximately

\\[ (0.99)^{20}\approx81.8\\%, \\]

even with perfect chemical welding.

When I also include the earlier contact-establishment probability at \\(g/d=200\\), the maximum overall reliability falls to approximately 74.0%.

That is below the 90% target, regardless of one-hit linker abundance.

### A hybrid mechanism

I therefore constructed a mixed interface:

- Preassociated xyloglucan supplies fast one-hit connections.
- A smaller number of multivalent linkers form direct two-sided covalent connections.
- The two-sided connections remain functional when the xyloglucan association alone fails, as an explicit model assumption.

I retained the same random shared catalytic window, 20 repair regions, 80% donor retention, 50% old-side targeting for the one-hit reaction, and a guaranteed post-shutdown catalytic period equal to 0.2 times the mean original access period.

I optimized the number of each linker type, charging one reaction-site opportunity for a one-hit candidate and four for a four-arm candidate.

| Construction | One-hit candidates | Four-arm linkers | Modeled reaction-site opportunities | Reliability   |
| ------------ | ------------------ | ---------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------- |
| One-hit only | Unlimited          | 0                | Unlimited                           | At most 74.0% |
| Two-hit only | 0                  | 83               | 332                                 | At least 90%  |
| Hybrid       | 109                | 28               | 221                                 | 90.01%        |

Exact quadrature optimization of a synthetic failure model, with a separate 100,000-population stochastic check of the contact and reaction-window calculations. Reaction-site count is a comparison metric, not a measured molecular, energy or financial cost.

The hybrid requires approximately 33% fewer modeled reaction-site opportunities than the fully two-sided covalent strategy.

This is a useful architectural result. The faster but mechanically less secure chemistry does most of the initial work, while the slower chemistry supplies protection against the failure that fast chemistry cannot solve.

The advantage changes with the probability of shared xyloglucan detachment.

| Shared delamination probability per region | Lowest modeled reaction-site cost | Preferred composition        |
| ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| 0%                                         | 139                               | One-hit only                 |
| 0.1%                                       | 171                               | Mostly one-hit               |
| 1%                                         | 220                               | One-hit plus two-hit         |
| 3%                                         | 252                               | Substantial two-hit fraction |
| 10%                                        | 288                               | Predominantly two-hit        |
| 100%                                       | 332                               | Two-hit only                 |

The result is not proof of material superiority. The permanent strength of both reaction types remains unmeasured, and the model assumes their failure mechanisms are sufficiently distinct.

Nevertheless, it gives a concrete reason to investigate a hybrid interface rather than a single universal adhesive chemistry.

## 5. Another hidden trade-off: longer xyloglucan is not automatically better

The preassembled interface introduces a molecular-size problem.

A high-molecular-weight xyloglucan chain can have stronger or more extensive associations with cellulose. But a conventional linear xyloglucan chain has only one nonreducing terminal acceptor for the HTG grafting reaction.

At a fixed mass of xyloglucan, making the chains longer reduces the number of available molecular ends.

The 2006 bacterial-cellulose study investigated xyloglucans with molecular masses around 200 and 880 kDa, reporting substantially different incorporation and composite structures.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://bsapubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com\&sz=32)

Botanical Society of America



For equal xyloglucan mass, 880 kDa chains provide 4.4 times fewer molecular termini than 200 kDa chains.

Therefore, under equal catalytic accessibility and mechanical performance per retained chain, the longer polymer must offer more than a 4.4-fold increase in the probability of mechanically useful association to provide more one-hit welding opportunities per unit mass.

I tested the exact mass-normalized relation:

\\[ \frac{\text{effective terminal sites}}{\text{xyloglucan mass}} \propto \frac{a\\,e\\,\theta\\,S}{M}, \\]

where \\(e\\) is terminal accessibility, \\(S\\) is mechanical survival and \\(M\\) is molecular mass.

If 95% of the long polymers remain usefully associated with new cellulose:

| Useful association of 200 kDa chains | Relative opportunities from 880 kDa chains at equal mass |
| ------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5%                                   | 4.32×                                                    |
| 10%                                  | 2.16×                                                    |
| 20%                                  | 1.08×                                                    |
| 30%                                  | 0.72×                                                    |
| 50%                                  | 0.43×                                                    |

This is an analytical comparison, not measured xyloglucan association probabilities.

The result reveals a possible optimum at an intermediate polymer length: sufficiently long for useful structural association, but not so long that molecular-end scarcity and reduced enzyme accessibility dominate.

It also suggests a more specialized future macromer: a mechanically associating region together with an accessible terminal segment designed for covalent grafting.

The chemical design would need to preserve both functions. Existing studies do not demonstrate such a macromer performing regenerative cellulose welding.

### The donor-cleavage risk remains

HTG does not simply add a harmless adhesive bond. Its reaction cuts a cellulose donor chain and attaches a donor-derived fragment to the acceptor.

A labelled chemical product could therefore be detected even if the transferred fragment no longer belongs to the load-bearing old cellulose network.

Under a simple per-turnover mechanical balance, net improvement requires

\\[ \boxed{ a\theta\eta F\_{\rm gained} > p\_{\rm damage}F\_{\rm lost}. } \\]

For an illustrative \\(a=0.5\\), \\(\theta=0.5\\), and \\(\eta=0.8\\), only 20% of catalytic turnovers generate a retained, correctly directed link in this simplified model.

If more than 20% of turnovers destroy an existing connection of equal mechanical importance, the expected net contribution becomes negative.

Neither the damage probability nor the relative mechanical forces are known. This is a stress test, not evidence that HTG damages bacterial-cellulose composites.

The distinction explains why the decisive measurement must concern net wet load transfer after enzymatic treatment, not just how much covalently labelled material appears.

## 6. Can the biological system manufacture the whole interface?

The proposed construction now requires three functional inputs: fresh cellulose, appropriately structured xyloglucan and active HTG.

The biological evidence is advancing, but an important production gap remains.

A 2026 study by Karbach and colleagues reconstructed plant-inspired hemicellulose biosynthesis in engineered Yarrowia lipolytica. The investigators produced β-glucan and O-acetylated β-glucan using plant-derived glycosyltransferases and an acetyltransferase.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed



That is a relevant foothold for genetically programmed production of more complex extracellular polysaccharides.

It does not establish production and delivery of the specific high-molecular-weight xyloglucan required by this welding mechanism.

The correct chain length, substitution pattern, reactive terminus, accessibility and sustained mechanical association would still need to be acquired.

For the first mechanistic demonstration, externally supplied, characterized xyloglucan is scientifically preferable to silently assuming an additional microbial biosynthetic pathway.

That weakens the immediate claim of autonomous regeneration, but makes the missing interface testable.

An equally informed nonliving system could also incorporate xyloglucan into a cellulose patch and apply purified HTG. It might perform better, with fewer establishment and feedstock complications.

Living synthesis becomes consequential only when it supplies a capability that a fixed external repair treatment lacks—for example, replenishing missing cellulose at an inaccessible defect over repeated cycles.

Existing vascular self-healing materials already demonstrate repeated delivery of repair chemistry, and bacterial-cellulose tapes have demonstrated substantial mechanical recovery. These are the appropriate engineering competitors.&#x20;

## 7. The construction that survives

The strongest mechanism emerging from this continuation is a hybrid, contact-guided cellulose interface.

It separates work that is currently being forced into one molecular reaction:

- Biological cellulose synthesis creates replacement fibrils and physical overlap with old material.
- Preassociated xyloglucan supplies fast opportunities for HTG-mediated grafting.
- A smaller population of more permanent two-sided connections provides redundancy against wholesale loss of the xyloglucan association.
- Retained extracellular enzyme activity allows chemical maturation to continue after active cellular growth or nutrient transport declines.

The important result is that preassociation changes the fundamental kinetic order of connection formation, while the hybrid provides a mathematically supported way to address a correlated mechanical failure.

But the construction cannot yet guarantee functional reconstruction. The outstanding physical requirements are sufficient old-side enzyme access, preservation of mechanically rooted donor fragments, long-lived xyloglucan load transfer and enough correctly positioned connections to sustain actual traction.

The most decisive comparison is therefore between an old–new cellulose overlap containing xyloglucan alone, the same overlap treated with active HTG, the multivalent-linker route, and a hybrid interface. Each must be assessed at equal replacement-cellulose mass and under the same wet loading conditions. Chemically labelled products would establish molecular provenance; fracture-energy, pullout and stress-transfer measurements would establish whether those reactions actually improve function.

No laboratory operation was performed. The mathematical calculations were checked against independent numerical integration, closed-form Poisson identities, stochastic trials and a matched hybrid optimization.

The executable derivations are retained in:

- Single-hit welding, competing reactions, molecular survival and material requirements
- Hybrid welding and shared-delamination reliability test

The resulting scientific principle is more precise than the previous one:

\\[ \boxed{ \text{Biological reconstruction} = \text{new matter} + \text{preorganized mechanical contact} + \text{durable junction formation}. } \\]

The relevant new insight is that preorganizing a useful physical association can reduce the number of biochemical events needed to complete a structural function. That can make the system dramatically less vulnerable to short, unpredictable reaction windows.

The equally important qualification is that a preorganized association must actually transmit force. Otherwise the supposed one-hit weld is merely a covalent decoration of the new material.

This is now the decisive frontier for the biological branch: converting a chemically demonstrated cellulose–xyloglucan reaction into a measured, durable old-to-new structural connection, without sacrificing the original cellulose network.
