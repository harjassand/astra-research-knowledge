## Astra Chemistry — A new chemical commitment mechanism

### 1. An important limitation in the previous NCL-based writer

The previous construction assumed that native-chemical-ligation-like chemistry would make completed scaffold sites effectively inert.

That is only partly correct.

A successful native chemical ligation (NCL) consumes the site's amino group, forming an amide, but regenerates its sulfur as a free thiol. That sulfur can subsequently participate in thiol exchange, disulfide formation or temporary S-acylation. This is established NCL chemistry, not a speculative failure pathway.&#x20;

As a distributed writer completes more sites, the number of product-associated thiols increases. Consequently, the previous model—which counted disulfide interference only among unfinished sites—is incomplete.

This matters most for long synthesis programs: earlier completed products can remain chemically involved in later reactions even when their amides are correct.

### 2. A more promising experimental precedent

I found a chemical mechanism that addresses both reactive groups in a single successful modification.

Li, Wang and Gao demonstrated thiazolidino-boronate-mediated acyl transfer in 2020. Their reagent first recognises a cysteine-like 1,2-aminothiol through reversible association, then undergoes an intramolecular acyl-transfer reaction that produces a stable N-acyl thiazolidine.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

The published experiments establish unusually relevant properties:

- Reversible precursor recognition, with a reported association-rate constant around \\(5{,}000\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}\\) for the investigated reagent.
- A stable final N-acyl thiazolidine under the tested physiological conditions.
- Selective modification of N-terminal cysteines on peptides, proteins and bacteriophage.
- Sequential modification of an N-terminal cysteine and another cysteine group in a model peptide.&#x20;

Unlike ordinary NCL, the finished N-acyl thiazolidine does not leave the same free cysteine thiol available for further reactions.

This offers a materially stronger physical starting point for a write-once chemical site.

There is an important trade-off: the final product retains the thiazolidine-containing chemical structure. It is not a traceless route to arbitrary native amides, and the published studies do not demonstrate DNA-addressed, independently repairable polymer synthesis.

### 3. New theorem: correct products can progressively interfere with future writing

I extended the donor-recovery model to include the free thiols left behind by previously completed NCL sites.

At instruction \\(j\\), suppose there are \\(j\\) completed products. An incoming acyl donor can undergo its intended reaction at rate \\(k\\), or temporarily transfer its acyl group onto one of those product thiols at rate \\(b\\) per product.

The temporary sulfur-acyl intermediate can return to an available donor at rate \\(r\\), become harmlessly lost at rate \\(h\\), or cause an irreversible wrong acyl-transfer outcome at rate \\(x\\).

Under these specified reactions, with harmless donor replacement and unchanged parent competence, the exact probability of correct completion across \\(L\\) instructions is

\\[ \boxed{ P_L=\prod\_{j=0}^{L-1}\frac{1}{1+\rho_0+sj}, \qquad s=\frac{bx}{k(r+x+h)} } \\]

where \\(\rho_0\\) represents wrong-reaction competition unrelated to previously completed products.

For sufficiently small competing hazards,

\\[ \log P_L\approx-L\rho_0-\frac{sL(L-1)}2. \\]

This is a product-generated error penalty. Unlike accumulated spent donors, the interfering sulfur groups belong to material that must be retained.

Even when every unwanted sulfur-acyl intermediate reverses harmlessly, the expected number of such temporary transfers scales quadratically:

\\[ \boxed{ \mathbb E[N\_{\rm diversion}] =\frac{b}{k}\frac{L(L-1)}2. } \\]

For \\(L=1000\\) and the hypothetical ratio \\(b/k=0.01\\), that means 4,995 unnecessary sulfur-capture events during an otherwise correct synthesis.

I verified the absorbing-state equations against a separately constructed Markov generator and an independent stochastic reaction simulation.

### 4. A surprising result: initially worse chemistry can become better at scale

I compared an ordinary NCL-based writer with a chemically self-silencing alternative.

In this deliberately hypothetical comparison, the self-silencing chemistry has a higher initial wrong-reaction ratio, but creates no reactive product thiol.

| Instructions | NCL with retained product thiols | Self-silencing chemistry |
| ------------ | -------------------------------- | ------------------------ |
| 100          | 99.881%                          | 99.501%                  |
| 300          | 99.048%                          | 98.511%                  |
| 500          | 97.438%                          | 97.531%                  |
| 1,000        | 90.313%                          | 95.123%                  |

Conditional correct-completion probabilities. Assumed NCL background ratio \\(2\times10^{-6}\\), product-dependent increment \\(2\times10^{-7}\\) per completed instruction, and self-silencing chemistry ratio \\(5\times10^{-5}\\). These are not measured comparative yields.

The exact crossover occurs at 482 instructions.

This yields a useful design principle: selecting chemistry only for excellent single-step yield can be misleading. A reaction that produces a chemically quiet product may become superior for long sequences, even if its isolated selectivity is worse.

The benefit disappears if ordinary NCL product thiols are already inaccessible or harmless, and it can be offset if the new heterocyclic products obstruct later DNA recognition. Both possibilities remain experimentally unresolved.

### 5. The alternative has its own serious obstruction: off-template chemical capture

The self-silencing reaction solves product-thiol interference, but that does not make it suitable for a 256-donor mixture.

In the 2020 chemistry, the cysteine-like site can associate with the acyl reagent chemically, independently of DNA recognition. An incorrect donor may therefore form a reversible thiazolidine intermediate even when its DNA barcode does not match.

I modeled correct and wrong precursor formation at rates \\(\alpha_C,\alpha_W\\), followed by chemical commitment \\(c_C,c_W\\) or reversal \\(r_C,r_W\\).

The eventual wrong-to-correct product ratio is

\\[ \boxed{ \frac{P_W}{P_C} = \frac{\alpha_W}{\alpha_C} \frac{c_W/(c_W+r_W)}{c_C/(c_C+r_C)}. } \\]

This is exact for the specified renewal model.

If chemical commitment and reversal are identical for correct and wrong precursors, DNA dissociation after precursor formation adds no proofreading.

For a hypothetical pool of 255 incorrect donors, each at 1 nM, using the published precursor-association rate as an illustrative common rate, the total incorrect capture hazard is \\(0.001275\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\).

Assuming correct capture at \\(0.1\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\), correct precursor commitment probability 50%, and the published pH 6 conversion half-time as an approximate commitment time scale, 99% correctness over 1,000 instructions would require wrong precursors to reverse at approximately 1.63 s⁻¹ or faster.

That is more than 2,500 times the assumed correct-precursor reversal rate.

No experimental result establishes that separation for the proposed DNA-addressed substrates.

Consequently, the new chemistry is not ready for unrestricted simultaneous exposure to 256 donor identities. It needs additional prevention of incorrect precursor capture or a genuinely demonstrated difference in chemical commitment.

### 6. A second recovery mechanism also has a strict limit

I tested a less structurally intrusive alternative:

1. Reversibly mask unfinished 1,2-aminothiol sites using 2-formylphenylboronic acid.
2. Permanently cap the free thiols belonging to completed NCL products.
3. Restore the protected unfinished sites for another writing attempt.

The 2016 work of Faustino and colleagues experimentally supports selective, reversible protection of N-terminal cysteines in the presence of other thiols.&#x20;

[image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov\&sz=32)

PubMed



However, the protective intermediate can temporarily dissociate while the permanent thiol-capping reagent is present.

An exact two-state kinetic calculation shows that, under illustrative masking and capping rates, keeping unintended capping below \\(5\times10^{-6}\\) per unfinished site requires spontaneous mask dissociation below approximately \\(8.60\times10^{-7}\\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}\\).

That corresponds to a protected-state mean lifetime exceeding 13 days in the absence of a deliberately triggered release.

This does not disprove the method; a stable but chemically removable protecting group might satisfy it. It establishes that a high average protected fraction is not sufficient when even rare openings can cause irreversible damage.

### 7. Research decision and next physical discriminator

The strongest construction now has two candidate chemistries:

- Self-silencing 1,2-aminothiol modification: use a stable N-acyl thiazolidine, or the experimentally established cyclopropenone–cysteine reaction that forms a stable thiazepanone ring. The cyclopropenone alternative has demonstrated aqueous, selective protein modification with diverse attached groups, but its recoverability and address-specific operation remain unestablished.&#x20;

  [image](https://www.google.com/s2/favicons?domain=https://pubs.acs.org\&sz=32)

  ACS Publications

- Protected NHS-PNA amidation: retain this as the strongest directly demonstrated option for sequential, regenerable PNA growth. It does not introduce a new product thiol and avoids the bulky cysteine-derived heterocycle.

For the more ambitious distributed writer, the next decisive attack is whether an incorrect, chemically captured precursor can return to the original competent site before irreversible acyl transfer, without requiring that its DNA recognition strand remain permanently attached.

The minimum revealing model has two chemically distinguishable cysteine-like sites, a correctly addressed acyl donor, an incorrectly addressed competitor and a previously completed product. It must separately establish precursor reversal, correct permanent attachment, product-sulfur inactivity and removal of any DNA guide attached to the final product.

The strongest surviving principle is:

Successful chemical writing must create a product that no longer competes with future writing, while unsuccessful recognition must not irreversibly consume the original site.

The new analysis establishes that these are separate requirements, and that resolving one without the other can simply relocate the failure.
