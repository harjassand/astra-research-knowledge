# Opening: use the EMS drive coil as a melt-state sensor

**Independence boundary.** This is an independent upstream candidate, not a continuation of the frozen imaging unit. My prior exposure includes battery control, physical-science source audits, graph/CTMC acquisition arguments, and a metallurgical powder/iron imaging audit. That history narrows independence; this is not a cold-start claim.

## Question and candidate operation

Can the existing rotating-field induction coil in electromagnetic stirring (EMS) also provide a useful, non-contact signal of when a solidifying aluminum charge has entered the short mushy-state interval in which rapid clockwise/counterclockwise (CW/CCW) reversals fragment dendrites, allowing a per-cast reversal controller to maintain fine, homogeneous grains as cooling conditions vary?

The proposed readout is the drive coil's complex terminal impedance, $Z(t)=V(t)/I(t)$, measured from isolated coil-terminal voltage/current waveforms during the normal EMS sequence (and, only if necessary, brief low-amplitude diagnostic intervals). The controller outputs a binary "inside calibrated fragmentation window" state and applies the already demonstrated CW/CCW reversal period while that state holds. It stops outside the window. This proposal does **not** assume that the drive signal is already telemetered: the apparatus must expose synchronized terminal voltage and current, or the measurement interface must be added. The state estimate is only a batch-scale scalar; it is not a spatial image or a defect map.

## Literal access and resource contract

The target platform is a 7150 aluminum charge in the three-phase-stator arrangement used by Li et al.: the field runs at 210 Hz with 198 V and 19.5 A; exchanging two AC phases reverses CW/CCW rotation; a timer/relay can set the reversal interval. In their 1.55 kg, 90 mm-diameter crucible test, the melt began at 650 °C and EMS ended at 610 °C. A candidate implementation observes only drive-coil voltage/current (plus the controller clock); it changes only field direction/timing. It consumes the same alloy charge and EMS electrical energy, which must be metered. No internal probe, tomography array, or assumed commercial telemetry is part of the input contract.

During development, thermocouples and interrupted/quench sections can label the transition stage, but they are withheld from the controller at evaluation. The output is a per-cast sequence of reversal/stop commands. The measured endpoint is radial grain-size distribution and coarse-grain tail/segregation in blinded metallographic sections, plus integrated EMS energy. This is a feasibility test, not a claim of a deployable controller.

## Why this is worth a gate, and why it may fail

Li et al. directly demonstrate that reversal timing matters in 7150 Al: their fixed 0.5 s interval produced about 140 μm average grains; 0.3 s gave about 270 μm; 0.8–1.5 s changed the mean less but produced coarse outliers; continuous one-way stirring produced a roughly 280 μm mean and a maximum grain near 1120 μm. Their inverter and phase-reversal interface are real, but they chose the interval with a timer and controlled the endpoint using sample temperature. The paper therefore supplies a strong fixed-recipe incumbent, not evidence for impedance feedback.

The physical sensor hypothesis has only adjacent evidence. Ma et al. measured inductance/phase changes across a liquid-solid transition with induction coils and reconstructed solidified-shell thickness from a 16-coil magnetic-induction-tomography array. Their real-time experiment used a low-melting eutectic wood alloy with a narrow, couple-degree transition; the paper does not demonstrate a single actuator coil estimating mushy fraction in hot 7150 Al. It notes the ill-posed conductivity inversion, small received signals, and need for high-temperature shielding in an industrial installation. Li et al. motivate possible coupling because primary solid and liquid differ in electrical resistivity, but they did not measure coil impedance as a phase-fraction sensor.

The control problem is also regime-dependent. Zimmermann et al.'s Al-10 wt% Cu experiments found that moderate stirring under laminar flow did not refine grains; strong forced flow improved refinement only in the investigated range, and the benefit vanished at higher solidification velocities. So a temperature/state-trigger alone cannot be presumed to encode the flow and transport regime that makes fragments survive. A one-port impedance signal is also a spatially weighted aggregate; the candidate is not intended to recover asymmetric shell geometry or local defects.

## Decisive matched test

First, test observability before closing the loop. On repeated 7150 casts spanning two predeclared cooling schedules, record coil-terminal complex impedance continuously and obtain independent state labels from thermocouple/thermal analysis plus interrupted-quench metallography. Fit any impedance-to-window mapping on some casts and evaluate it on held-out casts, including both schedules. Fail the candidate if the signal cannot identify the same useful reversal window robustly under the held-out condition, or if temperature/cooling changes alias with the phase-state signal.

Only if that gate passes, compare: (a) Li et al.'s fixed 0.5 s reversal recipe, (b) the impedance-triggered 0.5 s reversal policy, and (c) no-EMS reference, under matched alloy chemistry, superheat, mold, and measured cooling schedule. Randomize cast order. The adaptive policy succeeds only if, across the predeclared cooling envelope, it preserves or improves radial grain-size homogeneity and coarse-grain tail relative to the fixed timer while reducing EMS energy or avoiding a separately tuned recipe. If fixed 0.5 s remains equally good with the same energy, there is no capability gain; retire the control proposal. If impedance fails the held-out state test, the remaining route is spatial multi-coil sensing, which is a different, more instrumented operation and not this candidate.

## Strongest incumbent and status

The strongest incumbent is the fixed timer plus measured-temperature stop already used in Li et al.; ordinary thermocouple/calorimetric solid-fraction estimation is cheaper wherever an immersed probe is acceptable. Ma et al.'s multi-coil MIT is a stronger spatial observer where its array and thermal shielding are acceptable. The candidate's possible advantage is narrower: use the actuator's own electrical loading to retime an already proven stirring action across changing cooling conditions without adding a probe into the melt or a separate coil array. That advantage is **not established**. This opening is not a novelty, field-performance, cost, or scale-up claim.
