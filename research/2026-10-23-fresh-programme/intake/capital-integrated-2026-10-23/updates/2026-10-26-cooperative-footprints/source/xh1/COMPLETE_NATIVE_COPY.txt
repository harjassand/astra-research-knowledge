## Astra Chemistry — Product-footprint repair

### 1. A simpler chemical mechanism survives the strongest comparison

The previous multiview writer faced a difficult problem: a successful amide reaction could leave an entire DNA recognition strand permanently attached to the product, obstructing later operations.

A closer look at He and Liu's 2011 DNA-templated synthesis identifies a substantially simpler route. Their self-cleaving acyl-transfer reagents transferred organic building blocks from DNA-linked NHS esters onto a growing product. The donor's oligonucleotide linkage was cleaved as a direct consequence of acylation. They demonstrated six sequential reactions in one vessel, obtaining 35% overall product yield.&#x20;

ACS Publications



By contrast, the 2026 oxanine–amine chemistry forms covalent connections between DNA-bearing reactants. That is valuable for permanent DNA assemblies, but less directly suited to manufacturing products whose recognition guides should disappear after incorporation.&#x20;

Oxford Academic



The distinction suggests a stronger construction principle:

Use DNA to position the reactant, but transfer the chemical building block without permanently transferring its DNA guide.

This does not yet give autonomous, chemically fault-tolerant synthesis. Hydrolysis can consume an activated ester without making the desired bond, and incorrect amides remain possible. However, it eliminates one major proposed failure mechanism—the persistent donor-DNA footprint—through demonstrated reaction chemistry rather than a speculative post-reaction removal motor.

### 2. New obstruction: every pairwise reaction can work while the three-site synthesis is impossible

I generalized the previous product-obstruction graph to allow cooperative blocking.

Consider three chemical sites, A, B and C. Suppose a product at A alone does not obstruct C, and a product at B alone does not obstruct C. But the two retained product guides together close the route into C.

Assume the same condition applies symmetrically to all three sites.

Every two-site assembly succeeds. Yet whichever site reacts last finds that the other two completed products jointly block its reaction approach.

Therefore:

\\[ \boxed{ P(\text{complete all three})=0 } \\]

under the permanent-footprint model, even with perfect intended-bond chemistry.

I verified this by enumerating every reaction order and implementing an exact completion-state search. The result exposes a limitation of the previous pairwise graph theorem: a directed graph cannot represent a reaction site obstructed only by two or more products acting together.

The correct representation is a collection of blocking sets. A reaction approach becomes unavailable when all products in one of its blocking sets are present.

This leads to a stricter condition for reliable chemical synthesis: the controller must avoid not only pairwise product conflicts, but cooperative combinations of completed products that eliminate every remaining reaction approach.

### 3. An alternate reaction approach does not automatically solve the problem

I tested adding a second, obstruction-resistant approach to site C.

A compiler that knows the final reaction order can now complete A, then B, then C. No chemical guide removal is required.

But suppose all three sites are activated simultaneously, react at equal independent rates, and the controller does not observe which amides have formed.

C must still react last. Since each of the six reaction orders is equally likely,

\\[ \boxed{ P(\text{complete all three})=\frac13. } \\]

The alternate approach changes structural feasibility from impossible to possible, but does not ensure that the reaction sequence follows the one successful schedule.

This distinguishes a chemically possible synthesis from a reliable molecular manufacturing protocol. The previous compiler established the former; a genuinely autonomous or sensorless writer must additionally establish the latter.

### 4. Exact result: how many product guides must be removed?

For ordinary pairwise obstruction, I derived a useful design rule.

If removing a completed product's DNA guide eliminates all obstructions caused by that guide, then the minimum number of guides that must be removed to permit serial completion is exactly the size of a minimum directed feedback vertex set in the obstruction graph.

This is an application of established graph theory to chemical-reaction scheduling.

I verified the result using an independent reaction-state compiler on all 4,096 possible directed four-site graphs.

| Minimum guide removals needed | Number of graphs |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------- |
| 0                             | 543              |
| 1                             | 2,632            |
| 2                             | 920              |
| 3                             | 1                |

A single well-chosen guide-release operation can therefore eliminate otherwise irresolvable cyclic obstruction in many small chemical programs.

The result assumes the guide can be removed without breaking the desired organic bond. For oxanine DNA crosslinks, that operation has not been established. For He and Liu's acyl-transfer topology, the donor recognition strand is not permanently joined to the product, making subsequent noncovalent displacement more credible.

### 5. Correlated cleanup can destroy the apparent advantage

Consider the three-site cooperative obstruction. The third site becomes blocked only if both earlier DNA guides remain after clearance.

If each guide is independently cleared with probability \\(r\\),

\\[ P(\text{no jam})=1-(1-r)^2. \\]

But independent clearance is a strong assumption.

Illustrative 90% clearance efficiency per guide

| Outcome                                                 | Independent failures | Completely correlated failures |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| One three-site assembly avoids jamming                  | 99%                  | 90%                            |
| All 100 independent three-site assemblies avoid jamming | 36.60%               | 0.00266%                       |

The calculations show that measuring the average fraction of displaced donors is insufficient. What matters is whether the same molecular neighborhoods systematically retain multiple unwanted strands.

This is particularly important for a shared wash, strand-displacement reagent or conformational reset, because failures caused by local crowding or incorrect register may be correlated.

### 6. A stronger sensorless recovery operation

I next tested whether the transferred-acyl architecture could eliminate the need to diagnose every successful modification.

The proposed distributed writer uses one uniquely targeted primary amine per site. Correct amidation consumes that amine, while an unsuccessful, chemically intact site retains its amine. A successfully attached building block carries no newly exposed reactive amino terminus.

Under those restrictions, completed sites become chemically inert to subsequent rounds. Unfinished sites remain available for another attempt.

If one exposure has correct-reaction probability \\(p\\), permanent wrong-reaction probability \\(w\\), and otherwise leaves the site retriable, the exact correct-product yield after \\(m\\) attempts is

\\[ \boxed{ Y_m=\frac{p}{p+w}\left[1-(1-p-w)^m\right]. } \\]

For \\(L\\) independent sites, entirely correct product yield is \\(Y_m^L\\).

Using hypothetical \\(p=0.6\\), \\(w=10^{-6}\\), and 1,000 sites:

| Shared reaction opportunities | Correct complete-product yield |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| 12                            | 98.17%                         |
| 13                            | 99.17%                         |
| 14                            | 99.57%                         |
| 16                            | 99.79%                         |

This is a conditional route to blind chemical retries: no molecular sensor is required to distinguish completed sites before every new reagent exposure.

The limitation is important. Even unlimited retries cannot deliver 99% correctness across 1,000 sites when the irreversible wrong-reaction probability exceeds approximately \\(6.03\times10^{-6}\\) per attempt for this particular \\(p=0.6\\) model.

Thus removing donor-DNA footprints helps with physical jamming, but not with irreversible wrong chemistry.

### 7. Strongest construction retained

The research now favors a self-cleaving, DNA-addressed acyl-transfer writer on a persistent molecular scaffold over an oxanine-linked multiview writer for the first recovery demonstration.

Its distinguishing features would be a permanent scaffold root, independently addressable chemical sites, expendable DNA-linked acyl donors, physical clearance of donor recognition strands, and repeated exposure of still-unmodified sites without disturbing completed amides.

The three strongest remaining uncertainties are whether donor strands can be fully removed without displacing the scaffold's positional anchor, whether exposed amines on different programs can avoid cross-acylation, and whether the smaller organic products themselves create cooperative steric obstructions.

The strongest practical comparator is still simpler staged NHS chemistry, alongside the engineered enzyme cascade demonstrated by Schaub and colleagues in July 2026. The enzyme system has much more extensive measured substrate scope, with over 120 on-DNA amide examples, but has not demonstrated this particular independently repairable distributed writer.&#x20;

The next discriminating construction is a three-site scaffold with four preset conditions: neither neighboring site modified, only A modified, only B modified, and both A and B modified. The central site C is challenged under identical chemistry. This separates individual footprint effects from cooperative obstruction. Comparing donor-guide retention against selective guide displacement then tests whether the proposed repair actually restores C's accessibility.

The substantive finding: a chemically correct product can make subsequent assembly impossible through cooperative obstruction, even when every individual reaction and pairwise compatibility test succeeds. A chemically separable donor guide offers a plausible way around that failure without a perfect bond sensor, but only for product chemistries that remain reactive-site-specific and self-inactivating after correct transfer.
