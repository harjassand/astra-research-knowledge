## 1. The sharp theorem

Let \(d\ge2\), and let \(P\) be the projection onto gradient fields. On the unit flat torus \(\mathbb T^d\), its Fourier symbol is

\[
\widehat P(m)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{mm^{\mathsf T}}{|m|^2},&m\ne0,\\[4pt]
0,&m=0.
\end{cases}
\]

Thus \(Pf\) is a gradient with zero mean, while \((I-P)f\) is divergence-free. Define the reflection

\[
H=2P-I.
\]

It preserves the gradient component and reverses the divergence-free component. On \(\mathbb R^d\), use the same symbol at nonzero frequencies.

For real vector fields, with the Euclidean pointwise norm, the exact result is

\[
\boxed{
\|H\|_{L^p\to L^p}
=
C_p
:=
\max\left\{p-1,\frac1{p-1}\right\},
\qquad 1<p<\infty.
}
\]

This holds on both \(\mathbb T^d\) and \(\mathbb R^d\). The constant is independent of dimension and is sharp.

The corresponding elliptic result is as follows. Suppose \(A(x)\) is a real symmetric measurable matrix field satisfying

\[
0<\lambda I\le A(x)\le\Lambda I.
\]

Write

\[
s=\sqrt{\Lambda/\lambda}.
\]

For the periodic scalar equation

\[
\operatorname{div}(A\nabla u)=\operatorname{div}F,
\qquad \int_{\mathbb T^d}u=0,
\]

the maximal open interval admitting a uniform bound for the energy-solution gradient operator is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{2s}{s+1}<p<\frac{2s}{s-1}.
}
\]

When \(s=1\), this means \(1<p<\infty\). Within the interval, every \(F\in L^p\) has a unique mean-zero \(W^{1,p}\) solution, and

\[
\boxed{
\|\nabla u\|_p
\le
\frac{C_p(1+C_p)}
{\lambda\bigl[(s+1)-(s-1)C_p\bigr]}
\|F\|_p.
}
\tag{1}
\]

The **exponent interval is sharp** over the entire stated coefficient class. The prefactor in (1) is an explicit sufficient bound; its optimality is not claimed. At \(p=2\), it reduces exactly to \(1/\lambda\).

The scalar unknown and the boundary conditions matter. This theorem covers anisotropic \(d\times d\) coefficients and arbitrary dimension. It does not assert the same conclusion for coupled elliptic systems or arbitrary-domain boundary regularity.

## 2. How the central estimate is earned

The dimensional transfer works through a scalar gradient paired with a divergence-free field.

For \(a,b\in\mathbb R^d\), define

\[
\alpha=\frac{|a+b|}{2},
\qquad
\beta=\frac{|a-b|}{2},
\qquad
R=\alpha+\beta,
\qquad
D=a\cdot b=\alpha^2-\beta^2.
\]

For \(t>0\), introduce

\[
L_t(a,b)=
\begin{cases}
a\cdot b,&R\le t,\\
t|a+b|-t^2,&R>t.
\end{cases}
\]

Its useful property is the exact representation

\[
L_t(a,b)=
\sup_{z\in Z_t(a)}
\bigl[z\cdot b+z\cdot(a-z)\bigr],
\tag{2}
\]

where

\[
Z_t(a)=\{z:|z|=t\}
\;\cup\;
\begin{cases}
\{a\},&|a|\le t,\\
\varnothing,&|a|>t.
\end{cases}
\]

This identity follows from two elementary facts:

\[
|\alpha-\beta|\le |a|\le\alpha+\beta
\]

and

\[
D-(2t\alpha-t^2)
=(t-\alpha-\beta)(t-\alpha+\beta).
\]

The sphere in (2) supplies \(2t\alpha-t^2\); the additional point \(z=a\), when permitted, supplies \(D\). The displayed factorization determines which expression is larger.

Now let

\[
a=a_0+\nabla\eta,
\qquad
\operatorname{div}b=0,
\qquad
\int_{\mathbb T^d}b=b_0.
\]

The scalar projection lemma supplies a gradient \(z=a_0+\nabla\zeta\), whenever \(|a_0|\le t\), such that

\[
|z|\le t,\qquad
z=a\ \text{or}\ |z|=t\quad\text{a.e.},
\qquad
\int z\cdot(a-z)\ge0.
\tag{3}
\]

Its scalar cone-perturbation and translation arguments apply in every dimension. These are the precise source conclusions used here. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

Equations (2)–(3) give the dimension-independent inequality

\[
\int_{\mathbb T^d}L_t(a,b)\ge L_t(a_0,b_0).
\tag{4}
\]

There are two cases.

If the mean pair satisfies \(R_0>t\), the convex lower bound \(t|a+b|-t^2\) proves (4) by Jensen’s inequality.

If \(R_0\le t\), then \(|a_0|\le t\), so choose \(z\) from (3). Integration by parts gives

\[
\int z\cdot b=a_0\cdot b_0.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\int L_t(a,b)
\ge
\int z\cdot b+\int z\cdot(a-z)
\ge a_0\cdot b_0
=L_t(a_0,b_0).
\]

The essential dimensional step is this **gradient–divergence-free pairing**. A planar determinant is unnecessary.

### From the family \(L_t\) to the sharp norm

For \(p>2\), set

\[
B_p(a,b)=
\bigl((p-1)\beta-\alpha\bigr)(\alpha+\beta)^{p-1}.
\]

Direct integration gives

\[
B_p(a,b)
=
\frac{p(p-1)(p-2)}2
\int_0^\infty t^{p-3}\bigl[L_t(a,b)-D\bigr]\,dt.
\tag{5}
\]

Only \(0<t<R\) contributes. Because

\[
\int a\cdot b=a_0\cdot b_0,
\]

integrating (4) through (5) yields

\[
\int B_p(a,b)\ge B_p(a_0,b_0).
\tag{6}
\]

The remaining scalar inequality is

\[
\alpha^p-(p-1)^p\beta^p
\le
-c_p B_p(a,b),
\qquad
c_p=p(1-1/p)^{p-1}.
\tag{7}
\]

For clarity, this majorization has a short calculus proof. Divide by \(R^p\) and write \(v=\beta/R\). The difference between the right and left sides is

\[
g(v)=c_p(1-pv)-(1-v)^p+(p-1)^p v^p.
\]

It satisfies \(g(1/p)=g'(1/p)=0\). Its second derivative changes sign once, from negative to positive, before \(1/p\). Hence its minimum on the convex portion is zero; on the remaining concave portion the minimum is at an endpoint. The other endpoint value is \(g(0)=c_p-1\ge0\). This proves (7).

For a smooth periodic \(f\), choose

\[
a=Pf,\qquad b=-(I-P)f.
\]

Then

\[
a+b=Hf,\qquad a-b=f.
\]

The mean of \(a\) is zero, so the mean pair has \(\alpha_0=\beta_0\), and therefore \(B_p(a_0,b_0)\ge0\). Combining (6) and (7),

\[
\int|Hf|^p\le(p-1)^p\int|f|^p.
\]

Density extends this to \(L^p\). At \(p=2\), the Fourier symbols are orthogonal reflections. Self-adjointness and \(L^p\) duality give the bound \(1/(p-1)\) below \(2\).

### Euclidean extension and sharpness

The Euclidean result can be obtained without assuming an unevaluated transference operation. Start with Schwartz fields whose Fourier transforms are supported away from zero. Their gradient component has a Schwartz scalar potential; their divergence-free component has a Schwartz antisymmetric potential. Cut off those potentials, preserving the gradient and divergence-free constraints, and place the resulting compactly supported fields in a large periodic cube. Passing to the \(L^p\) limit gives the Euclidean inequality.

Sharpness is explicit. In the plane, use the smoothly truncated map

\[
u(z)=z|z|^{-2/p}
\]

on a large logarithmic annulus. There,

\[
|\partial_z u|
=\left(1-\frac1p\right)|z|^{-2/p},
\qquad
|\partial_{\bar z}u|
=\frac1p|z|^{-2/p}.
\]

The annular \(p\)-integrals grow logarithmically, while the cutoff contributions remain bounded. Taking

\[
a=\nabla u_1,\qquad b=-J\nabla u_2,
\qquad
J=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\]

produces fields for which

\[
\frac{\|Hf\|_p}{\|f\|_p}\longrightarrow p-1.
\]

On higher-dimensional tori, extend the fields independently of the extra coordinates. On \(\mathbb R^d\), use a slowly varying cutoff in those coordinates; the additional gradient terms vanish relative to the principal terms. Duality supplies sharpness below \(2\).

Thus both the upper bound and the matching obstruction are earned.

## 3. Inversion, stability and the elliptic consequence

Suppose \(m(x,z)\) is measurable in \(x\), vanishes at \(z=0\), and satisfies

\[
|m(x,z)-m(x,w)|\le k|z-w|.
\]

If

\[
\rho=kC_p<1,
\]

then

\[
Hf-m(x,f)=h
\]

has a unique \(L^p\) solution. Since \(H^2=I\), the iteration

\[
f_0=0,\qquad
f_{j+1}=H\bigl(m(x,f_j)+h\bigr)
\tag{8}
\]

is a contraction. It gives

\[
\|f-f_N\|_p
\le
\frac{C_p\rho^N}{1-\rho}\|h\|_p.
\tag{9}
\]

More usefully for a computed answer \(v\), its residual

\[
r=Hv-m(x,v)-h
\]

certifies

\[
\boxed{
\|f-v\|_p
\le
\frac{C_p}{1-\rho}\|r\|_p.
}
\tag{10}
\]

This follows immediately by subtracting the two equations and absorbing the term \(kC_p\|f-v\|_p\).

To apply it to the elliptic equation, normalize

\[
C=\frac{A}{\sqrt{\lambda\Lambda}},
\qquad
G=\frac{F}{\sqrt{\lambda\Lambda}}.
\]

The eigenvalues of \(C\) lie in \([1/s,s]\). Its Cayley transform is

\[
M=(I-C)(I+C)^{-1},
\qquad
\|M(x)\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le
k=\frac{s-1}{s+1}.
\]

Let

\[
e=\nabla u,\qquad j=Ce-G,\qquad f=e+j.
\]

The PDE says \(\operatorname{div}j=0\), so \(e=Pf\). Direct algebra turns the equation into

\[
Hf=Mf+h,
\qquad
h=2(I+C)^{-1}G.
\tag{11}
\]

The contraction condition is exactly

\[
\frac{s-1}{s+1}
\max\left\{p-1,\frac1{p-1}\right\}<1,
\]

which is equivalent to the claimed sharp interval.

Furthermore,

\[
\|h\|_p\le\frac{2}{\lambda(s+1)}\|F\|_p,
\qquad
\|P\|_p\le\frac{1+C_p}{2}.
\]

Combining these estimates with (10) proves (1).

## 4. Why neither endpoint can be improved

The upper endpoint has a concrete coefficient, solution and forcing family.

Set

\[
\gamma=\sqrt{\lambda/\Lambda}=\frac1s.
\]

In a planar disk, take the radial anisotropic coefficient

\[
A=\Lambda\,e_r\otimes e_r
+\lambda\,e_\theta\otimes e_\theta,
\]

and define

\[
u_0=r^\gamma\cos\theta,
\qquad
\psi=-\Lambda\gamma r^\gamma\sin\theta.
\]

A direct differentiation verifies

\[
J\nabla\psi=A\nabla u_0.
\]

Choose a smooth radial cutoff \(\eta\), equal to one near the origin and supported inside the disk, and set

\[
u=\eta u_0,
\qquad
F=u_0A\nabla\eta-\psi J\nabla\eta.
\]

Then

\[
A\nabla u-F=J\nabla(\eta\psi),
\]

so the PDE holds exactly. The forcing \(F\) is smooth and supported in an annulus. The solution belongs to the energy space, but near the origin

\[
|\nabla u|\asymp r^{\gamma-1}.
\]

Its \(L^p\) integral diverges precisely when

\[
p\ge\frac{2}{1-\gamma}
=\frac{2s}{s-1}.
\]

This gives failure at the upper endpoint itself and above it. The same family extends independently of extra torus coordinates, so every dimension \(d\ge2\) is covered.

For the lower endpoint, the energy-solution operator

\[
T_A:F\longmapsto\nabla u
\]

is self-adjoint because \(A\) is symmetric. A uniform \(L^p\) bound would therefore imply the corresponding \(L^{p'}\) bound. For

\[
1<p\le\frac{2s}{s+1},
\]

the conjugate exponent satisfies \(p'\ge2s/(s-1)\), contradicting the upper-endpoint family.

This establishes the full sharp interval for the uniform estimate. Particular coefficients can admit better estimates; the obstruction concerns what follows from the stated ellipticity bounds alone.

## 5. A nonlinear consequence under explicit conditions

when \(W\) is measurable in \(x\), twice continuously differentiable in \(z\), and

\[
D_zW(x,0)=0,\qquad

To see why, normalize the constitutive map by

\[
\mathcal C_x(z)=
\frac{D_zW(x,z)}{\sqrt{\lambda\Lambda}},

and define

\[
R_x(w)=2(I+\mathcal C_x)^{-1}(w)-w.
\]

Strong monotonicity makes the pointwise inverse well-defined. Its derivative calculation shows that the eigenvalues of \(D R_x\) lie in \([-k,k]\), where \(k=(s-1)/(s+1)\). Consequently \(R_x\) is \(k\)-Lipschitz.

With the same gradient–flux decomposition, the equation becomes

\[
Hf
=
\bigl[R_x(f+G)-R_x(G)\bigr]
+

The first bracket is the required contraction in \(f\); the second has the forcing bound used above. This proves existence, uniqueness and estimate (1) throughout the same interval. Its universal sharpness follows because the linear examples are included in this class.

## 6. The finite algorithm and its resource contract

\[
\|e_N-\nabla u\|_p\le\varepsilon.
\]

### Every iteration is finite

Multiplication by \(M\) is finite convolution of Fourier coefficients. Application of \(H\) at frequency \(m\ne0\) is multiplication by the rational matrix

\[
2\frac{mm^{\mathsf T}}{|m|^2}-I.
\]

At zero frequency, it is multiplication by \(-I\). Thus the operation in (8) is evaluated using exact rational arithmetic.

Let \(H_0\) be the sum of absolute real and imaginary parts of the coefficients of \(h\). On the unit-volume torus, it bounds \(\|h\|_p\). Choose \(N\) so that

\[
\frac{(1+C_p)C_p}{2(1-\rho)}
\rho^N H_0\le\varepsilon.
\tag{12}
\]

Repeated rational multiplication and comparison determine \(N\); no numerical logarithm is needed.

An earlier stop is permitted when the Fourier coefficients of the exact residual give a sufficient bound through (10). The a priori cap (12) is retained: convergence in \(L^p\) alone does not guarantee decay of a Fourier coefficient \(\ell^1\) bound.

### Bit cost

Let \(L\) be the complete binary input length, including coefficient denominators, frequency indices, dimension, accuracy and certificate. Let \(S\) be the number of modes of \(M\), and let \(D_M,D_h\) bound the coordinate magnitudes of the input frequencies.

Set

\[
R=\max\{1,D_h+(N+1)D_M,2D_M\},
\qquad
V=(2R+1)^d,
\]

and

\[
\ell=\left\lceil\log_2(2+dR^2)\right\rceil.
\]

A sufficient working bit bound is

\[
B=
O\!\left(
(N+2)\bigl[L+V\ell+\log(d(S+1)+2)\bigr]
\right).
\]

One obtains it by taking a common denominator for the input and a common denominator for all reflection matrices in the frequency box. Their bit lengths are at most \(L\) and \(V\ell\), respectively.

A conservative total bound for a common-denominator implementation is

\[
O\!\left(
\left[
L^2+d^3S^2+dV^2
+(N+1)d^2V(S+1)
\right]B^2
\right)

bit operations. This includes preprocessing, certificate verification, denominator construction, arithmetic and the final residual. For the delivered implementation using reduced fractions, replacing \(B^2\) by \(B^3\) is a conservative bound covering elementary gcd calculations.

The cost is explicit, but **it is not uniformly polynomial in \(L\)**. Variable dimension, large binary-encoded frequencies, or an exponentially small contraction gap can make it large.
