SHARP REAL HODGE REFLECTION AND ROUGH SCALAR ELLIPTIC EQUATIONS
Research derivation and finite computation, 6 November 2026

STATUS AND QUESTION
===================

The unrestricted exact-QUBO problem remains unresolved. The two preceding QUBO
packages are preserved unchanged. The user's latest instruction releases that
fixed target and authorizes independent scientific question selection.

The question selected here is:

  In arbitrary spatial dimension, what is the exact worst-case L^p amplification
  of the real gradient/divergence-free reflection, and what is the resulting
  maximal uniform L^p gradient-estimate interval for scalar elliptic equations
  with merely measurable symmetric coefficients of prescribed ellipticity?
  Can the inversion also be realized and certified for explicitly encoded data,
  with its finite arithmetic and information costs exposed?

This report gives a complete source-grounded mathematical derivation for that
question, with precise domains and an implemented encoded example. It uses the
scalar projection lemma in the September/October 2026 primary paper [1]. That
paper already proves the planar Beurling-Ahlfors result and explicitly announces
higher-dimensional and elliptic applications. Historical priority for the
consequences below has not been established. This is not an independent claim
to have discovered that paper's operation.

The real-vector reflection is the operator in Iwaniec-Sbordone's Conjecture 9.1
[2]. Their equation (11.10) identifies the corresponding proposed sharp
elliptic range. We establish the real-vector case here, including the Euclidean
and periodic settings, then supply the sharpness and computational contracts.

No higher-degree differential-form assertion is needed. The PDE has one scalar
unknown; coefficients may be anisotropic d by d matrices. Coupled elliptic
systems, arbitrary-domain boundary regularity, and unrestricted optimization
are outside the theorem stated here.

1. NOTATION AND MAIN RESULTS
===========================

Let d >= 2, 1 < p < infinity, and

  C_p = max(p-1, 1/(p-1)).

All vector fields are real-valued, with the Euclidean pointwise norm. Q denotes
the unit-volume flat torus R^d/Z^d. The mean-zero gradient projection P is the
Fourier multiplier

  P_hat(m) = m m^T / |m|^2       (m != 0),
  P_hat(0) = 0.

Set H = 2P-I. Thus H preserves gradients and reverses divergence-free fields,
including constant fields on Q. On R^d, use the same nonzero-frequency symbol.

THEOREM A. On both Q and R^d,

  ||H||_{L^p(R^d-valued) -> L^p(R^d-valued)} = C_p.

THEOREM B. Let m(x,z) be measurable in x, continuous in z, m(x,0)=0, and

  |m(x,z)-m(x,w)| <= k |z-w|,       0 <= k < 1.

If rho = k C_p < 1, then for every h in L^p the equation

  Hf - m(x,f) = h

has a unique L^p solution. Its Picard iteration is

  f_0=0,       f_{j+1}=H(m(x,f_j)+h),

with explicit bounds

  ||f||_p <= C_p/(1-rho) ||h||_p,
  ||f-f_N||_p <= rho^N C_p/(1-rho) ||h||_p.

For any candidate v and residual r=Hv-m(x,v)-h,

  ||f-v||_p <= C_p/(1-rho) ||r||_p.                 (1.1)

THEOREM C. Suppose A(x) is real symmetric, measurable on Q, and

  lambda I <= A(x) <= Lambda I,       0 < lambda <= Lambda < infinity.

Put s=sqrt(Lambda/lambda). The maximal open interval of exponents admitting a
uniform bound for the energy-solution gradient operator is

  p_- = 2s/(s+1) < p < 2s/(s-1) = p_+.            (1.2)

For s=1 the upper endpoint means infinity. Throughout this interval, every
F in L^p(Q;R^d) has a unique mean-zero W^{1,p} solution of

  div(A grad u) = div F,

and

  ||grad u||_p <=
    [C_p(1+C_p) / {lambda[(s+1)-(s-1)C_p]}] ||F||_p.       (1.3)

The interval is sharp over this whole coefficient class. The numerical
constant in (1.3) is explicit and sufficient; no optimality claim is made
for that prefactor. At p=2 it is exactly 1/lambda.

The homogeneous-gradient Euclidean version follows by the same construction
from Theorem A, with u understood up to constants. The periodic theorem avoids
any question about boundary geometry. Section 6 gives a nonlinear extension
with a precisely stated constitutive condition.

2. THE DIMENSIONAL TRANSFER
==========================

The only non-elementary new primary-source ingredient used is [1], Lemma 4:
scalar L^2 projection onto a convex constraint on the gradient supplies both
an active-set alternative and a nonnegative integrated gradient pairing.
Its proof works in R^d: the support-function cone and max/min perturbations
are scalar, and the translation argument is summed over d coordinates.
No planar rotation is used in that lemma.

Here is the exact specialization needed. For a0 in the radius-t ball and
v(x)=a0.x+eta(x), with eta periodic Lipschitz, project eta in L^2 onto

  {zeta periodic Lipschitz : |a0+grad zeta| <= t a.e.}.

For z=a0+grad zeta and a=a0+grad eta,

  |z| <= t,
  z=a or |z|=t a.e.,
  integral_Q z.(a-z) >= 0.                         (2.1)

These are the exact conclusions of the cited scalar lemma in dimension d.
Its existence assertion is a proof device. The numerical algorithm below
does not call this infinite-dimensional projection.

Now take arbitrary a,b in R^d and define

  alpha=|a+b|/2,   beta=|a-b|/2,
  R=alpha+beta,    D=a.b=alpha^2-beta^2.

For t>0 set

  L_t(a,b) = D                    if R <= t,
             t|a+b|-t^2          if R > t.

The following support formula is exact in every dimension:

  L_t(a,b) = sup_{z in Z_t(a)} [z.b + z.(a-z)],       (2.2)
  Z_t(a) = {|z|=t} union {a if |a|<=t}.

Indeed, the sphere supplies 2t alpha-t^2, and z=a supplies D. Use

  |alpha-beta| <= |a| <= alpha+beta,
  D-(2t alpha-t^2) = (t-alpha-beta)(t-alpha+beta).

If R<=t, D is the larger expression and a is allowed. If R>t and |a|<=t,
the sphere expression is larger. If |a|>t, only the sphere is allowed.

Let a=a0+grad eta be smooth periodic and b smooth divergence-free, with mean
b0. Define R0 from a0,b0. We claim

  integral_Q L_t(a,b) >= L_t(a0,b0).                (2.3)

If R0>t, L_t >= t|a+b|-t^2 everywhere, and Jensen's inequality proves (2.3).
If R0<=t, then |a0|<=t. Choose the scalar projected gradient z in (2.1).
Equation (2.2), divergence-free integration by parts, and (2.1) give

  integral L_t(a,b)
    >= integral z.b + integral z.(a-z)
    >= a0.b0 = L_t(a0,b0).

The same integration by parts gives integral a.b=a0.b0. This is the crucial
dimensional change: an ordinary div-curl pairing replaces the planar determinant.
It remains valid for a single scalar potential in every d.

3. THE SHARP REFLECTION NORM
===========================

For p>2 define the real-vector Burkholder expression

  B_p(a,b) = ((p-1)beta-alpha)(alpha+beta)^{p-1}.

Direct integration yields

  B_p(a,b) = p(p-1)(p-2)/2 *
    integral_0^infinity t^{p-3}[L_t(a,b)-D] dt.       (3.1)

Only 0<t<R contributes. For explicit verification, the integral equals

  -D R^{p-2}/(p-2) + 2 alpha R^{p-1}/(p-1) - R^p/p.

For bounded smooth fields, all integrals are finite. Subtract the corresponding
constant-field expression, use (2.3) and integral D=D0, and obtain

  integral_Q B_p(a,b) >= B_p(a0,b0).                (3.2)

We also need the elementary scalar majorization

  alpha^p-(p-1)^p beta^p <= -c_p B_p,
  c_p=p(1-1/p)^{p-1}.                             (3.3)

For completeness, divide by R^p (R=0 is immediate) and put v=beta/R. The
difference between the right and left sides becomes

  g(v)=c_p(1-pv)-(1-v)^p+(p-1)^p v^p,  0<=v<=1.

It has g(1/p)=g'(1/p)=0, and

  g''(v)=p(p-1)[(p-1)^p v^{p-2}-(1-v)^{p-2}].

The second derivative changes sign once, from negative to positive, at
v0=1/[1+(p-1)^{p/(p-2)}] < 1/p. Hence g is nonnegative on [v0,1], where its
minimum is at 1/p. On [0,v0] it is concave, so its minimum is at an endpoint.
The endpoint v0 was handled; g(0)=c_p-1>=0. To see the last fact, log(c_p)
vanishes at p=2 and has derivative 2/p+log(1-1/p)>=2/p-1/(p-1)>=0.

For a smooth periodic real vector field f, take

  a=Pf,       b=-(I-P)f.

Then a is a mean-zero gradient, b is divergence-free,

  a+b=Hf,       a-b=f.

Their means satisfy a0=0, b0=-mean(f), and consequently alpha0=beta0. Thus
B_p(a0,b0)>=0. Applying (3.2) and (3.3),

  integral_Q |Hf|^p <= (p-1)^p integral_Q |f|^p.

Extend by density. At p=2 the Fourier symbols are orthogonal reflections.
For 1<p<2, the symmetric symbol and L^p-L^{p'} duality give the constant
1/(p-1). This proves the upper bound on Q.

Euclidean upper bound, without an uncomputed transference operation:
-----------------------------------------------------------------

First take a real Schwartz f whose Fourier transform is smooth and supported
in an annulus away from zero. Then a=Pf and b=-(I-P)f are Schwartz. Write
a=grad phi and b_i=sum_j partial_j Psi_ij, with Psi antisymmetric and Schwartz.
The scalar and antisymmetric potentials are obtained by dividing their Fourier
expressions by |xi|^2; annular support makes this nonsingular. Explicitly, under
the exp(2 pi i x.xi) convention,

  Psi_hat_ij=(xi_j b_hat_i-xi_i b_hat_j)/(2 pi i |xi|^2).

Let chi_R be a smooth cutoff equal to one on a growing ball and define

  a_R=grad(chi_R phi),
  (b_R)_i=sum_j partial_j(chi_R Psi_ij).

Both are compactly supported, a_R is a gradient, b_R is divergence-free, and
they converge in L^p to a,b. Put their support inside a sufficiently large
periodic cube. Both means vanish: for b_R this follows by integrating
div(x_i b_R)=(b_R)_i. Rescaling (3.2) to that cube gives integral B_p(a_R,b_R)>=0.
The p-growth and continuity of B_p permit the L^p limit. Equation (3.3) now gives
the Euclidean upper bound for annular Schwartz data. Such data are dense in
L^p, 1<p<infinity, by smooth high- and low-frequency cutoffs. Duality handles
p<2 exactly as on Q.

Sharpness:
----------

For p>2, consider the planar map u(z)=z|z|^{-2/p} on epsilon<|z|<r0, with
smooth inner and outer cutoffs. The cutoffs can be chosen so that their
gradient p-integrals stay bounded independently of epsilon. In the main
annulus,

  |partial_z u|=(1-1/p)|z|^{-2/p},
  |partial_bar_z u|=(1/p)|z|^{-2/p}.

Each p-integral grows as log(1/epsilon). Set

  a=grad u1,       b=-J grad u2,       J=[[0,-1],[1,0]],
  f=a-b.

Then Hf=a+b, |a+b|=2|partial_z u|, and |a-b|=2|partial_bar_z u|. The norm ratio
tends to p-1. The compactly supported map fits inside the torus.

For higher-dimensional tori, take fields independent of the additional
coordinates. For R^d, multiply u1 and u2 by a slowly varying cutoff chi(x'/R)
in the extra coordinates, use the full gradient for a, and use -J grad_{1,2}u2
times that cutoff in the first two components for b. The latter is still
divergence-free. The extra gradient components are lower by a factor R^{-1}
in L^p relative to the principal terms. Let R tend to infinity and then let
epsilon tend to zero. Duality gives sharpness below 2. Theorem A follows.

4. INVERSION AND RESIDUAL CERTIFICATES
====================================

Since H^2=I, Hf-m(x,f)=h is equivalent to

  f=H(m(x,f)+h).

The right side has L^p Lipschitz constant rho=k C_p<1. Banach's contraction
theorem gives uniqueness, existence, the stated iterates, and their geometric
error. Comparing a candidate v with the solution gives

  ||f-v||_p <= C_p k ||f-v||_p + C_p ||Hv-m(x,v)-h||_p,

which proves (1.1). The gradient component has the further factor

  ||P||_p <= (1+C_p)/2.                            (4.1)

For linear multiplication by M(x), the inverse is also the convergent series

  (H-M)^{-1}h = sum_{j>=0} (HM)^j Hh.

This series is an analytic identity. Section 8 supplies the finite operations
for the encoded case; no general claim of computability of arbitrary measurable
input functions is implicit in Theorem B.

5. THE ELLIPTIC INTERVAL AND BOTH ENDPOINT OBSTRUCTIONS
=====================================================

Normalize A by alpha0=sqrt(lambda Lambda):

  C=A/alpha0,       G=F/alpha0.

The eigenvalues of C lie in [1/s,s]. Define the pointwise Cayley transform

  M=(I-C)(I+C)^{-1},       k=(s-1)/(s+1).

Symmetry implies ||M(x)||_op<=k. For e=grad u and j=C e-G, the PDE says that
j is divergence-free. Let f=e+j. Then e=Pf, j=(I-P)f, and direct algebra gives

  Hf=Mf+h,       h=2(I+C)^{-1}G.                   (5.1)

Conversely, an L^p solution of (5.1) recovers the gradient and flux of the PDE.
The contraction criterion k C_p<1 is precisely (1.2). Moreover,

  ||h||_p <= 2/[lambda(s+1)] ||F||_p.

Combining this with Theorem B and (4.1) proves (1.3). The periodic gradient has
zero mean and integrates to a unique mean-zero W^{1,p} potential. For p>=2,
this agrees with the variational energy solution; uniqueness inside the full
stated p interval follows directly from the contraction representation.

Upper endpoint counterexample:
------------------------------

Assume lambda<Lambda and put gamma=sqrt(lambda/Lambda)=1/s. In a planar disk,
let

  A=Lambda e_r tensor e_r + lambda e_theta tensor e_theta,
  u0=r^gamma cos(theta),
  psi=-Lambda gamma r^gamma sin(theta).

Then J grad psi=A grad u0. In particular div(A grad u0)=0 distributionally,
including at the origin; gamma>0 makes the shrinking-circle boundary term
vanish. Also

  |grad u0| is comparable to r^{gamma-1}.

Choose a smooth radial cutoff eta which equals one near the origin and vanishes
before the boundary of the disk. Define

  u=eta u0,
  F=u0 A grad eta - psi J grad eta.

The identity

  A grad u-F=J grad(eta psi)

proves the PDE exactly. F is smooth and supported in an annulus. Outside the
disk choose any constant matrix within the same eigenvalue bounds. The
potential is in W^{1,2}, but

  integral_0 r^{p(gamma-1)+1} dr

diverges exactly when p>=2/(1-gamma)=p_+. Hence no uniform L^p estimate can
hold at or above p_+, even for smooth forcing. This construction can be placed
inside Q and extended independently of extra torus coordinates, so it applies
to every d>=2. Extra coefficient eigenvalues can be chosen equal to lambda.

For a Euclidean higher-dimensional counterexample, additionally cut off in
the extra coordinates, keep the planar stream flux in the first two
components, and define F=A grad u-j. The extra forcing is bounded and compactly
supported because u0 is bounded. The same radial integrability obstruction
remains.

Lower endpoint obstruction:
---------------------------

Let T_A:F -> grad u be the energy-solution operator. Symmetry of A gives

  integral (T_A F).G = integral F.(T_A G)

for L^2 data. Therefore boundedness on L^p would imply boundedness on L^{p'}.
For 1<p<=p_-, its conjugate p' is >=p_+, contradicting the example above.
This proves sharpness at the lower endpoint as a uniform operator estimate.
It does not assert that every individual equation fails outside the interval.

6. A NONLINEAR SCALAR CONSEQUENCE
================================

Let W(x,z) be measurable in x and C^2 in z, with

  D_z W(x,0)=0,
  lambda I <= D_z^2 W(x,z) <= Lambda I

for all z and almost every x. Consider

  div(D_z W(x,grad u))=div F.

The same interval and estimate (1.3) hold on the torus. Here are the additional
steps, so no linearization assumption is hidden.

Set C_x(z)=D_z W(x,z)/alpha0. The map z -> z+C_x(z) is globally invertible:
its derivative is positive definite with eigenvalues in [1+1/s,1+s], and its
strong monotonicity and quadratic growth give existence of the inverse.
Define

  R_x(w)=2(I+C_x)^{-1}(w)-w.

Its derivative has spectrum in [-k,k]; hence it is globally k-Lipschitz and
R_x(0)=0. With G=F/alpha0, e=grad u, j=C_x(e)-G and f=e+j, the equation becomes

  Hf=R_x(f+G)+G
     =[R_x(f+G)-R_x(G)] + [R_x(G)+G].

The bracketed nonlinear term is k-Lipschitz in f and vanishes at zero. Since
R_x(G)+G=2(I+C_x)^{-1}(G), its norm is bounded by
2/[lambda(s+1)]|F|. Theorem B proves the assertion. The linear class is a
subclass, so the p interval remains universally sharp.

This is an existence and stability theorem for measurable constitutive laws.
For a finite computational algorithm, pointwise evaluation or inversion of a
general W would need its own finite representation and cost bound. The
implementation in this package uses linear rational Fourier coefficients and
does not hide a nonlinear inverse operation.

7. SCIENTIFIC MEANING OF THE BOUNDS
==================================

The exponent interval describes precisely how strongly a uniformly elliptic
medium can concentrate the gradient of a scalar field under L^p forcing.
Smoothness of the coefficients is not assumed in the analytic theorem.
All spatial dimensions have the same threshold after ellipticity is fixed.
The endpoint family establishes that a better threshold cannot follow from
ellipticity alone.

The concrete output is more than existence: it includes an explicit inverse
bound, geometric iteration error, and a residual-to-solution certificate.
For example, (1.3) immediately implies the level-set estimate

  measure{|grad u|>t} <= (C ||F||_p/t)^p,

where C is the displayed coefficient in (1.3). These are consequences for the
mathematical model. No material experiment or empirical performance claim is
part of this work.

P=NP does not follow. The continuum theorem uses analytic structure that is
not supplied for arbitrary discrete optimization. Section 8 also exposes the
dimensional and precision dependence of the finite algorithm; it supplies no
unrestricted polynomial-time search operation.

8. FINITE INPUT, OUTPUT, AND BIT COST
====================================

An arbitrary measurable coefficient function is not a finite binary input.
We therefore distinguish the preceding analytic theorem from the following
explicitly encoded computational problem.

Input:
------

* An explicitly listed dimension d.
* A real matrix trigonometric polynomial M, and a real vector trigonometric
  polynomial h. Each Fourier mode is an explicit signed-integer d-tuple;
  each complex coefficient has rational real and imaginary parts supplied
  by binary signed numerators and positive denominators. Reality is checked
  by conjugate symmetry.
* Rational p>1, k>=0, epsilon>0, with k C_p<1.
* A finite, exactly checked bound certificate for ||M||_infinity,op<=k.

One permitted certificate is the directly computable sufficient bound

  sum_m sum_i,j (|Re Mhat_ij(m)|+|Im Mhat_ij(m)|) <= k.

A less conservative certificate used in the example is: M is real symmetric
and exact Fourier convolution verifies M^2=k^2 I. This implies its pointwise
operator norm is k. Constructing and checking either certificate is charged
to preprocessing. The algorithm does not assume a global-norm oracle or
accept an unevaluated inequality. Its input class is the stated certified
finite Fourier class, not all possible measurable matrices.

Output:
-------

A rational Fourier polynomial f_N and e_N=P f_N, together with a rigorous
certificate that ||e_N-Pf||_p<=epsilon for the unique solution of Hf-Mf=h.
The zero-mean potential can also be represented by

  uhat_N(m)=(m.ehat_N(m))/(2 pi i |m|^2),  m!=0;
  uhat_N(0)=0,

using an explicit symbolic 1/(2 pi i) factor. The output here is a certified
approximation, not an exact finite representation of a general PDE solution.

Algorithm:
----------

1. Parse and coalesce input modes, verify conjugate symmetry, and verify the
   supplied coefficient certificate using exact rational arithmetic.
2. Compute C_p=max(p-1,1/(p-1)) and rho=k C_p exactly.
3. Let H0 be the rational sum of absolute real and imaginary parts of all
   h coefficients, which bounds every finite L^p norm on Q.
4. Choose the smallest integer N such that

     ((1+C_p)/2) [C_p/(1-rho)] rho^N H0 <= epsilon.     (8.1)

   Repeated rational multiplication and comparison suffices; no logarithm
   evaluation is required. If rho=0, one iteration gives the exact f=Hh.
5. Iterate f_{j+1}=H(M f_j+h) by finite Fourier convolution and the exact
   rational matrix 2m m^T/|m|^2-I at each nonzero mode; at zero use -I.
6. Optionally stop earlier if the exact residual

     r=Hf_j-Mf_j-h

   has rational coefficient bound R0 satisfying

     ((1+C_p)/2) [C_p/(1-rho)] R0 <= epsilon.          (8.2)

   The a priori cap (8.1) is retained. A Fourier coefficient l1 residual need
   not decay even when the L^p iteration converges, so relying solely on that
   optional residual stopping rule would not be a general termination proof.

Complete resource bound:
------------------------

Let L be total binary input length, including the variables/dimension list,
all frequency indices, all rational numerators and denominators, p,k,epsilon,
and any certificate. Let S be the number of M modes. Let D_M,D_h be maximum
coordinate magnitudes of the input frequencies, and use

  R=max(1,D_h+(N+1)D_M,2D_M),       V=(2R+1)^d,
  ell=ceil(log_2(2+d R^2)).

All iterate, projected-output, certificate-convolution and final-residual
frequencies lie in this box. A deliberately conservative dense-box implementation is enough for a
fully finite bound; the delivered code stores only nonzero modes.

Let Q0 be the product of all input denominators, with log_2 Q0<=L. Let QH be
the product of |m|^2 over nonzero modes in this box, so log_2 QH<=V ell.
All fractions arising in the jth iterate have denominators dividing
(Q0 QH)^j; the output projection and residual require at most N+1 powers.
Coefficient magnitudes can grow by at most a factor bounded by
d^2(S+1)2^{L+1} per convolution/reflection stage. Thus one may take

  B = O((N+2)[L+V ell+log_2(d(S+1)+2)])             (8.3)

as a common bit bound for stored numerators, denominators and accumulators.
One extra fixed multiplicative constant covers intermediate products.

Using common denominators, schoolbook integer multiplication and exact
division, a sufficient total bit-operation bound is

  O([L^2 + d^3 S^2 + d V^2
       + (N+1)d^2 V(S+1)] B^2).                    (8.4)

The d^3 S^2 term covers the optional identity certificate M^2=k^2 I; direct
coefficient-budget certificates are cheaper. Mode sorting, index arithmetic,
normalization, denominator construction, rational comparisons, coefficient
norm bounds, and final residual computation are covered by this conservative
bound. A common-denominator implementation avoids requiring any faster
  integer-arithmetic or gcd assumption. For the delivered Python Fraction
  implementation, a conservative bound replaces B^2 in (8.4) by B^3:
  Euclidean gcd takes at most O(B) divisions of O(B)-bit integers, each of
  which can be done in O(B^2) bit operations. Thus its fraction reductions
  also have an explicit finite bound.

Output storage is O(d V B) bits, and working storage can be bounded by
O(d^2 V B+d^2 S B+L), including the matrix certificate accumulator.
A sufficient iteration bound is

  N <= 1 + max(0,
        log(((1+C_p)C_p H0)/(2(1-rho)epsilon))/(1-rho)),

for 0<rho<1. Ceiling conventions can increase the bound by one.

This is polynomial in the displayed expanded parameters. It is not uniformly
polynomial in L: the dimension, binary frequency sizes, and reciprocal
contraction gap may cause exponential growth. If dimension is fixed,
frequencies are polynomially bounded and the inverse gap is polynomially
bounded, it becomes a polynomial bit algorithm in the remaining encoded
parameters and accuracy bits. Those are actual conditions, not implicit
promises for arbitrary data.

9. COMPLETED EXACT-ARITHMETIC INSTANCE
=====================================

On Q=T^2 set p=4, k=3/10, rho=9/10 and

  M(x)=k [[ cos(2 pi x1),  sin(2 pi x1)],
          [ sin(2 pi x1), -cos(2 pi x1)]],

  h(x)=(cos(2 pi x2), sin(2 pi(x1+x2))).

Exact Fourier convolution verifies M^2=k^2 I. The physical elliptic
coefficient and forcing are explicitly

  A=[(1+k^2)I-2M]/(1-k^2),
  F=(I-M)h/(1-k^2).

The eigenvalues of A are 7/13 and 13/7. The full sharp interval for its
ellipticity class is 13/10<p<13/3, so p=4 lies inside it.

The provided executable uses only Python's standard library and exact
rational arithmetic. It completed ten iterations. Its output gradient has
40 nonzero Fourier modes. The final reflection residual has eight modes.
The rigorous certificate is

  ||grad u-e_10||_4 <=
    2980987023573 / 1627930625000000
    < 0.001832 < 1/256.

Here the error factor multiplying the exact residual bound is exactly 60.
The a priori cap would have been 99 iterations. INPUT.json has 1,772 UTF-8
bytes; this is a measured serialization size, not an assertion of minimum
binary encoding length. Maximum numerator/denominator bit length in the
stored gradient and flux output is 56. Across stored iterates and reflection
residuals it is 59; this measurement does not exclude larger temporary
arithmetic products. Timing and process memory observations are in
AUDIT_RESULTS.json and are not complexity proofs.

verify_certificate.py reads the input and final answer, without the iteration
history, and verifies:

  * reality and the exact matrix coefficient certificate;
  * the Cayley coefficient and forcing identities;
  * zero mean and curl-free gradient, and divergence-free flux;
  * the exact reflection and physical residuals;
  * the rational residual inequality yielding the requested L^4 accuracy.

All these checks passed. The analytic implication from residual to error is
Theorems A and B; a finite computation is not treated as proof of those
general theorems.
