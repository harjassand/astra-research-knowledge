Invent a manufacturable heat-to-electricity converter that could make cheap stored heat into useful dispatchable electricity.

Can a new physical converter architecture achieve high NET efficiency and low full-system cost at a materially easier hot-side operating temperature than today's highest-temperature thermophotovoltaic experiments? The invention sought is the complete hot-to-electric conversion operation, with attainable materials, actual photon/heat management, cooling and electrical output. A cell-efficiency record or an ideal spectral calculation alone is not the capability.

Use a 100-watt net DC converter as the complete design target. It receives heat from an externally maintained reservoir whose hottest emitting/contact surface is at most 1400 degrees Celsius, rejects heat to ambient air at 35 degrees Celsius, and supplies regulated useful DC power continuously over a 100-hour run. Choose and justify the operating hot-side temperature, output voltage and electrical load range. The target net heat-to-electric efficiency is at least 35 percent at rated output, including every converter auxiliary and loss. There is no supplied cold-water stream, free vacuum system, lossless mirror, perfect selective emitter or uncharged control/actuation channel.

Define the hot boundary explicitly: Q_hot is the total net heat removed from the hot reservoir by the ENTIRE converter, including hot enclosure, supports, apertures and plumbing introduced by the design. P_net is useful regulated power delivered to the load after subtracting fans, pumps, vacuum maintenance, controls, power electronics and all other electrical auxiliaries, irrespective of how they are powered. Startup energy, startup time and shutdown are additional charged quantities; give full-run energy efficiency including them as well as the steady rating. The source may be electrically heated in a later laboratory test, but heater input is not a net electrical gain and must be metered separately.

The production target is at most US$500 per kilowatt of rated NET converter capacity at a specified credible manufacturing scale, initially evaluate 10 megawatts per year. Include cells or other active elements, emitters and reflectors, heat exchangers, insulation, containment, atmosphere/vacuum system, electrical interconnects, electronics, assembly, yield, test and necessary replacement. Equipment and process development for a first prototype are separate explicit costs, not silently amortized away. Treat this cost as an invention target, not an assertion that current components are available at it. Analyze service life, thermomechanical cycling and degradation; do not turn a 100-hour model into an established multiyear lifetime.

A heat-engine design using thermophotovoltaics is an opening, not a prescribed solution. You may change geometry, optical architecture, emitter spectrum, cell platform, thermal coupling or the conversion principle itself if that better achieves the same useful operation and costs. You may introduce a new material or fabrication mechanism, but its achievable properties, fabrication, stability and uncertainty must be part of the invention rather than a freely assigned constitutive law. Credit existing converters and thermal-battery companies. If an existing matched system already meets these targets, reproducing it is not a new invention: identify a consequential additional capability or at least a twofold full-cost improvement at matched useful performance, and construct the new mechanism that produces it.

For a photon converter, close the actual control-volume energy balance and optical return path. A reflected sub-bandgap photon is useful only to the extent it is returned to and absorbed by the hot reservoir before being lost. Cell calorimetry P_el/(P_el+Q_cell) omits reflected light and is not automatically the complete module efficiency. Include angular and spectral dependence, finite view factors, cold contacts and gaps, all enclosure surfaces, emitter absorptivity, absorption in filters/reflectors, nonuniform illumination, electrical series resistance, cell temperature and coolant/fan performance. Repeated optical returns must conserve energy. Show where unwanted photons and all rejected heat go; neither a radiation shield nor a cold mirror can remove entropy for free.

A useful elementary steady balance is Q_hot=P_el+Q_cell+(1-r)P_refl+Q_other and P_net=P_el-P_aux, when those terms are disjoint and the actual return fraction r is defined consistently. Thus if eta_cell=P_el/(P_el+Q_cell), a=P_aux/P_el, u=P_refl/P_el and e=Q_other/P_el, then eta_net=(1-a)/(1/eta_cell+(1-r)u+e). This is bookkeeping for a specified physical design, not a supplied high-efficiency cavity or a claim that a single scalar return factor replaces radiative transport.

Existing experimental work is already strong. Large-area single-junction GaInAs cells have measured 38.8+/-2.0 percent calorimetric cell efficiency at an 1850-degree-Celsius emitter, with substantial power density and repeatable wafer processing. That temperature lies ABOVE the present target, and the number is not net module efficiency. Separate close-gap calorimetry at view factors up to 0.98 shows why high illumination, current resistance, cell cooling and the precise efficiency definition matter. An optical improvement that raises flux but worsens resistance or cooling can lose overall.

Construct the complete geometry, materials stack, thermal circuit, electrical architecture, operating point, tolerances and fabrication sequence. Use actual temperature-dependent material data, measured optical/electrical device behavior or transparent bounded extrapolations. Give an executable energy/optical/electrical model that can reproduce the operating point and expose the dominant sensitivities. Check conservation, spectral/angular integration, array mismatch, cooling, mechanical gaps under expansion, contamination and cycling. A best-case nominal point with no feasible tolerance or service margin is not the full design.

Compare the SAME useful operation with the strongest suitable alternatives, including existing TPV architectures and a small conventional heat engine where relevant. Keep measured performance, company targets and cost models distinct. For the larger energy opportunity, translate the converter into a specific thermal-storage duty cycle and calculate the resulting electrical round-trip efficiency and full-cycle delivered-energy cost, charging energy price, storage loss, capacity factor, heat-store/charging costs, replacements and balance of plant. This is a check of whether the converter would unlock a meaningful storage advantage, not permission to assume a free heat store. The converter need not solve an entire grid-storage deployment here, but a cheap cell that does not improve a useful system is insufficient.

Develop a concrete first prototype and a decisive experiment for later physical execution. Specify the buildable mechanism, necessary components/access, calorimetric boundary, electrical metering, ordinary comparator, uncertainties, expected signals, cost and numerical pass/fail criteria. The experiment must distinguish your claimed new advantage from a mistaken energy balance or favorable cell-only metric. Design it here; do not perform a physical experiment. Separate what that prototype would establish from lifetime, manufacturing and plant-scale claims it would leave open.

Please solve the substantive invention problem, not merely write a research proposal or summarize existing TPV performance. Pursue a new mechanism/design, work through the full physical and engineering costs, and state exactly which required performance follows and which critical physical claims remain untested. A failure of one material stack or cavity is not an all-converter impossibility. You may challenge the framing and choose a better representation while pursuing the complete useful operation.
