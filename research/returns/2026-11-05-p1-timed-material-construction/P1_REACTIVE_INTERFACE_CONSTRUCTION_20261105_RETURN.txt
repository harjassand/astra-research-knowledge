## 1. The operation that was actually constructed

I used planar compound growth as a setting where the local inputs can be traced to independent measurements and the interface operation can be stated completely. This also supplies a strong ordinary-method comparison: the construction uses established Wagner diffusion and Stefan interface balances.

For the sequence

\[
\mathrm{Cu}\;|\;\mathrm{Cu_3Sn}\;|\;\mathrm{Cu_6Sn_5}\;|\;\mathrm{Sn},
\]

the state consists of the physical layer thicknesses \(u,v\), the remaining elemental inventories per area, and the prescribed temperature history. Phase compositions and atomic molar volumes connect that state to component amounts.

The constitutive inputs are the **integrated interdiffusion coefficients** of the two compounds under their specified neighboring phases. These are not interchangeable with tracer diffusivities, ordinary concentration-dependent diffusivities, or coefficients fitted to the complete specimen being predicted.

The coupled evolution is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\frac{du}{dt}
&=\frac{80}{9}\frac{D_\varepsilon}{u}
-\frac{44}{9}\frac{V}{W}\frac{D_\eta}{v},\\[2mm]
\frac{dv}{dt}
&=-\frac{44}{9}\frac{W}{V}\frac{D_\varepsilon}{u}
+\frac{121}{18}\frac{D_\eta}{v},
\end{aligned}
\]

where \(\varepsilon=\mathrm{Cu_3Sn}\), \(\eta=\mathrm{Cu_6Sn_5}\),

\[
V=8.59\times10^{-6},\qquad
W=10.59\times10^{-6}\quad
\mathrm{m^3/mol\!-\!atoms},
\]

and \(D\) has units \(\mathrm{m^2/s}\). Thickness is in metres and time is in seconds.

The same fluxes determine both moving interfaces and consumption of the elemental reservoirs:

\[
-\frac{dN_{\mathrm{Cu}}}{dt}
=\frac{4D_\varepsilon}{Vu},
\qquad
-\frac{dN_{\mathrm{Sn}}}{dt}
=\frac{11D_\eta}{6Wv}.
\]

Thus one reaction cannot independently consume material that another reaction also spends. Geometry, inventories, and interface motion are coupled through common component balances.

## 2. A completed deduction: some endpoint predictions need far fewer constitutive laws

The strongest mathematical result arose from asking a narrower operational question:

> Can completion be bounded without knowing how quickly every competing compound transports material?

Within the specified planar model, the answer is **yes**.

### Completion-dose theorem

Consider a binary system with a maintained reservoir of \(A\), finite total areal inventory \(N_B\), and any number of ordered line compounds whose \(B\) atomic fractions satisfy

\[
0<a=c_1<b=c_2<\cdots<c_n<1.
\]

The desired final compound is \(c_1\). Its final thickness is

\[
L=\frac{V_1N_B}{a}.
\]

Assume ordinary quasistationary diffusion and local interface equilibrium. Products are already available. After elemental \(B\) is exhausted, outer compounds may disappear successively. The nearest competitor \(c_2\) must remain adjacent to the target until the final competing phase disappears.

Let \(D_1(t)\) be the independently known integrated diffusivity of the target under those **actual neighboring phases**, and define its accumulated diffusion dose

\[
H(t)=\int_0^t D_1(s)\,ds.
\]

In the ideal limit of initially zero product thickness,

\[
\boxed{
\frac{a(b-a)}{2b}L^2
\;\leq\;
H(t_{\mathrm{complete}})
\;\leq\;
\frac{a(1-a)}{2}L^2.
}
\]

The interval contains **none of the other compounds’ transport coefficients**. Those coefficients can vary with time and history along admissible trajectories. The number and molar volumes of the additional compounds also disappear from the bound.

The theorem does not discover which phases can form. Its ordered phase family and admissible interface topology are assumptions. Nor does it transfer a measured \(D_1\) to different neighboring chemical potentials. If a new phase changes the target’s immediate neighbor, the corresponding target law must be known separately.

### Why the elimination works

The useful operation is a weighted amount that remains continuous when allowed interfaces disappear.

Write the physical thickness of phase \(i\) as \(h_i\), and introduce amount-scaled widths

\[
\ell_i=\frac{V_1}{V_i}h_i,
\qquad
d_i=\left(\frac{V_1}{V_i}\right)^2D_i.
\]

Ordinary interface balances become

\[
\dot{\boldsymbol{\ell}}
=C\left(\frac{d_i}{\ell_i}\right)_{i=1}^n,
\]

where \(C\) is tridiagonal. With \(c_0=0,c_{n+1}=1\),

\[
C_{ii}
=\frac{1}{c_i-c_{i-1}}
+\frac{1}{c_{i+1}-c_i},
\qquad
C_{i,i+1}
=-\frac{1}{c_{i+1}-c_i}.
\]

Its inverse has the explicit form

\[
G_{ij}
=\min(c_i,c_j)\,[1-\max(c_i,c_j)].
\]

The first row therefore defines

\[
Z=(G\boldsymbol{\ell})_1
=a\sum_i(1-c_i)\ell_i.
\]

All competing fluxes cancel from its derivative:

\[
\dot Z=\frac{D_1}{h_1}.
\]

Consequently,

\[
dH=h_1\,dZ.
\]

This is an exact identity of the ordinary model. It is not a fitted clock.

Finite inventory and nonnegative phase amounts enclose the target thickness between two functions of \(Z\):

\[
\max\!\left(
0,
\frac{bZ-a^2(1-b)L}{a(b-a)}
\right)
\leq h_1
\leq
\frac{Z}{a(1-a)}.
\]

At completion,

\[
Z_f=a(1-a)L.
\]

Integrating the two envelopes against \(dZ\) gives the stated bounds. An outer phase contributes zero to \(Z\) when it disappears, so the quantity stays continuous through successive allowed interface removals.

The upper bound is also a stopping certificate **inside the model**. Every incomplete state has \(Z<Z_f\); it cannot accumulate more dose than the upper envelope permits before reaching completion.

The report includes the corresponding formula for positive, measured initial product layers. That version avoids treating zero-thickness continuum growth as a prediction of nucleation.

### What this changes

The theorem separates two questions that ordinarily receive the same large constitutive input set:

- **Detailed phase trajectories** generally require the individual transport laws.
- **A guaranteed interval for final conversion** can require only the target law, total inventory, and nearest competing composition, subject to the stated topology.

Adding more \(B\)-rich intermediate compounds does not by itself widen the interval. The ratio of upper to lower bounds is

\[
\frac{b(1-a)}{b-a}.
\]

It becomes large when the nearest competitor has a composition close to the target. Thus the nearest composition gap controls the uncertainty left after all competing rates are eliminated.

For Cu₃Sn/Cu₆Sn₅,

\[
a=\frac14,\qquad b=\frac5{11},
\]

and the bound becomes

\[
\frac9{160}L^2
\le H_{\mathrm{complete}}
\le\frac3{32}L^2.
\]

The ratio is \(5/3\). For an ideal one-front, 10 µm final target and \(D_1=1.85\times10^{-17}\,\mathrm{m^2/s}\), this corresponds to **84.46–140.77 hours**. That is a model consequence; no such film was prepared here.

The proof is analytic. Numerical cases with 2, 3, 5, and 8 compounds, changing rates, and explicit phase disappearance check the implementation but are not evidence of an empirical phenomenon.

### Novelty boundary

General coupled compound growth, finite-layer disappearance, and quadratic diffusion identities are established. In particular, Gusak and Storozhuk derive a related penetration identity involving the phase coefficients, and ordinary thin-joint models already treat finite Sn and Cu substrates. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="1"}

I did not find this particular competitor-free completion interval, with arbitrary admissible rate histories and successive outer deletions, in the sources examined. That is not an exhaustive priority claim.

An equally informed ordinary solver has the same physics. The earned consequence is **reduced input requirements for a bounded endpoint question**, rather than improved empirical accuracy or a new constitutive law.
