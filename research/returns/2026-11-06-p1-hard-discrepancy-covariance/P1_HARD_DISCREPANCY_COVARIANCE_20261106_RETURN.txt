## 1. The scientific question

The question I chose was:

> **When can a fractional feasible design be converted into a distribution of discrete feasible designs that preserves the design exactly in expectation, satisfies prescribed hard measurement limits at every outcome, and avoids excessive variance in every other linear measurement—and can that distribution actually be constructed?**

The joint requirement matters. A distribution can preserve every marginal while creating large correlations. A different distribution can control variance while occasionally violating the prescribed hard limits. Having both distributions separately does not supply one distribution with both properties.

There is a simple illustration on the Boolean cube. Start at \(x=(1/2,\ldots,1/2)\), and require zero error in the measurements
\[
\frac{z_i-z_{i+1}}2.
\]
Every permissible outcome must then have all coordinates equal. Exact marginals force the equal mixture of \(0^n\) and \(1^n\), whose relative covariance factor is \(n\). Allowing measurement error \(1/2\) admits the independent law, with factor one. Thus imposing a hard condition can substantially change uncertainty in other directions.

The proposed result identifies a geometric condition under which **bounded hard discrepancy and bounded relative covariance remain compatible in one constructible law**.

## 2. The proposed theorem

Let
\[
P=\operatorname{conv}(V),\qquad V\subseteq\{0,1\}^n,
\]
be the polytope of feasible discrete designs. Define
\[
k(P)=\max\{\|u-v\|_1:[u,v]\text{ is an actual edge of }P\}.
\]

This is the largest number of coordinates changed along a polytope edge. It concerns the geometry of the feasible set, rather than the distance between arbitrary feasible designs.

The inputs are:

| Input | Required contract |
|---|---|
| Fractional design | A rational point \(x\in P\) |
| Measurements | An explicit rational matrix \(A\in\mathbb Q^{m\times n}\), with each column having Euclidean norm at most one |
| Geometry | An integer upper bound \(k\geq k(P)\) |
| Computational access | An exact strong linear-optimization oracle for \(P\), returning an optimal Boolean vertex for any rational linear objective |

Every oracle call, objective encoding, measurement row, and input-acquisition cost is charged.

**Proposed joint rounding theorem.** There is a deterministic algorithm with polynomial bit complexity, relative to that oracle, which constructs a finite rational law on feasible vertices \(Z\) such that
\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E Z&=x,\\
\|A(Z-x)\|_\infty&\leq1024k
&&\text{at every supported outcome},\\
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)&\preceq256kD_x,
& D_x&=\operatorname{diag}\bigl(x_j(1-x_j)\bigr).
\end{aligned}}
\]

The support contains at most
\[
\frac{n(n+1)}2+1
\]
vertices, and every supported vertex lies in the minimal face of \(P\) containing \(x\).

The covariance statement means that, under this **same law**, every fixed linear measurement \(b\) satisfies
\[
\operatorname{Var}(b^\top Z)
\leq256k\sum_j b_j^2x_j(1-x_j).
\]
The expression on the right is the variance of independent Bernoulli rounding, multiplied by \(256k\), even when independent rounding would violate feasibility.

The law depends on the supplied \(A\) and \(x\). The theorem does not assert one law with hard bounds for every possible measurement matrix. Its covariance guarantee applies to all fixed linear queries under the law that was constructed.

The report also removes the need to obtain an edge-length certificate beforehand. A bounded sequence of guesses, with exact output verification, gives the guarantees
\[
\|A(Z-x)\|_\infty\leq2048k(P),
\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)\preceq512k(P)D_x.
\]
A failed guess is not treated as evidence about the true edge length.

### Why the dependence on \(k\) is substantial

Both dependences are necessary up to universal constants.

Take the midpoint of an exposed edge \([u,v]\) that changes \(k\) coordinates. Any exact-mean vertex law at that midpoint must place probability \(1/2\) on each endpoint.

A suitably signed measurement row then has discrepancy \(k/2\). On the changed coordinates,
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)=\frac14ss^\top,
\qquad
D_x=\frac14I,
\]
where \(s=v-u\) has \(k\) entries of magnitude one. Therefore the smallest possible relative covariance factor is exactly \(k\).

Consequently, the proposed theorem gives a general characterization:

> **Uniformly bounded edge Hamming length is necessary and sufficient, up to universal constants, for uniformly bounded hard discrepancy and relative covariance to coexist in an exact-mean rounding law.**

The sufficiency includes a finite construction under the stated optimization interface.

## 4. How the joint construction works

The central operation is a favorable response to an arbitrary linear-plus-quadratic test.

For every rational vector \(c\) and rational positive semidefinite matrix \(Q\), the construction returns a feasible vertex \(v\) with the hard discrepancy guarantee and
\[
\boxed{
c^\top(v-x)+(v-x)^\top Q(v-x)
\leq65k\,\operatorname{tr}(QD_x).
}
\]

This operation uses the original **linear** optimization oracle. It does not assume quadratic optimization over \(P\), optimization over the unknown set of good vertices, or a sampler for that set.

The proof has five connected parts.

### A. Short edges leave enough feasible directions

Suppose the current minimal face has \(s\) varying coordinates. Its dimension satisfies
\[
\dim F\geq\frac{s}{k}.
\]

To see this, choose a basis of incident edge directions at a vertex of the face. Their supports must cover every varying coordinate. Each basis direction changes at most \(k\) coordinates, so \(s\leq k\dim F\).

This gives a quantitative supply of directions in which the current point can move while staying in the original face.

The face itself is recoverable from the original optimization interface. For a point \(y\) in sign coordinates, the symmetric core
\[
\mathcal P\cap(2y-\mathcal P)
\]
has the same affine hull as the minimal face containing \(y\). This avoids assuming access to a facet list or a separate face oracle.

### B. A quadratic compensation term fits inside that supply

Write
\[
y=2z-\mathbf1,\qquad y^0=2x-\mathbf1.
\]
For a positive semidefinite test matrix \(Q\), introduce
\[
J_Q(y)
=
(y-y^0)^\top Q(y-y^0)
+
64k\sum_jQ_{jj}(1-y_j^2).
\]

The first term measures accumulated quadratic error. The second records a budget associated with coordinates that remain fractional.

Its Hessian is
\[
\nabla^2J_Q
=
2\bigl(Q-64k\,\operatorname{diag}(Q_{jj})\bigr).
\]

On \(s\) active coordinates, this matrix has at most \(s/(64k)\) positive directions. The reason is a diagonal normalization: the normalized positive semidefinite matrix has trace at most \(s\), so it cannot have more than \(s/(64k)\) eigenvalues above \(64k\).

We can therefore restrict movement to a rational subspace on which \(J_Q\) has nonpositive curvature. Adding the equation
\[
\nabla J_Q^\top h=0
\]
makes \(J_Q\) nonincreasing in **both** directions along the entire feasible line. This is exact because \(J_Q\) is quadratic.

The hard-discrepancy mechanism also consumes dimensions. With the parameters used in the report, the remaining dimension is at least
\[
\frac{s}{k}
-\frac{10s}{R^2}
-\frac{s}{64k}
-3
\geq
\frac{23s}{64k}-3,
\qquad R^2\geq16k.
\]
It stays positive while \(s>16k\).

That inequality is the compatibility calculation: the hard constraints, spectral descent, linear cost, and quadratic budget can all be enforced by the same movement.

### C. The descent has a finite-bit implementation

An infinitesimal direction would not suffice. The report supplies the steps needed to turn it into a finite algorithm.

A positive matrix ridge gives a uniform spectral gap. Derivatives of the spectral potential are computed to certified rational accuracy. A shifted-Hessian calculation then finds a rational direction with a strict negative-curvature margin.

Each move either takes a prescribed small step or reaches an endpoint in the current original face. Endpoint moves strictly reduce face dimension. There is no assumed lower bound on their length.

After a move, the algorithm solves an exact LP inside a small enclosing dyadic box, preserving the original face, the original linear-cost equation, and the relevant original measurement equations. This bounds denominator growth.

Two details are essential:

- A face can shrink while every coordinate remains fractional. The progress argument counts original-face losses.
- Rounding coordinates independently to dyadic numbers can violate exact equalities. The repair LP can retain a coordinate such as \(1/3\) while still controlling its encoding length.

The walk terminates when at most \(16k\) coordinates vary. At that point, ordinary exact convex decomposition supplies a finite terminal law. Its remaining quadratic error is covered by the compensation budget. Selecting a favorable atom yields the displayed linear-plus-quadratic response.

### D. Favorable responses produce one common law

Let \(G\) be the feasible vertices satisfying the hard discrepancy bound. Associate to each such vertex
\[
d_v=v-x,\qquad K_v=d_vd_v^\top.
\]

If there were no law on \(G\) with mean \(x\) and sufficiently small covariance, convex separation would produce a linear-plus-quadratic test violated by every good vertex. The favorable-response construction contradicts that separator.

This establishes existence. Finding explicit probabilities requires more.

The report gives a quantitative alternative for a finite set of collected vertices: if that set cannot support an exact-mean law with covariance at most \(128kD_x\), then its separating system contains a ball of known positive radius inside a known outer ball.

An ellipsoid procedure repeatedly asks for favorable responses. Each response supplies another good vertex and cuts the separating region. After polynomially many cuts, a separating ball could no longer remain. The collected vertices must therefore support the required law.

This closes the gap between “a small-support law exists” and “the vertices supporting such a law can be found.”

### E. Exact means and rational probabilities survive extraction

The final probability computation keeps
\[
\sum_v\lambda_v=1,\qquad
\sum_v\lambda_vv=x
\]
as exact rational equations.

It does not assume that an arbitrary feasible semidefinite problem has a conveniently encoded rational solution at its boundary. Instead, the construction proves an interior margin by mixing with a relative-interior point of the exact-mean probability polytope. The larger target \(256kD_x\) supplies explicit covariance slack.

A rational feasibility procedure then returns the weights. Exact linear algebra compresses the support while preserving first and second moments, yielding the bound \(n(n+1)/2+1\).

The constants \(65,96,128,256\) have distinct roles in this conversion. Their gaps provide room for separation, finite precision, and rational extraction.

## 5. Deductions beyond the main statement

### All quadratic risks under the same law

For every positive semidefinite \(Q\),
\[
\mathbb E\bigl[(Z-x)^\top Q(Z-x)\bigr]
\leq256k\,\operatorname{tr}(QD_x).
\]

For every fixed real matrix \(B\),
\[
\mathbb E\|B(Z-x)\|_2^2
\leq256k\,\operatorname{tr}(BD_xB^\top).
\]

Thus the law controls expected squared error for any later fixed collection of linear measurements, while its supplied hard bounds remain valid at every outcome. Averaging independent draws divides the covariance by the number of draws.

These are second-moment statements. Gaussian tails, Bernstein tails, negative dependence, and convex-order domination require additional arguments.

### Exact affine constraints improve the covariance bound

Suppose the initial face satisfies
\[
H(Z-x)=0.
\]
Then the covariance bound improves to
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)
\preceq
256k\left[
D_x-D_xH^\top(HD_xH^\top)^\dagger HD_x
\right].
\]

The derivation is direct: adding \(H^\top t\) to a query vector does not change its realized error, so minimize the original variance bound over \(t\).

This removes variance in directions prohibited by feasibility. The projection principle is standard; it is a deduction from the joint law here.

### Arbitrary matroid bases

Matroid base-polytope edges change two coordinates. Substituting \(k=2\) gives
\[
\mathbb EZ=x,\qquad
\|A(Z-x)\|_\infty\leq2048,\qquad
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)\preceq512D_x.
\]

An independence oracle supplies the required linear optimization through greedy optimization. No representation or strong base-orderability assumption is introduced.

The constants are conservative. For nonnegative weighted columns, existing strongly polynomial matroid discrepancy bounds can be much more attractive in relevant parameter ranges; the report retains that comparison. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="2"}

### Integral base polyhedra

The existing adjacent-cell reduction extends the joint result to integer-valued submodular base polyhedra.

Writing
\[
p=x-\lfloor x\rfloor,
\]
the output has the exact mean \(x\), each coordinate lies in its adjacent integer pair, and
\[
\operatorname{Cov}(Z)
\preceq512\,\operatorname{diag}\bigl(p_j(1-p_j)\bigr).
\]

The corresponding hard discrepancy bound is \(2048\). Tight original constraints remain tight. The reduction and its oracle requirements are credited to prior work; the computational accounting uses the bit lengths of capacities and function values.

## 6. An exact example with an optimal alternative

I worked out the complete tradeoff for a small spanning-tree problem.

Take the six edges of \(K_4\), with fractional mean \(x_e=1/2\). For each of the four triangles, use a measurement equal to half the number of selected triangle edges. Every measurement column has norm \(1/\sqrt2\), so the normalization is satisfied.

There are four star trees and twelve Hamilton-path trees. The exact comparisons are:

| Law | Probability assignment | Maximum triangle discrepancy | Smallest \(\beta\) with \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z)\preceq\beta D_x\) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| Uniform spanning tree | \(1/16\) on each tree | \(3/4\) | \(3/2\) |
| Optimal covariance law | \(1/10\) on each star; \(1/20\) on each path | \(3/4\) | **\(6/5\)** |
| Tight hard-bound law | \(1/12\) on each path | **\(1/4\)** | **\(2\)** |

All three laws have exactly the same edge means.

The optimal covariance law has
\[
C_{\mathrm{opt}}
=
\frac3{10}I-\frac1{20}J.
\]
Its factor \(6/5\) is globally optimal: every exact-mean spanning-tree covariance has trace \(3/2\) and annihilates the all-ones vector. Its largest eigenvalue is therefore at least \((3/2)/5=3/10\). The displayed law attains that bound.

If the hard radius is below \(3/4\), stars are forbidden. Symmetry averaging shows that the uniform path law minimizes the largest covariance eigenvalue among the remaining exact-mean laws. Its optimal factor is two.

The complete frontier is therefore
\[
\beta_{\min}(r)=
\begin{cases}
\text{infeasible},&r<1/4,\\
2,&1/4\leq r<3/4,\\
6/5,&r\geq3/4.
\end{cases}
\]

The bundle verifies the distributions and covariance certificates with exact rational arithmetic. This example illustrates the compatibility issue and compares against an optimal alternative; it is not presented as execution of the general algorithm or as a separate priority claim.
