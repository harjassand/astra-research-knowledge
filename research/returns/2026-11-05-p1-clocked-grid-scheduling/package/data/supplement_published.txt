Supplementary Figure 2.
Comparison of waveforms for all grids that exhibit a 1 min pattern
averaged over all available data. For most grids (Great Britain, Australia, US West, US East,
Malaysia, South Korea, South Africa, Mallorca, Ireland and Iceland), the frequency drop occurs
at the start of each full minute.
This initial drop supports the hypothesis that the pattern is
driven by recurring peaks of the power demand in the beginning of the minute associated with
clocked control algorithms. In the Continental European and Scandinavian grids, the frequency
also decreases shortly after the start of each hour, but more gradually, reaching a minimum after
approximately 30 s.

Supplementary Figure 3.
The 1 min pattern in the secondary control setpoint in Germany. We
analyse the time series of the automatic frequency restoration reserve (aFRR) setpoint in the
German grid for the year 2025. The data are publicly available at 1 s resolution [1]. a, The Fourier
spectrum of the aFRR setpoint exhibits a narrow peak at a period of 1 min. b, The monthly

follows the corresponding pattern in the frequency signal (cf. Main Text). The solid line shows the
mean hourly amplitude, and the shaded area indicates ±1 standard deviation.

Supplementary Figure 8.
Frequency response to a power imbalance leading to a typical deter-
ministic frequency deviation (DFD). a, The power imbalance ∆Pext(t) increases rapidly in the
beginning of a 15 min market interval due to generation ramps and decreases slowly afterwards.
We consider the case of a pure ramp (solid lines) and a ramp with a weak superimposed cosine
signal with a period of 60 s (dashed line, inset) modelling a periodic power imbalance due to clocked
devices. b, Frequency response ∆f(t) to the power imbalance for D = 0.1 and D = 1. Without a
periodic driver (left panels), the ramping of ∆Pext(t) excites a resonance oscillation with a period
of approx. 60 s, but this is rapidly damped out even for D = 0.1. Importantly, the oscillations are
phase-shifted by 180◦depending on whether the power imbalance ramp is upwards or downwards.
With a small periodic driver superimposed to the ramp (right panels), the oscillations no longer
decay, and a stable phase emerges toward the end of the market interval that is independent of the
direction of the ramp.

Supplementary Figure 9. Frequency response to ramps in the power imbalances with noise. a, We
consider power imbalances ∆Pext(t) leading to characteristic DFDs with additive noise, comparing
cases with and without a weak addition signal modelling a periodic power imbalance due to clocked
devices. The upper panel shows a single 15 min market interval with a strong initial ramp and
noise in ∆Pext(t). The lower panel shows a sequence of ramps of ∆Pext(t) in consecutive market
intervals with varying amplitude and direction with superimposed noise.
b: Response of the
frequency deviation ∆f(t) for a single market interval.
The solid line shows the response to
∆Pext(t) including noise, while the dashed line corresponds to the noise-free case. In the absence
of a periodic driver (left panel), oscillations with a period of 60 s can be excited, but these are short
lived and incoherent. In the presence of a periodic driver (right panel), oscillations are stabilized
both in amplitude and phase. c: Fourier spectrum of the frequency response ∆f(t) for several
consecutive market intervals. In the absence of a periodic driver (left panel), no pronounced 60 s
peak is visible. In the presence of a periodic driver (right panel), a clear peak emerges at a period
of 60 s.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 1: THE 1 min PATTERN IN THE AFRR-SETPOINT
IN GERMANY
Automatic frequency restoration reserve (aFRR) corresponds to secondary frequency con-
trol in the European power system.
The aFRR process aims to restore the Frequency
Restoration Control Error (FRCE) to zero and thereby return the system frequency to its
nominal value [9]. The corresponding aFRR demand is computed by the transmission system
operators (TSOs) responsible for balancing the respective load-frequency-control areas and
depends on both frequency deviations and unscheduled power exchanges with neighbouring
areas. In practice, the resulting control actions are further modified by imbalance netting
within the International Grid Control Cooperation, which reduces simultaneous reserve ac-
tivations in opposite directions across Europe.
Here, we analyse publicly available aFRR setpoint data for the four German control
areas from 2025 at a temporal resolution of 1 s [1]. For simplicity, the setpoints of the four
German TSOs are aggregated into a single time series (Supplementary Fig. 3).
Fourier
analysis reveals a pronounced and highly coherent spectral peak at exactly 1 min.
The
corresponding waveform is approximately sinusoidal with minor differences between the
months of the year. The daily profile of the signal amplitude closely follows the respective
curve for the frequency signal amplitude.
The observed oscillation does not directly imply substantial additional activation costs
for aFRR because reserve providers are remunerated both for reserved capacity and for
activated balancing energy, while positive and negative energy activations approximately
compensate over time. However, recurrent periodic changes in power output can contribute
to wear and tear of turbines and other assets participating in frequency control [10].

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 2: ULTRALOW FREQUENCY OSCILLATIONS
AND RESONANCES IN HYDRO POWER PLANTS
Hydro power plants have been identified as contributing factors of slow frequency oscil-
lations [2, 11–13]. These studies suggest that slow periodic oscillations with periods around
one minute can arise from resonance effects in turbine–governor dynamics of hydro power
plants participating in load-frequency control.
In this work, we adopt the turbine governor model proposed by Odelbrink [2] and in-
vestigate its dynamic behaviour.
This model allows us to isolate resonance effects and
demonstrate that the resonance alone cannot explain the properties of the observed 1 min
frequency pattern. Instead, we show that a coherent periodic power imbalance is required
to explain the observed long-term stable 1 min pattern.
An overview of the literature on the topic is provided at the end of this Supplementary
Note.

C.
Numerical Results
1.
Frequency Response at a period of 60 s
Figure 5 shows the Bode diagram of the full hydro closed-loop system (2). A pronounced
resonance peak at a period of approximately 60 s is clearly visible, whose magnitude depends
on the damping parameter D. In addition, the resonance behavior is strongly influenced
by the attenuation factor c. The largest frequency response at the 60 s period is observed
around c = 0.11, see Fig. 6a. This behavior can be understood by inspecting the Bode
diagrams for different values of c, shown in Fig. 6b.
For c = 0, no load-frequency control is active, and low-frequency disturbances are not sup-
pressed, leading to large and persistent frequency deviations. As c increases, low-frequency
disturbances are increasingly damped. However, this comes at the cost of an emerging feed-
back effect that leads to the resonance peak with a period on the order of 60 s. Moreover,
the location of this peak shifts towards higher frequencies as c increases, illustrating how
the strength and position of the resonance depend on the amount of hydro power reserved
for load-frequency control.
The extended system (3) allows us to continuously interpolate between the pure hydro
system (h = 1) and a fully synthetic PI-controlled system (h = 0). Increasing the hydro
share h leads to a monotonic increase in the frequency response at the 60 s period, see
Fig. 7a. This trend can likewise be explained by the corresponding Bode diagrams, see
Fig. 7b. The generic PI controller exhibits a weaker resonance at higher frequencies and
does not reproduce the pronounced peak observed in the hydro-based system.
44

Although the generic PI controller is a simplified representation and does not fully capture
the dynamics of other power plants participating in load-frequency control, it nevertheless
illustrates how the dispatch of hydro power influences resonance phenomena. In particular,
the amplitude of the system response to disturbances with a characteristic period of 60 s
depends sensitively on the composition of control resources, especially on the share of hydro
units contributing to frequency control.
2.
Time Domain Simulations
We next simulate the frequency response in the time domain under two scenarios for
the external power imbalance ∆Pext(t). We show results for D = 1 and D = 0.1, where
the latter value corresponds to an artificially weak damping. This parameter is chosen for
illustrative purposes, as resonance effects are particularly pronounced.
First, we consider a power imbalance ∆Pext(t) that leads to a typical deterministic fre-
quency deviation (DFD). We assume that generation is strongly ramped at the beginning
of 15 min market interval leading to a rapid increase or decrease of the power imbalance
∆Pext(t), which gradually returns to zero afterwards (cf. Fig. 8a). Second, we consider the
same overall power imbalance ∆Pext(t), but add a small periodic modulation with a period of
60 s, representing a periodic power imbalance due to clocked devices (dashed line in Fig. 8a)
In the absence of the 60 s modulation, a transient 60 s resonance oscillation is excited, but
it is rapidly damped. Moreover, the oscillations generated during ramp-up and ramp-down
DFDs are phase-shifted by 180◦.
As a consequence, such resonance-induced oscillations
cancel out when averaged over multiple market intervals and therefore (1) do not appear
as a stable peak in long-term Fourier spectra and (2) are not consistent with the actual
frequency time series, cf. the daily profile in the Nordic grid (Fig. 1).
In contrast, when a periodic 60 s power imbalance is present, a persistent and phase-
coherent 60 s frequency pattern emerges. Crucially, towards the end of the market interval
no phase shift is observed between the oscillations generated by initial ramp-up and ramp-
down of the power imbalance. This demonstrates that a weak external periodic driver can
introduce a stable, coherent 60 s oscillation of the grid frequency, while a pure resonance
effect cannot explain the stability of the signal amplitude and phase.
Finally, we also add noise to the power imbalances ∆Pext(t) (Fig. 9). We first consider the
scenario without the 60 s modulation. Interestingly, we observe a transient ∼60 s pattern in
the frequency response on short time scales. However, these patterns are not phase coherent.
Evaluating the response to a sequence of DFD inducing power ramps, the Fourier spectrum
shows no distinct peak at a period of 60 s in contrast to the observations. In contrast, when
a small signal with a period of 60 s is added to ∆Pext(t), a clear and stable peak at a period
of 60 s emerges in the Fourier spectrum.
In conclusion, resonance effects alone cannot explain the observed 60 s patterns observed
45

in grid frequency time series worldwide. Without an external driver, oscillations lack phase-
coherence and long-time stability. In contrast, a weak periodic and phase-coherent compo-
nent in the power imbalance readily explains the observed stable 60 s pattern in the grid
frequency. Resonance effects can amplify this signal depending on the design and parameters
of the control system.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 3: GBT MODEL FOR THE HOURLY AMPLITUDES
IN THE CE GRID
We investigate the role of potential contributing factors to the 1 min pattern using meth-
ods from eXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) [24, 25]. We train and analyze Gradient
Boosted Tree (GBT) models to predict the hourly amplitudes pattern in the CE grid. GBTs
are lightweight to train [26], provide state-of-the-art performance for tabular data [27], and
offer intrinsic interpretability [28].
Two models are considered, each using a different feature set. The actual model includes
hourly aggregated load and generation by technology type (e.g., solar, gas, nuclear), as well
as derived features such as generation ramps, defined as the difference in generation between
two consecutive hours. The full model extends this feature set by incorporating day-ahead
forecasts and day-ahead prices, together with additional derived features, including forecast
errors and artificial temporal encodings for hour of day, weekday, and month. The data for
the techno-economic features were taken from the ENTSO-E transparency platform [29],
following the procedure outlined in [30].
Model predictions are interpreted using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [28],
computed exclusively on the test set. This approach has previously been applied successfully
to explain DFDs and control activation mechanisms [30, 31].
The results (cf. Fig. 10) show that hourly amplitudes can be partially predicted from
techno-economic features. Both models achieve similar predictive performance (R2 = 0.46
for the actual model and R2 = 0.47 for the full model) and outperform the daily profile
benchmark, which is based on historical information, on the unseen test set (panel a).
However, the feature importance ranking of the full model, computed using mean absolute
SHAP values, reveals that the daily profile remains largely unexplained, as the hour of day
is the dominant feature. This suggests that the actual model instead reconstructs the hourly
profile through a combination of multiple techno-economic variables (panel b). Nevertheless,
both models consistently identify hydro, solar, nuclear, and gas generation among the most
influential features.
SHAP dependence plots for the full model (panel c) indicate that higher renewable gen-
eration, particularly solar, pumped hydro, and reservoir hydro, is associated with stronger
amplitudes, reflected by positive SHAP values. In contrast, conventional generation sources
such as nuclear and gas exhibit a negative correlation between generation levels and SHAP
values, indicating a damping effect on the amplitudes. The findings for solar, nuclear, and
gas are consistent with the role of inertia in damping low-frequency oscillations.
The positive correlation observed for hydro generation suggests that resonance effects, as
discussed in the main text, contribute to the signal. We find that SHAP values for both
pumped hydro and reservoir hydro generation are on the order of ±0.1 mHz. Most inter-
estingly, the partial dependency plot for pumped hydro generation shows a sharp decrease
when the feature value drops below 2 GW while it is largely constant above this value. This
48

can be interpreted as a step-wise contribution to the signal amplitude, with negative SHAP
values if most turbines are offline and positive SHAP values when most turbines are online
but independent of the actual generation.
The results of our data-driven analysis thus lead to the conclusion that hydro power
contributes to the signal amplitude in the CE grid, albeit at a moderate scale of ±0.1 mHz.
Given the results of the mechanistic model, hydro power should be viewed as an amplifying
factor rather than the cause of the observed pattern.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 4: THE IMPACT OF COVID-19 ON THE 1 min PAT-

The COVID-19 pandemic had a strong impact on the economic activities and daily life
across the globe and thus also affected the way electric power is consumed. The pandemic

remains high and decreases only later during the year.

the pattern amplitude. Large computing centres typically operate uninterruptible power

appears possible that increased internet traffic correlates with a stronger use of UPSs.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 5: REVISION OF THE PRIMARY CONTROL RE-
QUIREMENTS IN THE SCANDINAVIAN GRID
In this section, we discuss the revision of the Frequency Containment Reserve (FCR)
requirements in the Nordic synchronous area, comprising Norway, Sweden, Finland, and
eastern Denmark. In the main text, we use the term Scandinavian power grid for readability,
although the formally correct designation used by ENTSO-E is Nordic synchronous area.
Around 2010, the amplitude of the coherent ultra-low-frequency oscillation (ULFO) mode
in the Scandinavian grid regularly exceeded 10 mHz. However, the amplitudes reported in
this work are defined for the coherent waveform reconstructed over individual one-hour in-
tervals and therefore characterize only the long-term coherent component of the oscillation.
Larger transient deviations may occur during shorter oscillatory events or following distur-
bances [17].
At the same time, increasing frequency fluctuations and decreasing system inertia became
a growing operational concern for the Scandinavian transmission system operators (TSOs).
The Nordic power system has long been known to exhibit poorly damped low-frequency
oscillatory behaviour, and a substantial body of literature discusses resonance phenomena
in hydroelectric power plants and the associated mechanical wear of hydraulic turbines, wa-
terways, and governor systems (cf. the literature review above). Against this background,
the Scandinavian TSOs initiated a major revision of the Frequency Containment Reserve
(FCR) framework and corresponding prequalification procedures. According to the explana-
tory documents accompanying the revised methodology, the previously implemented FCR
requirements had been designed for a system with substantially higher inertia, whereas lower
inertia conditions increase the need for fast and well-damped frequency control responses.
The revised requirements therefore explicitly aimed to improve the dynamic performance of
FCR provision and to ensure sufficient damping of slow frequency oscillations with charac-
teristic periods around one minute.
The revised technical requirements, implemented in 2023, introduced substantially
stricter prequalification criteria for participating FCR providers [32].
In particular, the
new framework added explicit requirements concerning dynamic performance, linearity, and
oscillatory stability. Providers must now pass standardized sine-response tests and dynamic
ramp tests designed to evaluate the response of the reserve-providing units over oscillatory
timescales including periods around one minute. The revised rules furthermore require that
FCR responses contribute positively to the stabilization and damping of system frequency
oscillations. Additional linearity requirements were introduced to reduce dead bands, back-
lash, and nonlinear response characteristics that can otherwise amplify oscillatory behaviour.
The revised framework also distinguishes between dynamic and static variants of distur-
bance reserves (FCR-D), ensuring that a sufficiently large fraction of reserves is capable of
continuously following rapid frequency variations under low-inertia conditions. Overall, the
revised Scandinavian FCR framework can therefore be interpreted as a direct operational
51

response to the increasing importance of low-frequency oscillatory dynamics in the power
system.
The technical requirements for Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR) introduced in
2023 were revised in 2025 to further expand standardized dynamic performance testing of
reserve-providing units [33]. The revised requirements explicitly include sine-response tests
with oscillation periods of 50–70 seconds, i.e. on approximately one-minute timescales, and
specify detailed procedures for gain and phase characterization under different loading and
droop conditions.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 8: BACKGROUND ON BATTERY ENERGY MAN-
AGEMENT SYSTEMS
Regulatory requirements and market conventions shape the time scales on which battery
energy management systems (EMS), aggregators, and virtual power plants (VPPs) inter-
act with the grid. In European FCR frameworks, relevant response requirements operate
on time scales from seconds to tens of seconds. In Continental Europe, full FCR activa-
tion is conventionally associated with a 30 s time scale, while Nordic FCR specifications
define product-specific dynamic-performance, measurement, and verification requirements
over several time scales [32, 38]. These requirements constrain EMS and inverter-control de-
sign, because resources must respond rapidly to frequency deviations while avoiding unstable
control action, unnecessary cycling, and excessive degradation.
Some deterministic imprints in power-system frequency are well documented. ENTSO-E
reports deterministic frequency deviations in European frequency data and discusses them
mainly in relation to market schedules, dispatch changes, and ramping behaviour [39]. Kruse
et al. further show that power-grid frequency contains systematic, partly predictable struc-
tures, indicating that frequency trajectories are influenced by recurring operational patterns
[40]. These findings demonstrate that synchronized operational processes can become visible
in aggregate frequency measurements.
At the low-level control and measurement layer, FCR-providing resources require com-
paratively fast frequency measurement and response capabilities. NREL studies show that
distributed inverter-based resources, including storage inverters, can provide fast frequency
support through functions such as frequency-watt control [41]. In contrast, higher-level EMS
functions such as SOC management, recharge scheduling, ramp-rate constraints, and aggre-
gator dispatch are implementation-dependent and may operate at slower, discrete update
intervals.
Therefore, recurring minute-scale components in frequency measurements should be inter-
preted as potential signatures of synchronized control rather than as generic measurement
artifacts. In the context of the present study, UPS battery systems emerge as a plausi-
ble contributor to such periodic forcing.
One possible mechanism is periodic diagnostic
or health-management activity within UPS and battery EMS, including self-tests, state-of-
health checks, recharge control, or brief supervisory adjustments of charging and discharging
states.
Such minute-scale diagnostic routines are explicitly documented by multiple commercial
UPS manufacturers. For example, one manufacturer states that the battery circuit is tested
automatically every 60 s to detect open circuits or increased resistance [42]. Another specifies
that battery testing is performed automatically every 60 s for basic battery diagnostics,
complemented by a battery-efficiency test every 220 min during float charging [43]. Similar
periodic battery-connection self-tests have also been described for other commercial UPS
systems [44]. Although these documents describe individual products rather than the entire
59

UPS market, they demonstrate that minute-scale diagnostic routines are implemented by
multiple independent vendors and therefore represent a plausible physical mechanism for
synchronized minute-scale power perturbations when executed across large populations of
devices.
While the power perturbation of an individual UPS system is small, similar EMS routines
executed across large fleets of devices could aggregate into measurable frequency-domain
features if their timing is synchronized by firmware defaults, communication cycles, clock-
based scheduling, or aggregator-level coordination. This makes UPS battery EMS timing a
credible mechanism for shaping minute-scale grid-frequency dynamics.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 9: IMPACT OF CONTROL SOFTWARE CHANGES
FOR UPS AT BUILDING 3
The UPS at Building 3 offers a unique opportunity for detailed analysis and monitoring.
A wide range of measurements is available, including DC current measurements downstream
of the three inverters and directly upstream of the batteries, as well as AC measurements
both before and after the UPS, enabling isolation and characterization of the UPS behavior
on the AC level itself. In addition, a power quality (PQ) meter (ID 023) is installed at
the transformer level (see Table 1 and Fig. 5a in the main manuscript for an overview
of the electrical layout).
Furthermore, several operational parameters of the battery, in
particular, the EMS, can be actively controlled, and we maintain continuous exchange with
the manufacturers, allowing for deeper system insights and coordinated investigations.
The comprehensive measurement and control infrastructure enables targeted investiga-
tions of the UPS operating behavior and, in particular, the role of clock synchronization
within the battery energy management system (EMS). To assess the influence of network
time synchronization, we conducted comparative measurements under two conditions: (i)
with NTP synchronization disabled and (ii) with NTP synchronization enabled on the bat-
tery EMS.
We find a remarkable difference between the operation with and without NTP synchro-
nization. In both cases, the daily waveform measured at the Transformer progresses from
day to day, but the magnitude of the progression differs considerably (Fig. 18a,b).
Re-
markably, the phase progression is much larger with NTP synchronization enabled, with a
magnitude of approximately 5-7 seconds per day. The hourly waveforms of the power time
series at transformer level provide further insights (Fig. 18c,d). We find that the pattern
does not drift continuously, but jumps from day to day. A plausible explanation is that the
EMS reschedules its operation every day. In summary, self-tests are done periodically in
1 min intervals, but the starting point is reset from day to day.
Based on our observations, the manufacturer of the UPS at Building 3 implemented a
revised version of the algorithm used to estimate the battery’s state of charge, as part of
a maintenance intervention involving a software update for battery block 1. Following this
update, the self-tests are no longer performed strictly every minute for battery block 1, see
Fig. 31a. Instead, the tests occur at seemingly irregular time intervals, while the current is
drawn over longer durations such that the overall consumed energy remains approximately
constant. However, the start of the tests remains locked to the same second within each
minute, resulting in a quasi-periodic behavior. The other two battery blocks are not affected,
as they have not received an update yet.
This modification is clearly reflected in the Fourier spectrum of the DC current measured
at battery block 1.
Instead of a sharp spectral peak at a period of 1 min, the updated
behavior produces a significantly broader spectral feature, see Fig. 31b. Nevertheless, such
a broadband excitation may still interact with resonant processes in the power system and
61

thereby contribute to the emergence of a 1 min pattern in the grid frequency. The software
update, therefore, represents a partial mitigation step toward eliminating the source of the
observed signal, although it does not fully suppress the underlying synchronization effect.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 11: IMPACT OF IOT AND SMART DEVICES
The adoption of household Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices is increasing worldwide [47].
Many of these devices rely on internal clocks synchronized via Internet-based timing proto-
cols. As a consequence, large populations of distributed devices may execute internal control
routines, status updates, or scheduled operations in a synchronized manner. Such synchro-
nization can lead to quasi-periodic patterns in electricity consumption that may collectively
contribute to the emergence of the 1 min pattern.
Empirical evidence for such synchronized behaviour is observed in commercially available
smart household devices. For example, a smart baking oven whose internal clock is synchro-
nized via the Internet exhibits a distinct 1 min pattern in its power consumption whenever
the oven is operated using a timer function (cf. Fig. 22). The heating process is triggered
at the beginning of each minute, with the heating demand determining the intensity of
the power draw. This results in a stable, recurring 1 min power pattern. A Thermomix
also displays recurring power peaks during operation; however, these do not follow a strict
one-minute periodicity.
To further investigate synchronized timing effects in network-connected electronics, we
analyzed the power consumption of four racks containing 12 Raspberry Pis each. To prevent
the findings from being influenced by specific tasks performed with the devices, all Raspberry
Pis remained unused during the tests. This allowed us to monitor their behavior and the
corresponding power consumption due only to the basic operations of the operating system.
All racks exhibit clear periodic signatures in their Fourier spectra, most prominently at 30 s,
but also at 1 min and 2 min, see Fig. 23a. The corresponding time series reveal regularly
recurring power spikes at the beginning of the minute, see Fig. 23b. These patterns remain
stable over several days, indicating persistent and reproducible timing processes.
The analysis of the 30 s waveform further shows that three of the four racks are synchro-
nized to the beginning of the interval, and thus also to the beginning of each minute, see
Fig. 23c. Only one rack exhibits a persistent phase offset of approximately 20 s. These ob-
servations suggest that even relatively small networked computing devices can exhibit stable
and synchronized timing behaviour over extended periods. While the individual power vari-
ations are small, the rapidly growing number of IoT and network-connected devices raises
the possibility that their aggregated operation may contribute measurably to the 1 min fre-
quency pattern.
These observations highlight the need for further investigation into the behaviour and
control of smart devices, as their collective effects may pose additional challenges to fre-
quency stability.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 12: THE 1 min PATTERN IN MALLORCA
The strength of the 1 min pattern varies across power systems and fluctuates over time.
Here, we analyse the development of the signal amplitude on the Spanish island of Mallorca
from 2019 to 2023.
Mallorca is part of the Balearic synchronous area, which also includes the islands of
Menorca, Ibiza, and Formentera. This grid is connected to the Continental European (CE)
synchronous area only via a high-voltage direct current (HVDC) link [48], which allows
for power transmission but does not establish frequency synchronization between the two
systems.
In recent years, Mallorca’s electricity supply has changed considerably. Two out of four
generating units at the Es Murterar coal-fired power plant were withdrawn from regular op-
eration at the end of 2019, and the operating hours of the remaining units were reduced [49].
In 2020, demand was covered primarily by combined-cycle gas turbines and imports via the
HVDC connection [50]. Remarkably, the partial shutdown of the coal-fired power plant in
Mallorca was accompanied by an overall reduction in frequency fluctuations [50].
Figure 24 shows weekly averages of the 1 min signal amplitude together with electricity
generation from coal-fired power plants. The signal amplitude varies strongly and exhibits
a pronounced negative correlation with coal generation. In fact, average amplitudes above
3 mHz are observed only during periods without coal generation. In contrast, amplitudes
regularly drop below 2.5 mHz when coal-fired generating units are online. This observation
is particularly remarkable given the simultaneous overall reduction in frequency deviations.
We interpret these findings as follows. On the one hand, the retirement of coal-fired
generating units reduces rotational inertia, making the grid more susceptible to frequency
fluctuations caused by rapid changes in the power balance. On the other hand, the HVDC
converter station effectively contributes to secondary frequency control [51, 52], thereby re-
ducing sustained frequency deviations. In addition, the converter aggressively suppresses
frequency deviations exceeding 150 mHz [53]. As a consequence, slow and sustained fre-
quency deviations are reduced, whereas faster fluctuations, including the 1 min pattern,
become more pronounced.
Together, these observations illustrate the impact of system
inertia on the strength of the 1 min pattern.

B.
Results
1.
NPE model parameters and validation
We validate the inference in two steps. First, we compare the daily profile of the inferred
pattern amplitude A with the amplitude obtained directly from the measurements, see
Fig. 26. The two intraday trends agree closely, confirming that the neural posterior estimator
recovers the pattern amplitude reliably. Furthermore, the daily profiles of the remaining
parameters also exhibit a pronounced time-of-day dependence, see Fig. 27, and are consistent
with the estimates reported in Ref. [55]. Second, trajectories simulated with the inferred
parameters reproduce both the visual character and the autocorrelation structure of the
measured signal, see Fig. 28.
2.
Impact of increasing amplitudes on frequency dynamics
The two stability indicators respond differently to a growing amplitude as shown in
Fig. 29: the Nadir increases approximately linearly with A, whereas the MSD follows a
quadratic trend, as expected for a quantity that is quadratic in the frequency deviation.
Beyond the aggregated indicators, an increasing amplitude also reshapes the full distri-
butions. Figure 30a shows that a larger pattern amplitude broadens the distribution of
frequency deviations and, in particular, substantially increases the number of large devia-
tions relative to the empirical time series. Correspondingly, Fig. 30b shows markedly larger
frequency increments on a 10 s timescale.
For a fivefold increase of the amplitude with
respect to the value inferred from the measurements, the standard deviation of the 10 s
increments grows by a factor of 1.5.
Taken together, the growth of the Nadir, and in particular of its upper tail, and the
broadening of the frequency statistics point to the additive nature of frequency deviations:
the deterministic pattern enters the power imbalance alongside the stochastic fluctuations
and the market-driven step and drift, so all contributions superpose. At the amplitudes (up
70

to 10A) studied here, the 60 s pattern alone does not constitute an imminent threat to system
stability, as the deviations remain within normal operating conditions. Its relevance lies in
the superposition: a large pattern amplitude coinciding with a large stochastic fluctuation
or a market-boundary ramp yields larger frequency deviations, and correspondingly larger
Nadirs, than either contribution alone. The effect is therefore most pronounced in the tail
of the Nadir distribution, where the deviations approach the limits of normal operation.
Overall, the model captures the joint effect of frequency control, power imbalance, short-
term fluctuations, and the one-minute disturbance amplitude on the frequency deviations,
and thereby provides a starting point for more elaborate nonlinear descriptions [59]. A nat-
ural next step is to relate the inferred parameters, and the pattern amplitude in particular,
to techno-economic features of the power system.

SUPPLEMENTARY NOTE 14: EDR DEVICES
The Electric Data Recorder (EDR) described in Ref. [6] samples the voltage waveform
with 25 kHz. We align the samples to UTC via a GPS reference signal, and filter them
with a 200-sample Hamming window. From this oscillogram-like trace we interpolate the
timestamps of the zero-crossings (ZC). Zero-crossing with inclining and declining voltages
are separated. From the difference of subsequent ZCs with positive slope we determine the
duration of the periods to arrive at the frequency estimates. Averaging these estimates over
the desired reporting interval (1 second in this case) yields frequency time-series. Since the
grid frequency changes continuously, the ZC timestamps do not add up to full seconds (and
are shifted against UTC). Periods that overlap the left or right edge of the reporting window
are therefore included in the average with a weighing factor.
This factor is determined
according to the overlap, assuming a constant frequency during the period. See Ref. [60] for
further details and experimental verification of the method under dynamic conditions.
In order to verify the EDR frequency time series to other measurement methods, we
compare EDR recordings to openly available data from German TSOs as well as to mea-
surements by the PQ meters. We perform a comparison of these 3 different measurements in
the Continental European synchronous area for a period of 15 days, ranging from June 28,
2025 to July 12, 2025. The EDR measurement is recorded in Karlsruhe while the frequency
measurements by PQ meter are recorded in J¨ulich. The EDR as well as the measurements
by the German TSOs have a sample rate of one second, while the PQ measurements have a
sample rate of three seconds. In order to compare the data, we sample all three time series
down to a sample size of three seconds.
As observed in Ref. [34] the frequencies differ only minimally within the Continental
European Synchronous Area. We note that the various frequency measurements yield very
similar frequency data series. In Fig. 32 we observe that the Fourier spectrum looks approx-
imately identical for the three measurement recordings that are obtained by different signal
processing techniques. Furthermore, we evaluate the Pearson correlation coefficients for the
different frequency time series recorded with a PQ Meter in J¨ulich, recorded with an EDR
