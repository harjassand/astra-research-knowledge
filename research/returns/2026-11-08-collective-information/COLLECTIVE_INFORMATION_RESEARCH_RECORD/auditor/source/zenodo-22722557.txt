Abstract
We study layerwise neural representation dynamics through a coarse-grained
reduced-dynamics lens. From a Mori–Zwanzig perspective, we treat selected
representation summaries as resolved variables and the remaining degrees of free-
dom as unresolved variables whose elimination induces memory terms. This yields
an operational criterion for coarse-state quality: a useful coarse state should support
an accurate short-memory closure of the depth-wise dynamics.
We derive a local resolved–unresolved surrogate showing that short-memory clo-
sure is promoted by unresolved contraction, weak cross-subspace coupling, and
stable transport on task-relevant resolved directions. This connects coarse-state
closure to task-relevant geometry preservation without requiring global dynamical
isometry. Empirically, we fit reduced models to candidate coarse states extracted
from trained networks across synthetic tasks and architectures. We find that closure
quality is related to, but not determined by, predictive accuracy, and that the best
coarse state depends strongly on task structure and architectural bias. These results
suggest that coarse-state selection is a reduced-modeling problem rather than a
purely heuristic choice of representation summary.
1 Introduction
Deep neural networks generate highly structured internal representations, yet these representations
remain difficult to describe at the full state-space level. Existing analyses often summarize repre-
sentations through geometric statistics, similarity measures, class separation, or task-specific probes
[Raghu et al., 2017, Kornblith et al., 2019, Papyan et al., 2020]. While these approaches reveal
what representations look like, they leave open a complementary question: whether representation
evolution across depth admits a principled reduced dynamical description.
In applied mathematics and nonequilibrium statistical physics, high-dimensional systems are often
studied through coarse-graining: a small set of resolved variables is retained, while unresolved degrees
of freedom appear as memory and fluctuation terms in an effective reduced dynamics [Mori, 1965,
Zwanzig, 1961]. Motivated by this viewpoint, we study layerwise neural representation dynamics
as a coarse-grained dynamical system. Rather than treating coarse variables as ad hoc summaries,
we view them as candidate reduced states whose quality is determined by how well they close the
depth-wise dynamics.
This leads to our central question: for a given task and architecture, which coarse state yields an
effective short-memory reduced model? Framed this way, coarse-state selection becomes a reduced-
modeling problem rather than merely a choice of representation statistic. A useful coarse state
should summarize the representation and support a predictive evolution law across layers with limited
memory.

We develop a resolved–unresolved formulation of layerwise representation dynamics. Eliminating
unresolved variables induces memory terms in the effective dynamics of the resolved coarse state,
and we derive conditions under which this memory is short: unresolved directions should contract,
cross-subspace couplings should be weak, and task-relevant resolved directions should be stably
transported. This connects short-memory closure to geometry-preserving ideas such as edge-of-chaos
propagation and dynamical isometry, but in a resolved and task-relevant rather than global sense
[Schoenholz et al., 2017, Pennington et al., 2017].
Motivated by this theory, we evaluate coarse-state closure empirically in trained neural networks. We
construct a bank of low-dimensional coarse variables from layerwise representations and fit reduced
models to their depth-wise evolution. Across synthetic tasks and architectures, we find that the best
coarse state is not universal. Instead, closure quality depends strongly on both task structure and
architectural bias. This variability is a central finding: it suggests that coarse-grained dynamics
provides a principled lens for identifying which aspects of neural representations are dynamically
relevant, and why different architectures favor different effective descriptions.
Our contributions are as follows.
• We introduce a Mori–Zwanzig perspective for analyzing layerwise neural representation
dynamics through coarse-grained reduced states with explicit memory structure.
• We derive conditions under which short-memory closure is justified, and connect this regime
to task-relevant geometry preservation and structured regularization implications.
• We empirically show that the best coarse state is strongly task- and architecture-dependent,
demonstrating that coarse-state selection should be understood as a reduced-dynamics
problem rather than as a purely heuristic choice of representation summary.
2 Related Work: Representation Analysis and Reduced Dynamics
2.1 Geometric Structure of Learned Representations
A large body of work analyzes neural representations through geometric or statistical summaries.
Representation similarity methods such as SVCCA and CKA compare hidden representations across
layers, models, or training runs, and have become standard tools for studying how internal repre-
sentations evolve during training [Raghu et al., 2017, Kornblith et al., 2019]. Other lines of work
characterize the geometry of learned features, including class separation, margin structure, and neural
collapse in the terminal phase of training [Papyan et al., 2020].
These approaches provide powerful descriptions of what representations encode or how similar two
representations are. However, they usually treat each layer or representation snapshot as an object to
be summarized, rather than asking whether a chosen set of variables forms a closed reduced state
for the depth-wise dynamics. Our work is complementary: instead of evaluating a representation
summary only as a diagnostic statistic, we ask whether it supports a predictive reduced dynamics
across layers.
2.2 Neural Networks as Dynamical Systems
Another line of work views neural networks as dynamical systems. Residual networks have been
interpreted as discretizations of differential equations, motivating analyses of stability, reversibility,
and continuous-depth models [Haber and Ruthotto, 2017, Chang et al., 2018, Chen et al., 2018].
Related theoretical work studies signal propagation through depth, including edge-of-chaos conditions
and dynamical isometry, which characterize when signals and gradients can propagate without rapid
explosion or decay [Schoenholz et al., 2017, Pennington et al., 2017].
These perspectives emphasize the dynamical nature of deep networks, but they typically focus on
the full hidden state, global Jacobian spectra, or stability of the network map. In contrast, our
focus is on the reduced dynamics of selected coarse variables. Rather than asking only whether the
full representation map is stable or geometry-preserving, we ask whether a low-dimensional state
extracted from representations can close the observed layerwise dynamics, and how this closure
depends on task structure and architectural bias.

2.3 Coarse-Graining, Memory, and Mori–Zwanzig Formalism
Coarse-graining provides a classical framework for reducing high-dimensional dynamical systems to
a smaller set of resolved variables. The Mori–Zwanzig formalism shows that eliminating unresolved
degrees of freedom generally produces effective dynamics with memory and fluctuation terms [Mori,
1965, Zwanzig, 1961, 2001]. This idea has been widely used in nonequilibrium statistical physics
and reduced modeling to understand how unresolved variables influence the evolution of observed
macroscopic quantities.
Recent work has also developed data-driven reduced models with memory, using observed trajectories
to learn effective dynamics that account for unresolved degrees of freedom [Lin et al., 2021, Ma
et al., 2019]. Closest to our theoretical motivation, Venturi and Li [2023] formulated deep learning
through the Mori–Zwanzig formalism. Our focus is different: we do not aim to provide a full
operator-theoretic formulation of learning, but instead adopt a resolved–unresolved view, motivated
by the Mori–Zwanzig perspective, as an operational criterion for evaluating coarse states in trained
neural representation dynamics.
In this sense, we use the Mori–Zwanzig formalism not as a detailed physical model of neural networks,
but as a reduced-dynamics principle: unresolved representation degrees of freedom should manifest
as memory effects in the evolution of selected coarse variables. This viewpoint motivates our criterion
for neural coarse states: a useful coarse state should admit an accurate short-memory closure of its
depth-wise dynamics.
2.4 Our Position
Existing representation analyses provide powerful geometric and statistical summaries of neural
representations, but they typically do not evaluate whether a chosen set of variables forms a closed
reduced state for the layerwise dynamics. Conversely, Mori–Zwanzig theory and data-driven reduced
modeling provide a principled language for memory and closure, but have not generally been used as
a practical criterion for selecting neural coarse states across tasks and architectures.
Our work connects these directions by treating coarse-state selection as a reduced-modeling problem.
A good coarse state is not merely one that summarizes a representation at a single layer, but one
whose evolution supports an accurate short-memory closure across depth. This allows us to compare
coarse states across tasks and architectures through the quality of their induced reduced dynamics.
3 A Mori–Zwanzig View of Layerwise Neural Representation Dynamics
Our goal is not to identify a unique set of coarse variables for neural representations. Instead, we
ask when a low-dimensional coarse state can support an effective reduced dynamics across depth.
We view layerwise representation evolution through a resolved–unresolved decomposition. This
decomposition naturally induces memory terms after unresolved degrees of freedom are eliminated,
leading to a criterion for coarse-state quality: a useful coarse state should support an accurate
short-memory closure while preserving task-relevant geometry on resolved directions.
Although this viewpoint is motivated by the Mori–Zwanzig formalism, our derivation below is
carried out for a local layerwise surrogate of neural representation dynamics. We introduce a
resolved–unresolved linearization, eliminate the unresolved variables, and obtain an explicit discrete
memory kernel. This derivation identifies the structural quantities that control short-memory closure:
contraction of unresolved dynamics, weak resolved–unresolved coupling, and stable transport on
task-relevant resolved directions.

4 Numerical Evaluation
4.1 Experimental Questions
The theoretical framework in Section 3 suggests that a useful coarse state should support a short-
memory closure of the layerwise representation dynamics. Our numerical evaluation asks whether
this principle holds in trained neural networks, and whether the resulting closure behavior depends
on the interaction between task structure and architectural bias.
We focus on three experimental questions.
Q1: Do low-dimensional coarse states support short-memory closure?We first test whether
the evolution of selected coarse variables across depth can be approximated by a reduced model
with a small number of lag terms. This evaluates whether the memory structure motivated by the
Mori–Zwanzig perspective and derived in Section 3 is empirically useful for neural representations.

PAGE 7
Q2: Is the best coarse state task- and architecture-dependent?We next ask whether the coarse
variables that yield strong closure are universal, or whether they depend on the task and architecture.
This directly tests our central claim that coarse-state selection should be treated as a reduced-dynamics
problem rather than as a fixed choice of representation summary.
Q3: Is closure quality distinct from predictive accuracy?Finally, we examine the relation
between task accuracy and reduced-model closure quality. If closure were merely a proxy for task
performance, then high-accuracy models would always exhibit high closure scores. Instead, we ask
whether models with comparable accuracy can exhibit different closure behavior, indicating that
closure captures dynamical regularity of the coarse state rather than predictive performance alone.

4.3 Short-Memory Closure Across Tasks and Architectures
We first examine whether short-memory closure is governed by a single universal reduced description
across tasks and architectures. Table 1 summarizes test accuracy and closure diagnostics for the main
1D tasks at sequence lengthsT∈{64,144,256}.
The results show that closure quality varies substantially across both task families and model classes.
The same architecture can exhibit different closure behavior depending on the task, and different
architectures can yield distinct closure quality on the same task. For example, CNN achieves strong
closure on the Presence and Weighted Spectrum tasks across sequence lengths, whereas GRU and
MLP show more task-dependent behavior. Motif Parity also exposes an important caveat: some
high-R2 closures are trivial and should not be interpreted as meaningful reduced-state descriptions.
These results support our central claim that coarse-state selection should be treated as a reduced-
dynamics problem. A useful coarse variable is not merely a descriptive statistic, but one whose
evolution closes well under the induced depth-wise dynamics. The table also shows that predictive
accuracy and closure quality are related but distinct: high accuracy does not necessarily imply strong
nontrivial closure, and closure failures can occur even in high-accuracy regimes.

5 Conclusion
We introduced a coarse-grained Mori–Zwanzig perspective on layerwise neural representation dy-
namics. Rather than treating representation summaries as standalone diagnostics, we proposed to
view coarse variables as candidate reduced states whose quality is determined by how well they close
the depth-wise dynamics. Through a resolved–unresolved decomposition, this perspective naturally
yields effective memory terms and motivates short-memory closure as a criterion for evaluating
coarse states.
Our analysis shows that short-memory closure is favored when unresolved directions contract,
resolved–unresolved couplings are weak, and task-relevant resolved directions are transported with
limited distortion. This provides a reduced-dynamics interpretation of geometry-preserving condi-
tions: the relevant requirement is not global dynamical isometry of the full representation map, but
approximate preservation of task-relevant resolved directions together with contraction of unresolved
degrees of freedom. Empirically, we found that the best coarse state is not universal. Across synthetic
tasks and architectures, closure quality depends on both task structure and architectural bias, and
cannot be reduced to predictive accuracy alone.
Together, these results suggest that coarse-grained reduced dynamics offers a principled lens for
comparing neural representations and diagnosing architecture–task matching. Several directions
remain open. A more rigorous mathematical foundation is needed for nonlinear, layer-dependent
neural networks beyond the local surrogate considered here. It is also important to test whether the
observed task–architecture dependence extends to larger datasets and modern architectures, and to
directly evaluate the proposed links to geometry-preserving conditions and structured regularization.
Finally, future work should move beyond hand-designed candidate banks toward automatic discovery
of coarse variables that optimize closure.

B.2 Dense Post-Activation Stage Construction
The stage sequence used for reduced-model fitting is not identical to the list of architectural layers.
Instead, for each eligible post-activation tensor, we apply deterministic probe maps that produce
ordered stage points for the coarse-grained dynamics. These probes include pooled summaries and
architecture-specific summaries such as temporal positions or hidden-state aggregates. As a result,
the number of stage points can differ across architectures even when the model depth is matched.
The model depth is matched at the architectural level, whereas the number of analysis stage points is
induced by the post-activation probe construction. Therefore, our comparisons match architectures
by model design rather than by forcing the same number of stage points.

B.5 Software Dependencies and Compute Resources
All reported experiments were designed to be workstation-scale. No distributed training, cloud cluster,
or specialized large-scale infrastructure was used. The reported 1D experiments consist of 3 tasks×
3 architectures×3 sequence lengths×5 random seeds, giving 135 benchmark training runs in total.
The subsequent closure analysis was run once for each trained model.
We estimated wall-clock runtime from the timestamps of generated output files. The interval
between successive benchmark summary.json files was approximately 1–1.5 minutes, and the
closure-analysis outputs were generated at a rate of approximately 10–13 files per minute. These
estimates correspond to roughly 2.3–3.4 workstation-hours for benchmark training and 0.17–0.23
workstation-hours for closure analysis, excluding small overheads such as job startup, file I/O, and
plotting.
