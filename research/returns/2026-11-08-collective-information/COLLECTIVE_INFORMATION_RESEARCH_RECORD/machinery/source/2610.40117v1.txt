Abstract
Recent advances in microscopic many-body methods have enabled increasingly accurate descriptions of equilibrium quantum states, yet large-scale simulations of emergent-order dynamics typically rely on phenomenological equations with prescribed free-energy functionals. We introduce the Adiabatic Variational Principle (AVP), a framework for deriving order-parameter dynamics directly from an interacting many-body Hamiltonian. When microscopic relaxation is fast compared with order-parameter evolution, the many-body state follows the collective configuration adiabatically, in analogy with the Born–Oppenheimer separation of electronic and nuclear motion. This assumption makes explicit the adiabatic closure implicit in conventional phase-field descriptions. Each instantaneous order-parameter configuration then defines a constrained quasi-stationary many-body state, and the Lagrange multipliers enforcing these constraints directly provide the thermodynamic forces driving the collective evolution. Nonlinear Mori–Zwanzig projection theory further connects the reversible dynamics to the microscopic algebra of the collective variables and relates dissipation and fluctuations to the eliminated degrees of freedom. Applications to charge- and spin-density-wave dynamics recover Allen–Cahn domain growth, resolve evolving domain networks together with their microscopic electronic structure, and yield generalized Landau–Lifshitz dynamics for itinerant magnetism. The AVP thus provides a solver-independent microscopic foundation for phase-field-like simulations of the nonequilibrium dynamics of emergent electronic order.
I Introduction
Collective electronic order driven by interactions among itinerant electrons is a central organizing principle of correlated quantum matter. Prominent examples include charge-density waves, magnetism, and superconductivity, which arise across a wide range of quantum materials, from transition-metal oxides and heavy-fermion compounds to moiré heterostructures [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Over the past several decades, substantial progress has been made in understanding the equilibrium properties of such phases, including how competing interactions, dimensionality, and electronic structure stabilize distinct ordered states and phase transitions [8, 9]. A broad array of many-body techniques—including quantum Monte Carlo, dynamical mean-field and quantum-embedding methods, and tensor-network approaches—has provided increasingly powerful microscopic descriptions of ground-state and finite-temperature properties of interacting electron systems [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17].
Extending microscopic many-body methods to excitation and nonequilibrium dynamics is considerably more demanding, despite substantial progress with a variety of time-dependent approaches [18, 19, 20, 21, 22]. An additional difficulty arises for symmetry-breaking phases, where the relevant order parameters are not independent classical fields but emergent collective coordinates of the underlying many-body state. Their evolution is therefore intrinsically coupled to the microscopic electronic structure that stabilizes the ordered phase and is itself modified as the order develops. Capturing this mutual dependence between collective order and microscopic electronic degrees of freedom is a central problem in the theory of nonequilibrium correlated systems.
Figure 1: 
Schematic of the Adiabatic Variational Principle (AVP). In analogy with the Born–Oppenheimer separation of slow and fast degrees of freedom, a slowly evolving order-parameter field ϕ⁡(𝐫,t)\phi(\mathbf{r},t) defines, at each instant, a constrained microscopic many-body state ρ^​[ϕ]\hat{\rho}[\phi]. Here, however, the slow variable is an emergent collective observable rather than an independent microscopic coordinate. The associated Lagrange multipliers provide the thermodynamic forces that drive the collective evolution, while the microscopic state follows the changing order-parameter configuration adiabatically.
This difficulty is amplified in spatially inhomogeneous electronic states, where collective evolution unfolds over extended length and time scales and gives rise to complex real-space patterns and textures [23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. Spatially resolved probes such as scanning tunneling microscopy, spectroscopic imaging, and near-field optical spectroscopy have revealed such structures on nanometer scales, including ordered domains separated by domain walls and other defects [31, 32]. Their formation and relaxation naturally connect the dynamics of electronic order to the broader problem of phase-ordering kinetics, encompassing domain growth, the development of spatial correlations, and the relaxation of order-parameter textures following a nonequilibrium perturbation [33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38]. For electronic phases, however, these evolving patterns remain embedded in a microscopic many-body state, so that domain growth, defect motion, and changes in the local amplitude or orientation of the order parameter can be accompanied by substantial reconstruction of the underlying electronic structure.
At a coarse-grained level, the dynamics of textured electronic states is often described using continuum theories such as time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau and related phase-field approaches [39, 40, 41, 42]. These frameworks treat the order parameter as an effective classical field evolving under a prescribed free-energy functional and kinetic coefficients, and provide a powerful description of universal aspects of domain growth and defect motion. Although microscopic calculations can determine these parameters in simplified settings or controlled expansion regimes, such descriptions generally do not determine the underlying many-body state for each instantaneous order-parameter texture. Establishing such a connection would enable phase-field-like simulations in which the electronic structure and thermodynamic driving forces are determined directly from the microscopic interacting model.
In this work, we address this challenge by introducing the Adiabatic Variational Principle (AVP), a microscopic framework for the dynamics of emergent electronic order. The construction is motivated by an adiabatic separation of time scales analogous to the Born–Oppenheimer picture [43, 44, 45], but with a crucial distinction: the slow variables are emergent collective observables rather than independent microscopic coordinates. The central physical picture is illustrated schematically in Fig. 1. For each instantaneous order-parameter configuration, the AVP associates a constrained many-body state and its conjugate thermodynamic forces, thereby extending equilibrium many-body methods to spatially inhomogeneous collective dynamics. Nonlinear projection theory provides the corresponding dynamical foundation [46, 47, 48], relating reversible evolution to the microscopic commutator structure of the collective observables and dissipation to the relaxation of the remaining microscopic degrees of freedom. The AVP thus establishes a microscopic bridge between interacting many-body theory and phase-field-like simulations, without requiring an empirical free-energy landscape or a phenomenological prescription for the reversible dynamics.
Variational methods are central to modern many-body physics, providing tractable representations of interacting quantum states within manifolds tailored to the problem at hand [49, 50, 51, 13, 52]. Beyond their widespread use in equilibrium and ground-state calculations, this strategy also extends naturally to quantum dynamics. A prominent example for isolated systems is the time-dependent variational principle (TDVP) [53, 54, 20, 55, 21], in which the many-body wavefunction is restricted to a chosen manifold—such as Slater determinants, tensor-network states, or neural-network quantum states—and the Schrödinger equation is projected onto its tangent space. When the ansatz permits broken symmetry, electronic order parameters can naturally emerge as collective coordinates characterizing the evolving state. The resulting projected equations then provide a microscopic description of their dynamics within the chosen manifold.
Similar ideas extend to open quantum systems by combining Lindblad master equations [56, 57] with TDVP or related variational methods. The density matrix is represented within a restricted manifold—for example, using matrix-product operators or neural-network density operators—and evolved by projecting the Lindblad equation onto its tangent space, while steady states can be obtained through variational optimization [58, 59, 60, 61]. These approaches have enabled studies of dissipative many-body dynamics, nonequilibrium steady states, and dissipation-driven phase transitions. Accurately representing the evolving density matrix nevertheless remains computationally demanding for large, spatially inhomogeneous systems, with the growth of operator-space entanglement posing a particular challenge for tensor-network methods.
The AVP exploits an emergent separation of time scales between collective order and the remaining microscopic degrees of freedom. Such a hierarchy may arise from relaxation induced by a thermal environment, intrinsic scattering and dephasing of microscopic excitations, or the slow evolution of collective modes. When the microscopic sector relaxes sufficiently rapidly toward a quasi-stationary state compatible with the instantaneous order-parameter configuration, this state can be determined variationally subject to that configuration as a constraint. The resulting constrained states define a manifold on which the slow collective variables evolve.
This construction differs fundamentally from TDVP–Lindblad approaches, which seek to approximate the real-time evolution of the many-body density matrix itself. The computational cost of following that evolution limits the spatial and temporal scales accessible for collective pattern formation and defect dynamics. By determining the electronic state conditionally on the evolving collective configuration, the AVP provides a route for adapting equilibrium many-body methods to these processes. This requires extending the underlying solvers to impose collective constraints and extract their conjugate forces, with an implementation that depends on the chosen many-body representation. Such constrained calculations retain the microscopic electronic response without explicitly resolving the fast electronic evolution.
The adiabatic premise of this reduction is not unique to the AVP. Widely used phenomenological theories of order-parameter dynamics, including time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau, Cahn–Hilliard, Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert, and phase-field models [39, 40, 41, 42], likewise close the evolution on a restricted set of slow variables without retaining independent dynamics for the microscopic degrees of freedom that have been eliminated. In their Markovian forms, these theories implicitly rely on a Born–Oppenheimer-like separation of time scales, in which the microscopic sector adjusts sufficiently rapidly to the instantaneous configuration of the slow variables. The AVP adopts the same physical premise, but replaces the phenomenological closure by an explicit microscopic construction of the constrained many-body state and its thermodynamic forces. In this sense, it provides a Born–Oppenheimer theory of emergent electronic order.
An important feature of the AVP is that its construction is not tied to a particular approximation for the underlying many-body problem. The constrained microscopic state and its conjugate thermodynamic forces may be obtained using a broad range of many-body solvers, from mean-field and quasiparticle approaches to tensor-network, Monte Carlo, and neural-network methods. This solver-independent structure allows the microscopic description to be systematically improved without altering the form of the reduced collective dynamics. The computational cost nevertheless depends on the chosen solver and can remain substantial.
As a proof of principle, we formulate the constrained many-body problem within a mean-field approximation and study the nonequilibrium dynamics of charge- and spin-density-wave order in interacting lattice models. Even at this level, the calculation is computationally demanding: the constrained mean-field state must be determined iteratively at every dynamical time step, requiring repeated solution of a large quadratic single-particle problem, for example through explicit diagonalization. Nevertheless, GPU acceleration enables us to follow charge-ordering dynamics on lattices containing roughly 4,000 sites, resolving extended domain patterns together with their thermodynamic forces and microscopic electronic structure. The applications illustrate domain growth and defect dynamics in charge ordering, as well as the rapid formation of local magnetic moments followed by slower development of antiferromagnetic orientational correlations.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Sec. II, we introduce the general AVP construction and formulate the constrained many-body state and its conjugate thermodynamic forces. In Sec. III, we derive the dynamical structure of the AVP using nonlinear projection theory, including its reversible and dissipative components. Section IV develops the mean-field AVP (MF-AVP). In Sec. V, we apply the resulting MF-AVP to charge-density-wave (CDW) ordering in the spinless tt–VV model. Section VI considers spin-density-wave (SDW) dynamics in the Hubbard model. We conclude in Sec. VII with a discussion of the broader implications, limitations, and future directions. Technical details of the numerical implementations and classical reference models are provided in the Appendixes.
II Adiabatic Variational Principle for Emergent Order Dynamics
The central assumption underlying the AVP is a hierarchy of time scales between slow collective variables and rapidly relaxing microscopic degrees of freedom. At each instant, the microscopic electronic state is regarded as quasi-stationary and parametrized by the instantaneous configuration of the retained slow variables. For a prescribed configuration, the remaining microscopic degrees of freedom relax before the collective configuration changes appreciably. Denoting the microscopic relaxation and collective evolution time scales by τqp\tau_{\rm qp} and τϕ\tau_{\phi}, respectively, the adiabatic regime requires τqp≪τϕ\tau_{\rm qp}\ll\tau_{\phi}.
The choice of slow variables follows the general principles established by Hohenberg and Halperin [39]. In addition to the order-parameter fields ϕ⁡(𝐫,t)\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{r},t), conserved densities and other long-lived modes must be retained whenever their characteristic time scales are comparable to or longer than that of the order-parameter dynamics. Such modes cannot be assumed to relax instantaneously to values determined by the order parameters alone. A consistent reduced description therefore requires that the retained variables exhaust the relevant slow sector.
For the open electronic systems considered here, coupling to a thermal environment, such as phonons or other dissipative baths, provides a natural mechanism for microscopic relaxation; intrinsic scattering and dephasing may also contribute. The microscopic state thus adjusts to the slowly evolving collective configuration, following the Born–Oppenheimer-like picture introduced above. This conditional relaxation does not require the collective configuration itself to be in equilibrium: domains and textures can continue to evolve while the microscopic degrees of freedom remain approximately relaxed subject to the instantaneous constraints.
We formulate this construction as the Adiabatic Variational Principle (AVP). Consider a general interacting lattice Hamiltonian ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} coupled to a thermal bath. We introduce a set of local order-parameter fields ϕ⁡(𝐫,t)=(ϕ1,ϕ2,…,ϕM)\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{r},t)=(\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\ldots,\phi_{M}) through the expectation values of local operators {A^iα}\{\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\},
ϕiα=⟨A^iα⟩.\phi_{i}^{\alpha}=\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle.
(1)
The AVP itself does not require these operators to form a closed algebra. For the class of order parameters considered below, however, it is convenient to assume that
[A^iα,A^jβ]=i​δi​j​∑γCα​β​γ​A^iγ,[\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha},\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta}]=i\,\delta_{ij}\sum_{\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\hat{A}_{i}^{\gamma},
(2)
where Cα​β​γC_{\alpha\beta\gamma} are the corresponding structure constants. Closure of the operator algebra allows the reversible dynamics to be expressed directly in terms of the commutation relations of the retained variables.
The description of slow collective dynamics has a long history in nonequilibrium thermodynamics. Relaxational dynamics goes back to Landau and Khalatnikov [62], while Onsager’s theory established the linear-response relations governing dissipative couplings [63, 64]. Subsequent developments, including the classification of dynamical universality classes [39] and unified thermodynamic formulations such as the general equation for the nonequilibrium reversible–irreversible coupling (GENERIC) [65, 66], organize the dynamics into reversible and dissipative sectors.
Eliminating microscopic degrees of freedom generally produces memory effects in both sectors. For the formulation and applications in this section, we adopt the Markovian limit, in which the evolution is local in time. To leading order in the thermodynamic forces, the dynamics then takes the familiar reversible–dissipative form [39, 65, 66],
d​ϕiαd​t=∑j,βℛi​jα​β​∂F∂ϕjβ−∑j,βΓi​jα​β​∂F∂ϕjβ+ηiα​(t).\displaystyle\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}{dt}=\sum_{j,\beta}\mathcal{R}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{j}^{\beta}}-\sum_{j,\beta}\Gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{j}^{\beta}}+\eta_{i}^{\alpha}(t).
(3)
Here F⁡[ϕ]F[\bm{\phi}] is the effective free-energy functional of the collective variables. The reversible tensor is antisymmetric, ℛi​jα​β=−ℛj​iβ​α\mathcal{R}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=-\mathcal{R}_{ji}^{\beta\alpha}, whereas the dissipative tensor is symmetric and positive semidefinite, Γi​jα​β=Γj​iβ​α\Gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=\Gamma_{ji}^{\beta\alpha}. Consequently, the reversible dynamics leaves FF unchanged, while the deterministic dissipative contribution drives relaxation toward lower free energy, with
d​Fd​t=−∑i,α,j,β∂F∂ϕiαΓi​jα​β∂F∂ϕjβ≤0\frac{dF}{dt}=-\sum_{i,\alpha,j,\beta}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}\Gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{j}^{\beta}}\leq 0
in the absence of fluctuations. The fluctuating force ηiα​(t)\eta_{i}^{\alpha}(t) represents the stochastic influence of degrees of freedom outside the retained slow sector. In thermal equilibrium, its statistics and the dissipative tensor Γ\Gamma are related by the fluctuation–dissipation relation.
Within the AVP, the reversible and dissipative tensors have distinct microscopic origins. For operators satisfying Eq. (2), ℛ\mathcal{R} follows from their commutation relations and acquires a Poisson-bracket-like structure. The tensor Γ\Gamma describes relaxation mediated by the eliminated degrees of freedom, including microscopic electronic excitations and the external environment. In the Markovian limit, their influence is represented by kinetic coefficients whose microscopic form depends on the dynamics and couplings of this complementary sector.
Equations of this form also underlie time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau descriptions of phase-ordering dynamics and, more broadly, phase-field approaches to mesoscale ordering and microstructure evolution [35, 41, 42]. In these theories, the thermodynamics is encoded in a coarse-grained continuum free-energy functional of the order-parameter fields and their spatial variations. Schematically, one writes
FGL​[ϕ]=∫d​𝐫​[V⁡(ϕ)+12​∑α​β,μ​νKα​βμ​ν​(ϕ)​∂μϕα​∂νϕβ+⋯],F_{\rm GL}[\bm{\phi}]=\int d\mathbf{r}\left[V(\bm{\phi})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\alpha\beta,\mu\nu}K_{\alpha\beta}^{\mu\nu}(\bm{\phi})\,\partial_{\mu}\phi^{\alpha}\,\partial_{\nu}\phi^{\beta}+\cdots\right],
(4)
where the local potential V⁡(ϕ)V(\bm{\phi}) and gradient terms are constructed from symmetry-allowed invariants. The forces −δFGL/δϕα-\delta F_{\rm GL}/\delta\phi^{\alpha}, together with appropriate kinetic coefficients, generate the collective dynamics. This framework has been highly successful in describing domains, interfaces, competing phases, and microstructure on mesoscopic scales.
The coefficients of FGLF_{\rm GL} may be specified phenomenologically, fitted to experiment, or derived from microscopic models. Microscopic derivations provide a valuable connection to the underlying Hamiltonian, but generally rely on approximations valid in particular regimes. In particular, low-order amplitude and gradient expansions may become inadequate for sharp interfaces, defect cores, or textures that substantially reconstruct the electronic state. Extending the description to such configurations therefore calls for a way to retain the microscopic electronic response without relying on a prescribed low-order continuum functional.
The AVP provides such a construction within the adiabatic regime. Starting from a specified interacting lattice Hamiltonian, it associates each instantaneous configuration ϕ\bm{\phi} with a constrained many-body state and thereby defines a microscopic free-energy functional FAVP​[ϕ]F_{\rm AVP}[\bm{\phi}]. The functional and its conjugate forces are obtained from this constrained problem without requiring an amplitude or gradient expansion. This offers a route to phase-field-like simulations that retain the electronic response to evolving textures, with an accuracy that can in principle be systematically improved through the underlying many-body solver.
The connection to microscopic dynamics extends beyond the free-energy functional. In Sec. III, we embed the AVP within nonlinear projection-operator theory, which yields the organized reversible motion, memory-dependent dissipation, and fluctuations of the retained variables. This provides a microscopic statistical-mechanical foundation for Eq. (3) and clarifies the conditions under which its Markovian form emerges.
II.1 Variational Formulation in Terms of Many-Body Wave Functions
Constrained variational constructions are well established in many-body physics, including constrained Hartree–Fock and density-functional approaches. Here we use this established construction as the microscopic foundation for a description of spatially inhomogeneous, nonequilibrium order-parameter dynamics. Although the general AVP is formulated in terms of density matrices, we begin with pure many-body states |Ψ⟩|\Psi\rangle, which provide a familiar setting for introducing its variational foundation. For a prescribed order-parameter configuration, constrained energy minimization defines the instantaneous adiabatic state and makes the relation between the constraint fields and thermodynamic forces explicit, without specifying a particular variational ansatz. This wave-function formulation serves as a pedagogical and formal starting point; it does not assume that the electronic subsystem remains pure during its actual evolution. Coupling to an environment generally produces a mixed reduced state, even for a zero-temperature bath, motivating the density-matrix formulation developed in the following subsection.
Within this simpler wave-function setting, the central construction is to regard the order-parameter configuration ϕ\bm{\phi} as specifying a set of macroscopic constraints on the underlying many-body state. For each ϕ\bm{\phi}, we seek a normalized state |Ψ⟩|\Psi\rangle consistent with Eq. (1), while all remaining microscopic degrees of freedom are variationally optimized. Within this zero-temperature description, the adiabatic approximation identifies the corresponding instantaneous state with the minimum-energy state compatible with these constraints:

(5)

Thus, the constrained adiabatic state satisfies a stationary eigenvalue equation for a Hamiltonian deformed by the conjugate fields fiαf_{i}^{\alpha}, with the relevant solution selected by the original constrained minimization. Without the order-parameter constraints, these fields vanish and the construction reduces to the ordinary ground-state problem for ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}}, with Λ\Lambda equal to the ground-state energy. For prescribed ϕ\bm{\phi}, the fields are determined self-consistently to enforce the corresponding expectation values, rather than imposed externally. As shown below, they also have a direct physical interpretation as the generalized thermodynamic forces conjugate to the order parameters.
In practice, solving the stationarity conditions depends on the chosen variational representation of |Ψ⟩|\Psi\rangle. Within a restricted variational class, stationarity is imposed with respect to the ansatz parameters, while the multipliers are adjusted until the constraints ⟨A^iα⟩=ϕiα\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle=\phi_{i}^{\alpha} are satisfied. The constrained state and its conjugate fields are therefore determined together in the same optimization problem. Although the numerical procedure depends on the underlying many-body solver, the variational construction itself is broadly applicable across different many-body representations.
The microscopic free-energy functional introduced above as FAVP​[ϕ]F_{\rm AVP}[\bm{\phi}] is defined, within the present wave-function formulation, by the energy of the constrained adiabatic state,
FAVP​[ϕ]=⟨Ψ⁡[ϕ]|ℋ^|Ψ⁡[ϕ]⟩.F_{\rm AVP}[\bm{\phi}]=\langle\Psi[\bm{\phi}]|\hat{\mathcal{H}}|\Psi[\bm{\phi}]\rangle.
(9)
For notational simplicity, we henceforth denote this functional simply by F⁡[ϕ]F[\bm{\phi}]. Thus, rather than postulating an effective functional of the order parameter, as in phenomenological Ginzburg–Landau theories, the AVP determines F⁡[ϕ]F[\bm{\phi}] directly from the underlying microscopic Hamiltonian through the constrained many-body problem. The resulting energy landscape reflects the interactions and correlations captured by the chosen variational state, and its accuracy can in principle be systematically improved by refining the ansatz.
We next establish the physical meaning of the Lagrange multipliers fiαf_{i}^{\alpha}. Consider neighboring optimized states along a smooth branch of the constrained manifold, |Ψ⁡[ϕ]⟩→|Ψ⁡[ϕ+d​ϕ]⟩=|Ψ⁡[ϕ]⟩+|δ​Ψ⟩|\Psi[\bm{\phi}]\rangle\rightarrow|\Psi[\bm{\phi}+d\bm{\phi}]\rangle=|\Psi[\bm{\phi}]\rangle+|\delta\Psi\rangle. (10)
Thus, the Lagrange multipliers fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} are precisely the generalized thermodynamic forces conjugate to the order parameters ϕiα\phi_{i}^{\alpha}, quantifying the energetic cost of maintaining a prescribed order-parameter configuration. The AVP uses this variational relation to obtain the forces entering Eq. (3) directly from the constrained many-body problem.
This identification also has an important practical consequence. The derivatives ∂F/∂ϕiα\partial F/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha} need not be evaluated by numerical differentiation of the effective free-energy landscape, which would require repeated constrained calculations and can be susceptible to numerical noise. Instead, the driving forces are obtained directly from the Lagrange multipliers fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} already determined in the constrained optimization.
II.2 Variational Formulation in Terms of Many-Body Density Matrix
The wave-function formulation provides a transparent introduction to the constrained variational structure of the AVP, but the density matrix is the natural object for its general formulation. The rapid microscopic relaxation required by the adiabatic approximation, τqp≪τϕ\tau_{\rm qp}\ll\tau_{\phi}, is generally facilitated by coupling to an external environment, so the reduced electronic state need not remain pure even when the environment is at zero temperature. The density-matrix formulation associates each instantaneous configuration of the slow variables with a quasi-stationary statistical state of the remaining microscopic degrees of freedom. This construction is distinct from solving for the full nonequilibrium evolution of the density matrix: the AVP determines the state conditioned on the instantaneous slow variables and, through it, the effective free energy and conjugate thermodynamic forces. We now establish the constrained statistical ensemble that implements this construction.
For a given order-parameter configuration ϕ\bm{\phi}, the adiabatic density matrix is obtained by minimizing the thermodynamic free energy over all microscopic states consistent with Eq. (1). The microscopic degrees of freedom thus equilibrate subject to the instantaneous values of the slow collective variables, defining a manifold of constrained quasi-equilibrium states ρ^​[ϕ]\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}]. At finite temperature, we therefore introduce the free-energy Lagrangian

(13)
Variation with respect to ρ^\hat{\rho} then yields the constrained Gibbs state
ρ^​[ϕ]=e−β​ℋ^∗​[𝒇]Z⁡[𝒇],Z⁡[𝒇]=Tr​e−β​ℋ^∗​[𝒇],\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}]=\frac{e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}]}}{Z[\bm{f}]},\qquad Z[\bm{f}]=\mathrm{Tr}\,e^{-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}]},
(14)
where β=1/T\beta=1/T and ℋ^∗​[𝒇]\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}] is the effective Hamiltonian defined in Eq. (8). The Lagrange multiplier Λ\Lambda is fixed by normalization, with β​Λ−1=−ln⁡Z⁡[𝒇]\beta\Lambda-1=-\ln Z[\bm{f}].
The constrained Gibbs construction gives the AVP adiabatic states a direct physical interpretation: the auxiliary Hamiltonian ℋ^∗​[𝒇]\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}] has a thermal equilibrium state that reproduces the instantaneous collective configuration ϕ\bm{\phi}. The fields fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} are determined self-consistently to enforce these values, rather than applied externally. Even a spatially inhomogeneous, globally nonequilibrium configuration can thus be represented by a constrained Gibbs ensemble, with the remaining microscopic degrees of freedom relaxed subject to the instantaneous slow variables. This conditional equilibration expresses the adiabatic separation underlying the AVP.
The effective free-energy functional F⁡[ϕ]F[\bm{\phi}] is obtained by evaluating the free energy with the variationally optimized density matrix,
F⁡[ϕ]=Tr⁡{ρ^​[ϕ]​ℋ^}+T​Tr​{ρ^​[ϕ]​ln⁡ρ^​[ϕ]}.F[\bm{\phi}]=\mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}]\,\hat{\mathcal{H}}\bigr\}+T\,\mathrm{Tr}\bigl\{\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}]\ln\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}]\bigr\}.
(15)
It includes both energetic and entropic contributions and defines the thermodynamic landscape governing the slow collective variables.
The physical meaning of the constraint fields follows from the same variational argument used in the wave-function formulation. Consider an infinitesimal change of the order-parameter configuration, accompanied by the corresponding variation of the optimized density matrix along the constrained manifold. At fixed temperature, stationarity of the free-energy Lagrangian with respect to ρ^\hat{\rho}, with its explicit arguments ϕ\bm{\phi}, 𝒇\bm{f}, and Λ\Lambda held fixed at the original solution, gives 0=δρ​ℒ=δ​F+∑i,αfiα​δ​ϕiα0=\delta_{\rho}\mathcal{L}=\delta F+\sum_{i,\alpha}f_{i}^{\alpha}\,\delta\phi_{i}^{\alpha}. Thus, also in the density-matrix formulation, the Lagrange multipliers introduced to enforce the order-parameter constraints are precisely the generalized thermodynamic forces conjugate to the slow variables, fiα=−∂F/∂ϕiαf_{i}^{\alpha}=-\partial F/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha}.
The relation between ϕ\bm{\phi} and the constraint fields 𝒇\bm{f} can equivalently be understood in terms of a thermodynamic Legendre transformation. The partition function Z⁡[𝒇]Z[\bm{f}] defines the thermodynamic potential of the deformed Hamiltonian ℋ^∗​[𝒇]\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}],
G⁡[𝒇]=−T​ln⁡Z⁡[𝒇],∂G∂fiα=⟨A^iα⟩=ϕiα,G[\bm{f}]=-T\ln Z[\bm{f}],\qquad\frac{\partial G}{\partial f_{i}^{\alpha}}=\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle=\phi_{i}^{\alpha},
(16)
with the fields fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} adjusted so that the corresponding expectation values reproduce the prescribed ϕiα\phi_{i}^{\alpha}. Transforming from 𝒇\bm{f} to ϕ\bm{\phi} gives
F⁡[ϕ]=G⁡[𝒇]−∑i,αfiα​ϕiα.F[\bm{\phi}]=G[\bm{f}]-\sum_{i,\alpha}f_{i}^{\alpha}\phi_{i}^{\alpha}.
(17)
At fixed temperature, d​G=∑i,αϕiα​d​fiαdG=\sum_{i,\alpha}\phi_{i}^{\alpha}\,df_{i}^{\alpha}, and hence
dF=−∑i,αfiαdϕiα.dF=-\sum_{i,\alpha}f_{i}^{\alpha}\,d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}.
(18)
This Legendre structure provides a complementary thermodynamic interpretation of Eq. (10) and makes clear that the constraint fields are not merely auxiliary variables, but the physical driving forces associated with variations of the order parameters. For unrestricted minimization over density matrices, the resulting functional is convex in the prescribed expectation values, since statistical mixtures remain admissible. This exact ensemble functional should be distinguished from a restricted variational landscape: the Gaussian manifold used in the MF-AVP applications below is not closed under statistical mixtures and can support nonconvex branches and barriers. The force relation remains valid along each smooth constrained variational branch.
As in the wave-function formulation, these forces are obtained directly from the Lagrange multipliers, without numerical differentiation of the free-energy landscape. Thus, for any instantaneous configuration ϕ\bm{\phi}, the AVP simultaneously provides the corresponding quasi-stationary microscopic state and the thermodynamic forces entering the collective dynamics. The constrained ensemble and its conjugate fields supply the statistical foundation for the reduced dynamical theory developed below.
Figure 2: Schematic illustration of adiabatic dynamics based on the AVP. For a given instantaneous configuration of the slow order parameters, the constrained variational problem determines the corresponding adiabatic microscopic state and the conjugate thermodynamic forces. The resulting state also provides the expectation values of microscopic observables characterizing the nonequilibrium system. The effective equation of motion, including both reversible and dissipative contributions, then advances the order parameters to the next time step. Repeating these two steps generates the coupled evolution of the slow collective variables and the microscopic many-body state.
II.3 Adiabatic Dynamics Simulation Workflow
Combining the above AVP construction with the effective equation of motion in Eq. (3) provides a practical framework for adiabatic dynamics simulations, as summarized schematically in Fig. 2. At a given time tt, the instantaneous configuration ϕ⁡(t)\bm{\phi}(t) is imposed as a set of constraints on the microscopic many-body state. The AVP then determines the corresponding quasi-stationary electronic state ρ^​[ϕ​(t)]\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}(t)] and its conjugate fields 𝒇⁡(t)\bm{f}(t). Other microscopic observables can also be evaluated in this state, allowing the electronic structure to be followed alongside the evolving order-parameter texture.
The conjugate forces are subsequently used in Eq. (3), together with the reversible and dissipative tensors and the appropriate fluctuating forces, to propagate the slow variables over one time step. The resulting configuration ϕ⁡(t+Δ​t)\bm{\phi}(t+\Delta t) defines a new constrained variational problem, and the electronic state and conjugate fields are recalculated. Repeating these steps generates the collective trajectory without explicitly propagating the fast microscopic electronic dynamics. This alternating procedure gives a practical realization of the Born–Oppenheimer-like picture: domains and textures evolve under forces determined by an electronic state that remains approximately relaxed subject to the instantaneous constraints. The microscopic dynamical foundation of this simulation scheme is developed in the next section.
III Order-parameter dynamics from nonlinear projection theory
We now turn to the microscopic foundation of the collective dynamics used in the AVP simulation scheme. Its Markovian form was introduced in Eq. (3) based on thermodynamic, symmetry, and conservation-law considerations. Here we derive this dynamics using the nonlinear projection-operator formalism of Mori–Zwanzig and Grabert [46, 47, 67, 68, 48]. Within this framework, the constrained density matrix ρ^​[ϕ]\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}] is identified with the relevant statistical ensemble associated with the chosen slow variables, placing the AVP in a broader statistical-mechanical context.
The central idea of the Mori–Zwanzig projection formalism is to separate the microscopic description into a relevant sector associated with the selected collective variables and a complementary sector containing the remaining microscopic information. Introducing projection operators 𝒫\mathcal{P} and 𝒬=1−𝒫\mathcal{Q}=1-\mathcal{P} onto these two sectors, respectively, the exact microscopic dynamics can be reorganized into a generalized Langevin equation for the retained variables. The eliminated degrees of freedom are not simply discarded: their influence reappears through memory-dependent and fluctuating contributions, while the evolution projected directly onto the relevant subspace describes the organized motion of the slow variables.
For adiabatic order-parameter dynamics, the relevant variables are the fields ϕ⁡(𝐫,t)\bm{\phi}(\mathbf{r},t) introduced in Sec. II, supplemented, when necessary, by conserved densities or other long-lived quantities required to exhaust the slow sector. Phase ordering generally explores spatially inhomogeneous and strongly nonequilibrium configurations, so the constrained ensemble associated with these variables changes along the collective trajectory. A fixed Mori projection constructed around an equilibrium reference state does not follow this evolving ensemble. This motivates a state-dependent projection 𝒫⁡(t)=𝒫⁡[ϕ⁡(t)]\mathcal{P}(t)=\mathcal{P}[\bm{\phi}(t)], which adapts to the instantaneous collective configuration and accommodates nonlinear order-parameter dynamics.
To formulate this construction, let {𝒜^μ}\{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\} denote a set of relevant observables, or macrovariables, whose expectation values aμ​(t)a_{\mu}(t) specify the instantaneous macroscopic state. The corresponding relevant ensemble is obtained by maximizing the entropy subject to these expectation values [48, 69, 70],
ρ^rel[a(t)]=1Z⁡(t)exp[−∑μλμ(t)𝒜^μ],\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[a(t)]=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\exp\!\left[-\sum_{\mu}\lambda_{\mu}(t)\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\right],
(19)
where the conjugate fields λμ​(t)\lambda_{\mu}(t) are determined by the constraints aμ​(t)=Tr⁡{ρ^rel​[a⁡(t)]​𝒜^μ}a_{\mu}(t)={\rm Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[a(t)]\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\}. At this stage, the density matrix and trace refer to the full closed microscopic system, including any environment incorporated into its Hamiltonian. The relevant ensemble reproduces the chosen macroscopic expectation values but need not coincide with the actual microscopic density matrix: it retains only the information specified by the macrovariables. The same maximum-entropy relevant ensemble underlies Zubarev’s nonequilibrium statistical-operator approach, which constructs the nonequilibrium state from a weighted history of microscopically evolved relevant ensembles [71, 72]. This provides a closely related route to nonlinear transport equations; here we use the projection-operator formulation with the initial preparation specified below.
The projection constructed from this evolving ensemble yields equations for both the macroscopic means and their fluctuations. We summarize the essential results here; further details, including the fluctuation equation and the role of the initial-state assumption, are given in Appendix A. For an initial state of constrained equilibrium, ρ^​(0)=ρ^rel​[a⁡(0)]\hat{\rho}(0)=\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[a(0)], the ensemble-averaged macrovariables obey an exact transport equation of the form [48]
d​aμ​(t)d​t=vμ​[a⁡(t)]+∫0td​s​𝒦μ​[t,s;a],\frac{da_{\mu}(t)}{dt}=v_{\mu}[a(t)]+\int_{0}^{t}ds\,\mathcal{K}_{\mu}[t,s;a],
(20)
where 𝒦μ​[t,s;a]\mathcal{K}_{\mu}[t,s;a] is the memory contribution associated with dynamics in the complementary sector. It describes how the past collective evolution influences the rate of change of aμa_{\mu} at time tt; the notation [t,s;a][t,s;a] indicates dependence on the two times and, in general, on the macroscopic trajectory {aν​(u):s≤u≤t}\{a_{\nu}(u):s\leq u\leq t\}. The instantaneous organized drift is given by
vμ​[a⁡(t)]=Tr⁡{ρ^rel​[a⁡(t)]​𝒜^˙μ}.v_{\mu}[a(t)]={\rm Tr}\!\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[a(t)]\,\dot{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mu}\right\}.
(21)
Here 𝒜^˙μ\dot{\hat{\mathcal{A}}}_{\mu} denotes the Heisenberg time derivative of the corresponding microscopic observable under the full microscopic Hamiltonian. The organized drift is determined entirely by the instantaneous relevant state and describes reversible motion that preserves the entropy of the relevant ensemble.
The absence of an explicit random force in Eq. (20) does not imply that fluctuations are neglected. The initial-state assumption ensures that the fluctuating force has zero ensemble average, while its correlations remain nonzero. Fluctuations about the mean trajectory obey an accompanying generalized Langevin equation driven by this force [48]. Its correlations are related to the dissipative transport kernels through a generalized fluctuation–dissipation relation, connecting relaxation of the mean variables with fluctuations about their evolution. Together, these equations provide the microscopic starting point for a stochastic description in which both the transport kernels and the fluctuating-force statistics depend on the evolving macroscopic state.
When correlations in the complementary sector decay rapidly compared with the evolution of the macrovariables, the memory integral can be approximated by a local-in-time contribution. The resulting Markovian transport equation retains coefficients that depend on the instantaneous macroscopic state. Together with the corresponding description of fluctuations, this reduction provides the microscopic basis for the local dynamical structure introduced in Eq. (3). For the AVP, it is consistent with the assumed hierarchy τqp≪τϕ\tau_{\rm qp}\ll\tau_{\phi}, provided the retained variables exhaust the relevant slow sector.
We now specialize this general framework to the AVP. We regard the interacting electronic system together with its environment as a closed microscopic system described by the total Hamiltonian
ℋ^tot=ℋ^+ℋ^B+ℋ^SB,\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm tot}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB},
(22)
where ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} is the electronic Hamiltonian introduced in Sec. II, ℋ^B\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B} describes the bath, and ℋ^SB\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB} denotes their coupling. The combined dynamics is unitary, while dissipation emerges upon eliminating electronic and environmental degrees of freedom outside the relevant sector.
The crucial step is the choice of macrovariables: we choose {𝒜^μ}\{\hat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mu}\} to consist of the system energy ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}}, the bath energy ℋ^B\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}, and the order-parameter operators {A^iα}\{\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\}. Their expectation values are E⁡(t)=⟨ℋ^⟩E(t)=\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}\rangle, EB​(t)=⟨ℋ^B⟩E_{\rm B}(t)=\langle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}\rangle, and ϕiα​(t)=⟨A^iα⟩\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(t)=\langle\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\rangle, with conjugate fields β⁡(t)\beta(t), βB​(t)\beta_{\rm B}(t), and λiα​(t)\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}(t), respectively. Retaining the system and bath energies separately allows energy exchange with the environment to be described alongside the order-parameter dynamics. With these constraints, the full relevant ensemble factorizes as ρ^reltot​(t)=ρ^rel​(t)⊗ρ^B,rel​(t)\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}^{\rm tot}(t)=\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}(t)\otimes\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}(t), where ρ^B,rel​(t)=ZB−1​(t)​e−βB​(t)​ℋ^B\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}(t)=Z_{\rm B}^{-1}(t)e^{-\beta_{\rm B}(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}}. In the reservoir limit, βB\beta_{\rm B} is held fixed. The relevant density matrix of the electronic system is
ρ^rel​(t)=1Z⁡(t)​exp⁡[−β⁡(t)​ℋ^−∑i,αλiα​(t)​A^iα],\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}(t)=\frac{1}{Z(t)}\exp\!\left[-\beta(t)\hat{\mathcal{H}}-\sum_{i,\alpha}\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}(t)\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\right],
(23)
where Z⁡(t)Z(t) normalizes the electronic trace. Introducing fiα=λiα/βf_{i}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}/\beta, this state assumes precisely the constrained Gibbs form in Eq. (14), obtained variationally in Sec. II. The system’s β⁡(t)\beta(t) may evolve as energy is exchanged with the bath; holding it fixed recovers the fixed-temperature density-matrix AVP.
III.1 Energy Relaxation and the Thermal Adiabatic Limit
We first consider the evolution of the electronic energy. Applying Eq. (20) to ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} gives the mean transport equation d​E​(t)/𝑑t=vE​(t)+∫0td​u​𝒦E​(t,u)dE(t)/dt=v_{E}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}du\,\mathcal{K}_{E}(t,u), where vEv_{E} is the organized drift and 𝒦E\mathcal{K}_{E} is the energy memory contribution, with its trajectory dependence left implicit. Since ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} commutes with both itself and ℋ^B\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}, its microscopic time derivative arises entirely from the system–bath coupling. We assume that the system–bath coupling has zero average in the reference thermal bath state, TrB​{ρ^B,rel​ℋ^SB}=0{\rm Tr}_{\rm B}\{\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}\}=0, as occurs naturally for linear coupling to harmonic-bath displacements about equilibrium. This condition excludes an additional static bath-average field acting on the electrons, while retaining the fluctuations responsible for energy exchange and relaxation. Together with the factorized relevant ensemble, the zero bath average implies vE=0v_{E}=0, leaving the memory term to govern mean energy relaxation. Appendix A.2 derives this result and discusses its relation to the standard centering procedure.
The thermodynamic driving force follows from the entropy of the relevant ensemble. The electronic state in Eq. (23) satisfies d​S=β​d​E+∑i,αβ​fiα​d​ϕiαdS=\beta\,dE+\sum_{i,\alpha}\beta f_{i}^{\alpha}\,d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}. Neglecting changes in the system–bath interaction energy in the weak-coupling limit gives d​EB=−d​EdE_{\rm B}=-dE. A reservoir at inverse temperature βB=1/TB\beta_{\rm B}=1/T_{\rm B} therefore contributes d​SB=−βB​d​EdS_{\rm B}=-\beta_{\rm B}dE, so that
d​Stot=(β−βB)​d​E+∑i,αβ​fiα​d​ϕiα.dS_{\rm tot}=(\beta-\beta_{\rm B})\,dE+\sum_{i,\alpha}\beta f_{i}^{\alpha}\,d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}.
(24)
Here Stot=S+SBS_{\rm tot}=S+S_{\rm B} is the entropy of the full relevant ensemble. The force conjugate to energy exchange at fixed order parameter is consequently (∂Stot/∂E)ϕ=β⁡(E,ϕ)−βB(\partial S_{\rm tot}/\partial E)_{\phi}=\beta(E,\phi)-\beta_{\rm B}.
In the Markovian limit, the dissipative fluxes are linear in the entropy-conjugate forces to leading order, with kinetic coefficients evaluated at the instantaneous relevant state. Including fluctuations, and now interpreting E⁡(t)E(t) as a stochastic coarse-grained energy, we write
d​Ed​t=LE​E​[β⁡(E,ϕ)−βB]+∑i,αLE,i​α​β​fiα+ηE​(t).\frac{dE}{dt}=L_{EE}\left[\beta(E,\phi)-\beta_{\rm B}\right]+\sum_{i,\alpha}L_{E,i\alpha}\,\beta f_{i}^{\alpha}+\eta_{E}(t).
(25)
Here LE​E>0L_{EE}>0 characterizes direct thermal relaxation, while LE,i​αL_{E,i\alpha} couples energy exchange to order-parameter relaxation. The zero-mean force ηE​(t)\eta_{E}(t) describes energy fluctuations; its correlations, including cross correlations with the order-parameter noise, are constrained by the fluctuation–dissipation relation. The diagonal term transfers energy from the hotter subsystem to the colder one, whereas the cross terms allow energy exchange driven by ordering even at β=βB\beta=\beta_{\rm B}. Nonnegative dissipative entropy production requires the full symmetric transport matrix to be positive semidefinite, rather than the energy contribution to be nonnegative separately.
For each fixed ϕ\phi, the condition β⁡(Ead,ϕ)=βB\beta(E_{\rm ad},\phi)=\beta_{\rm B} defines a thermal-balance energy Ead​(ϕ,TB)E_{\rm ad}(\phi,T_{\rm B}). Writing E=Ead+δ​EE=E_{\rm ad}+\delta E gives β(E,ϕ)−βB≃−δE/(TB2Cϕ)\beta(E,\phi)-\beta_{\rm B}\simeq-\delta E/(T_{\rm B}^{2}C_{\phi}), where Cϕ=(∂E/∂T)ϕ>0C_{\phi}=(\partial E/\partial T)_{\phi}>0 is evaluated at TBT_{\rm B}. The diagonal thermal relaxation scale is therefore
τE=TB2​CϕLE​E.\tau_{E}=\frac{T_{\rm B}^{2}C_{\phi}}{L_{EE}}.
(26)
The thermal adiabatic reduction starts from the hierarchy
τE≪τϕ,\tau_{E}\ll\tau_{\phi},
(27)
where τϕ\tau_{\phi} characterizes the order-parameter evolution, while also accounting for the cross-coupled energy current.
For a prescribed slowly varying order-parameter trajectory, the deviation from the moving thermal-balance energy obeys
d​δ​Ed​t≃−δ​EτE+JE​ϕ−d​Eadd​t+ηE​(t),\frac{d\delta E}{dt}\simeq-\frac{\delta E}{\tau_{E}}+J_{E\phi}-\frac{dE_{\rm ad}}{dt}+\eta_{E}(t),
(28)
where JE​ϕ=∑i,αLE,i​α​β​fiαJ_{E\phi}=\sum_{i,\alpha}L_{E,i\alpha}\beta f_{i}^{\alpha}. After the initial thermal transient, averaging over fast fluctuations gives the leading systematic lag
δ​E¯​(t)≃τE​[JE​ϕ​(t)−d​Eadd​t],\overline{\delta E}(t)\simeq\tau_{E}\left[J_{E\phi}(t)-\frac{dE_{\rm ad}}{dt}\right],
(29)
provided the remaining terms vary slowly on the scale τE\tau_{E}; the right-hand side is evaluated on the thermal-balance manifold. The mismatch between the two currents thus produces a lag proportional to τE\tau_{E}, without requiring their cancellation. When both currents remain on the collective scale, the associated temperature offset δ​T¯≃δ​E¯/Cϕ\overline{\delta T}\simeq\overline{\delta E}/C_{\phi} is small.
To leading adiabatic order, the energy averaged over fast thermal fluctuations follows E⁡(t)≃Ead​[ϕ⁡(t),TB]E(t)\simeq E_{\rm ad}[\phi(t),T_{\rm B}] and no longer constitutes an independent slow variable. The constrained electronic state can therefore be evaluated at β=βB\beta=\beta_{\rm B} for each instantaneous configuration. This fixes the temperature rather than the energy: the electronic energy changes as ordering proceeds, with a small systematic lag and thermal fluctuations about the evolving thermal-balance state.
III.2 Effective Order-Parameter Dynamics
We now turn to the order parameters. Applying Eq. (20) to the observables A^iα\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha} gives the mean transport equation
d​ϕiα​(t)d​t=viα​(t)+∫0td​u​𝒦iα​(t,u),\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(t)}{dt}=v_{i}^{\alpha}(t)+\int_{0}^{t}du\,\mathcal{K}_{i}^{\alpha}(t,u),
(30)
where viαv_{i}^{\alpha} is the organized drift and 𝒦iα\mathcal{K}_{i}^{\alpha} is the corresponding memory contribution, with its trajectory dependence left implicit. Since A^iα\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha} commutes with ℋ^B\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}, and the centered coupling has zero organized contribution under the factorized relevant ensemble, the drift reduces to
viα​(t)=i​Tr​{ρ^rel​(t)​[ℋ^,A^iα]},v_{i}^{\alpha}(t)=i\,{\rm Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}(t)[\hat{\mathcal{H}},\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}]\},
(31)
where the trace is over the electronic Hilbert space; see Appendix A.2. The bath continues to influence the dynamics through memory and fluctuations.
With fiα=λiα/βf_{i}^{\alpha}=\lambda_{i}^{\alpha}/\beta, the electronic relevant state can be written as ρ^rel∝exp⁡[−β​ℋ^∗​[f]]\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}\propto\exp[-\beta\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[f]], where ℋ^∗​[f]\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[f] is the constrained Hamiltonian introduced in Eq. (8). Since ρ^rel\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel} commutes with ℋ^∗\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*} and ℋ^=ℋ^∗−∑j,βfjβ​A^jβ\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}-\sum_{j,\beta}f_{j}^{\beta}\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta}, we obtain
viα\displaystyle v_{i}^{\alpha}
=\displaystyle=
i​Tr​{ρ^rel​[ℋ^∗−∑j,βfjβ​A^jβ,A^iα]}\displaystyle i\,{\rm Tr}\!\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}\left[\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}-\sum_{j,\beta}f_{j}^{\beta}\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta},\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\right]\right\}
(32)
=\displaystyle=
i​∑j,βfjβ​Tr​{ρ^rel​[A^iα,A^jβ]}.\displaystyle i\sum_{j,\beta}f_{j}^{\beta}\,{\rm Tr}\!\left\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha},\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta}]\right\}.
Using the closed operator algebra in Eq. (2), this becomes
viα=−∑β,γCα​β​γϕiγfiβ.v_{i}^{\alpha}=-\sum_{\beta,\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\phi_{i}^{\gamma}f_{i}^{\beta}.
(33)
Together with fiα=−∂F/∂ϕiαf_{i}^{\alpha}=-\partial F/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha} from Eq. (10), this identifies the organized drift with the reversible contribution in Eq. (3), with
ℛi​jα​β=δi​j​∑γCα​β​γ​ϕiγ.\mathcal{R}_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=\delta_{ij}\sum_{\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\phi_{i}^{\gamma}.
(34)
The antisymmetry of ℛ\mathcal{R} ensures that this motion preserves the free energy at fixed temperature. Its Poisson-bracket–like structure follows directly from the microscopic commutation algebra. A complementary interpretation based on the constrained wave-function formulation is presented in Appendix B.
The memory contribution accounts for the influence of the eliminated electronic and environmental degrees of freedom. When their correlations decay rapidly compared with the order-parameter evolution, it can be approximated by a local transport response. The thermal adiabatic reduction additionally eliminates energy as an independent slow variable. As discussed above, this step must account for energy–order-parameter cross couplings: any surviving contributions from the small temperature lag enter the effective order-parameter dynamics, together with the corresponding corrections to its fluctuations.
On the thermal-balance manifold, β≃βB\beta\simeq\beta_{\rm B}, the electronic relevant state becomes the fixed-temperature constrained density matrix ρ^​[ϕ]\hat{\rho}[\bm{\phi}] of Sec. II. Equation (24) then gives the entropy-conjugate force (∂Stot/∂ϕiα)E=βBfiα=−βB∂F/∂ϕiα(\partial S_{\rm tot}/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha})_{E}=\beta_{\rm B}f_{i}^{\alpha}=-\beta_{\rm B}\partial F/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha}. We denote the effective dissipative tensor after eliminating the fast energy variable by Γi​jα​β\Gamma_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}, absorbing the factor βB\beta_{\rm B} into its definition and including the effects of the cross couplings. The symmetric dissipative tensor must be positive semidefinite, ensuring nonnegative dissipative entropy production. Including fluctuations promotes ϕ⁡(t)\bm{\phi}(t) to a stochastic collective field, with a force ηiα​(t)\eta_{i}^{\alpha}(t) whose statistics are related to the effective dissipative response by the fluctuation–dissipation relation.
The reversible and dissipative sectors therefore require different microscopic information. For a specified electronic Hamiltonian ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} and set of retained observables, the constrained state determines the thermodynamic forces, while their commutation algebra fixes the reversible tensor. The dissipative coefficients, by contrast, depend on the dynamics of the eliminated electronic modes and, for relaxation mediated by the environment, on the bath Hamiltonian and system–bath coupling. These environmental ingredients are not specified by ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} alone. Treating Γ\Gamma phenomenologically is therefore a natural practical choice when the environment is not modeled explicitly, provided its form respects the relevant symmetries, conservation laws, and thermodynamic constraints. This retains microscopic thermodynamic forces while parametrizing the kinetics of relaxation.
Under these Markovian and thermal adiabatic approximations, the projected dynamics yields the fixed-temperature AVP structure introduced in Eq. (3). We now turn to its practical implementation for interacting electronic systems.
IV Mean-Field Formulation of the Adiabatic Variational Principle
Practical application of the AVP to an interacting electronic Hamiltonian requires a many-body approximation or numerical solver for the constrained electronic problem, while the AVP construction itself is independent of this choice. The constrained variational problem can, in principle, be formulated within a broad class of many-body representations, including Slater determinants, variational Monte Carlo wave functions, tensor-network states, and neural-network quantum states. From this perspective, the AVP provides a route for extending established equilibrium many-body methods to the dynamics of emergent order. The microscopic solver is applied along a sequence of constrained states specified by the evolving order-parameter configuration and determines, for each ϕ\bm{\phi}, both the corresponding electronic state and the conjugate thermodynamic forces needed to advance the collective dynamics. The chosen solver therefore controls the accuracy and computational cost of the constrained-state calculation.
A particularly accessible realization is a self-consistent mean-field formulation closely related to conventional Hartree–Fock (HF) theory. Constrained variational calculations have a long history, including constrained HF and related self-consistent mean-field methods [73, 74, 75, 76], as well as constrained density-functional theory (DFT) [77, 78, 79]. These methods impose prescribed collective observables through constraining fields and have been widely used to map energy landscapes and extract effective interactions. This established machinery provides a natural starting point for a mean-field implementation of the AVP.
In this work, we adopt this mean-field realization of the AVP. Our aim is to simulate phase-ordering dynamics and the evolution of spatially extended order-parameter textures in large systems over many time steps, for which the comparatively modest computational cost of a mean-field treatment is essential. We formulate the constrained mean-field problem directly at finite temperature using number-conserving fermionic Gaussian density matrices, as employed in our simulations. The conventional zero-temperature Hartree–Fock wave-function formulation then follows as a special case.
Consider a general interacting fermion Hamiltonian
ℋ^=∑i​j∑a​bti​ja​b​c^i,a†​c^j,b+12​∑i​j​k​l∑a​b​c​dVi​j​k​la​b​c​d​c^i,a†​c^j,b†​c^k,c​c^l,d,\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}t_{ij}^{ab}\hat{c}_{i,a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j,b}^{\,}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}\sum_{abcd}V_{ijkl}^{abcd}\hat{c}_{i,a}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j,b}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{k,c}^{\,}\hat{c}_{l,d}^{\,},
(35)
where c^i,a†\hat{c}_{i,a}^{\dagger} (c^i,a\hat{c}_{i,a}^{\,}) creates (annihilates) a fermion at lattice site ii with internal label aa, such as spin or orbital. The coefficients ti​ja​bt_{ij}^{ab} and Vi​j​k​la​b​c​dV_{ijkl}^{abcd} specify the hopping and two-body interactions, respectively. A number-conserving fermionic Gaussian density matrix ρ^G\hat{\rho}_{\rm G} is the normalized exponential of a number-conserving quadratic fermionic operator and is completely characterized by its one-particle density matrix
Gi​ja​b≡Tr⁡(ρ^G​c^j,b†​c^i,a).G_{ij}^{ab}\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\hat{\rho}_{\rm G}\,\hat{c}_{j,b}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i,a}^{\,}\right).
(36)
The single-particle density matrix GG is Hermitian, with eigenvalues between 00 and 11 and Tr​G=Ne\mathrm{Tr}\,G=N_{e}, where NeN_{e} is the average number of electrons. Conversely, any GG satisfying these conditions specifies a number-conserving Gaussian state.
Restricting the expectation value of the interacting Hamiltonian to this Gaussian manifold defines the Hartree–Fock energy functional,
EHF​[G]≡Tr⁡(ρ^G​[G]​ℋ^)\displaystyle E_{\rm HF}[G]\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(\hat{\rho}_{\rm G}[G]\hat{\mathcal{H}}\right)
(37)
=∑i​j∑a​bti​ja​b​Gj​ib​a+12​∑i​j​k​l∑a​b​c​dVi​j​k​la​b​c​d​(Gl​id​a​Gk​jc​b−Gk​ic​a​Gl​jd​b).\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{ij}\sum_{ab}t_{ij}^{ab}G_{ji}^{ba}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{ijkl}\sum_{abcd}V_{ijkl}^{abcd}\left(G_{li}^{da}G_{kj}^{cb}-G_{ki}^{ca}G_{lj}^{db}\right).
The second equality follows from Wick’s theorem: because ρ^G\hat{\rho}_{\rm G} is Gaussian, all interaction expectation values reduce to products of elements of GG, giving the usual direct and exchange contributions. The same Gaussian structure allows the many-body von Neumann entropy to be written entirely in the single-particle space as
SG​[G]=−Tr⁡[G​ln⁡G+(1−G)​ln⁡(1−G)],S_{\rm G}[G]=-\mathrm{Tr}\left[G\ln G+(1-G)\ln(1-G)\right],
(38)
where the trace in this expression is over single-particle states.
For a prescribed instantaneous order-parameter configuration ϕ={ϕiα}\bm{\phi}=\{\phi_{i}^{\alpha}\}, the finite-temperature MF-AVP free-energy Lagrangian is
ℒMF​[G,𝒇;ϕ]=EHF​[G]−T​SG​[G]+∑i,αfiα​(Aiα​[G]−ϕiα).\mathcal{L}_{\rm MF}[G,\bm{f};\bm{\phi}]=E_{\rm HF}[G]-TS_{\rm G}[G]+\sum_{i,\alpha}f_{i}^{\alpha}\left(A_{i}^{\alpha}[G]-\phi_{i}^{\alpha}\right).
(39)
Here Aiα​[G]≡Tr⁡{ρ^G​[G]​A^iα}A_{i}^{\alpha}[G]\equiv\mathrm{Tr}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm G}[G]\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}\} is the expectation value of the corresponding collective observable, and 𝒇={fiα}\bm{f}=\{f_{i}^{\alpha}\} are the conjugate fields enforcing its prescribed value. The first two terms constitute the finite-temperature Hartree–Fock free energy, while the last term imposes the order-parameter constraints. The energetic and constraint contributions define the effective single-particle Hamiltonian
Hi​ja​b​[G,𝒇]≡∂∂Gj​ib​a​[EHF​[G]+∑k,αfkα​Akα​[G]].H_{ij}^{ab}[G,\bm{f}]\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial G_{ji}^{ba}}\left[E_{\rm HF}[G]+\sum_{k,\alpha}f_{k}^{\alpha}A_{k}^{\alpha}[G]\right].
(40)
It contains the usual Hartree–Fock contribution together with the single-particle fields associated with the prescribed order parameters.
At any temperature, stationarity with respect to unitary rotations of GG requires
[H,G]=0.[H,G]=0.
(41)
Such rotations preserve the eigenvalues of GG and hence its Gaussian entropy, while stationarity of the energetic and constraint contributions yields the commutation condition above. Consequently, GG and HH can be diagonalized in the same single-particle basis. In an orbital representation, the corresponding eigenvalue problem is
∑j​bHi​ja​b​[G,𝒇]​ψn​(j,b)=ϵn​ψn​(i,a),\sum_{jb}H_{ij}^{ab}[G,\bm{f}]\psi_{n}(j,b)=\epsilon_{n}\psi_{n}(i,a),
(42)
where ψn\psi_{n} and ϵn\epsilon_{n} are the single-particle eigenvectors and eigenvalues. The distinction between zero and finite temperature then enters through the allowed eigenvalues, or occupations, of GG.
At T=0T=0, a pure constrained Gaussian solution, when available, has the form ρ^G=|ΨHF⟩​⟨ΨHF|\hat{\rho}_{\rm G}=|\Psi_{\rm HF}\rangle\langle\Psi_{\rm HF}|, where |ΨHF⟩|\Psi_{\rm HF}\rangle is a Slater determinant. Its one-particle density matrix is therefore the projector onto the occupied single-particle subspace. Beyond the general properties of GG stated above, the defining additional condition is its idempotency, G2=GG^{2}=G. For a gapped self-consistent solution, the NeN_{e} lowest-energy states of Eq. (42) are occupied, with GG given by the corresponding projector. Degeneracies at the chemical potential or constraints requiring fractional occupations may instead necessitate retaining a mixed Gaussian state at zero temperature.
At finite temperature, the eigenvalues of GG may vary continuously between 00 and 11. Minimization of the constrained free energy with respect to these occupations, at fixed average particle number, gives
G=1eβ⁡(H−μ)+1,G=\frac{1}{e^{\beta(H-\mu)}+1},
(43)
where β=1/T\beta=1/T and the chemical potential μ\mu enforces Tr​G=Ne\mathrm{Tr}\,G=N_{e}. Thus, in the common eigenbasis defined by Eq. (42), the eigenvalues of GG are the Fermi–Dirac occupations nF​(ϵn−μ)n_{\rm F}(\epsilon_{n}-\mu). Equation (43) also makes the relation [H,G]=0[H,G]=0 explicit, while its zero-temperature limit recovers the occupied-state projector when the self-consistent spectrum is gapped at the chemical potential.

Schematic of the MF-AVP workflow. Since H⁡[G,𝒇]H[G,\bm{f}] generally depends on GG, the effective Hamiltonian and density matrix must be determined self-consistently at either zero or finite temperature. At the same time, the fields 𝒇\bm{f} are adjusted until the prescribed constraints Aiα​[G]=ϕiαA_{i}^{\alpha}[G]=\phi_{i}^{\alpha} are satisfied. Components of GG not fixed directly by these constraints remain variational and are determined as part of the same constrained solution. Once convergence is reached, the fields 𝒇\bm{f} provide the thermodynamic forces identified in Eq. (10).
Although Eq. (42) gives a spectral representation of the mean-field problem, MF-AVP requires only the density matrix GG and the observables derived from it, without requiring explicit access to every single-particle eigenvector. Here we obtain these quantities by GPU-accelerated diagonalization of the effective single-particle Hamiltonian. Alternative implementations include the kernel polynomial method and related stochastic density-matrix techniques, rational or continued-fraction approximations to the Fermi–Dirac matrix function, and Green-function recursion or contour-integration methods [80, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85, 86]. Depending on the Hamiltonian and constraints, only selected matrix elements or traces involving GG may be needed, avoiding construction and storage of the full density matrix. Pairing orders can also be treated using Hartree–Fock–Bogoliubov states, with a generalized density matrix that includes anomalous correlations.
The constrained mean-field calculation forms the inner loop of the MF-AVP simulation summarized in Fig. 3. For a prescribed instantaneous configuration ϕ⁡(t)\bm{\phi}(t), trial values of GG and 𝒇\bm{f} define the constrained effective Hamiltonian H⁡[G,𝒇]H[G,\bm{f}]. Solving the single-particle problem updates GG and the observables Aiα​[G]A_{i}^{\alpha}[G], while any unconstrained mean fields are determined self-consistently. The fields fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} are adjusted using the constraint residuals Aiα​[G]−ϕiα​(t)A_{i}^{\alpha}[G]-\phi_{i}^{\alpha}(t), and the iteration continues until mean-field self-consistency and the prescribed constraints are satisfied. Details of the constraint updates for CDW ordering in the tt–VV model and SDW ordering in the Hubbard model are given in Appendices C and D, respectively.
The converged fields fiαf_{i}^{\alpha} then enter the effective equation of motion as thermodynamic forces. The outer loop advances the order parameters to ϕ⁡(t+Δ​t)\bm{\phi}(t+\Delta t), defining the next constrained electronic problem. Repeating this procedure generates a dynamical trajectory along which the microscopic state adjusts to the evolving order parameters. This nested scheme implements the AVP in the applications below. More sophisticated many-body solvers can replace the constrained mean-field calculation without changing the overall dynamical structure, as discussed further in the concluding section.
V Charge-Density-Wave Ordering Dynamics
CDW order is widespread in low-dimensional electronic materials [87], with layered transition-metal dichalcogenides providing prominent examples of commensurate and incommensurate ordering [88, 89]. The formation and relaxation of these spatial modulations motivate microscopic studies of charge-ordering dynamics. Here we apply MF-AVP to charge ordering on the square and triangular lattices at half filling and one-third filling, respectively. The square lattice provides a benchmark through conventional Allen–Cahn coarsening between two symmetry-related checkerboard states. The triangular lattice supports three-sublattice 3×3\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3} order, whose three equivalent realizations give rise to a richer network of domain walls and junctions. These examples explore how ordering patterns and defect structures shape the collective dynamics and the electronic structure of the evolving textures.
Both systems are described by the spinless tt–VV Hamiltonian,
ℋ^=−tnn∑⟨i​j⟩(c^i†c^j+h.c.)+V∑⟨i​j⟩n^in^j,\hat{\mathcal{H}}=-t_{\rm nn}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left(\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}^{\,}+{\rm h.c.}\right)+V\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\hat{n}_{i}\hat{n}_{j},
(44)
where c^i†\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger} (c^i\hat{c}_{i}^{\,}) creates (annihilates) a spinless fermion at site ii, n^i=c^i†​c^i\hat{n}_{i}=\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}^{\,} is the local density operator, and ⟨i​j⟩\langle ij\rangle denotes nearest-neighbor bonds, each counted once. The hopping amplitude tnnt_{\rm nn} sets the electronic energy scale, while V>0V>0 describes nearest-neighbor repulsion.
V.1 Charge-density-wave coarsening on the square lattice
We first consider the square lattice at half filling, where the tendency toward checkerboard CDW order can be understood from complementary weak- and strong-coupling perspectives. In the weak-coupling limit, the noninteracting nearest-neighbor band has a perfectly nested Fermi surface with nesting vector 𝑸=(π,π)\bm{Q}=(\pi,\pi). The resulting divergence of the particle-hole susceptibility at zero temperature drives an instability toward checkerboard charge order. In the opposite limit V/tnn≫1V/t_{\rm nn}\gg 1, the origin of this order is transparent in real space: at tnn=0t_{\rm nn}=0, the repulsive interaction is minimized by occupying one sublattice and leaving the other empty, avoiding all occupied nearest-neighbor pairs. Finite hopping introduces charge fluctuations around these configurations, but the preference for two-sublattice order persists in the strong-coupling regime. The two limits thus lead to the same ordering pattern, with two symmetry-related states distinguished by which sublattice has the higher density.
Denoting the coarse-grained sublattice densities by nAn_{A} and nBn_{B}, the scalar order parameter ϕ=nA−nB\phi=n_{A}-n_{B} changes sign under sublattice interchange. The CDW therefore breaks a discrete Z2Z_{2} symmetry, with the same symmetry-breaking structure as the Ising model. Its long-wavelength energetics is conventionally described by the scalar Ginzburg–Landau functional
FGL​[ϕ]=∫d2​r​[κ2​|∇ϕ|2+a2​ϕ2+b4​ϕ4],F_{\rm GL}[\phi]=\int d^{2}r\left[\frac{\kappa}{2}|\nabla\phi|^{2}+\frac{a}{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{b}{4}\phi^{4}\right],
(45)
with a<0a<0 and b>0b>0 in the ordered phase. Purely relaxational dynamics, ∂tϕ=−γδFGL/δϕ\partial_{t}\phi=-\gamma\,\delta F_{\rm GL}/\delta\phi, then yields curvature-driven coarsening of a nonconserved scalar order parameter. Here we retain a dissipative kinetic description but obtain the thermodynamic forces from the microscopic tt–VV model through the MF-AVP, allowing us to follow both the evolving domains and their associated electronic structure without prescribing FGLF_{\rm GL}.

For a spatially inhomogeneous configuration, we define the local checkerboard order parameter as
ϕi=(−1)xi+yi​(ni−14​∑j∈𝒩⁡(i)nj),\phi_{i}=(-1)^{x_{i}+y_{i}}\left(n_{i}-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}n_{j}\right),
(46)
where (xi,yi)(x_{i},y_{i}) are the integer lattice coordinates of site ii and 𝒩⁡(i)\mathcal{N}(i) denotes its four nearest neighbors. The density contrast measures the local charge modulation between opposite sublattices, while the factor (−1)xi+yi(-1)^{x_{i}+y_{i}} removes its checkerboard alternation. The resulting field has approximately uniform sign within each domain, with opposite signs distinguishing the two ordered states. For the dynamics, we retain the local densities nin_{i} themselves as the collective variables and evaluate ϕi\phi_{i} from Eq. (46). This avoids reconstructing the density profile from the order-parameter field, which additionally requires specifying the uniform density component.
The simulations are performed at fixed chemical potential, chosen beforehand at the center of the single-particle gap of the reference, perfectly ordered CDW state and held fixed during the evolution. The total particle number is allowed to fluctuate, corresponding to an effective reservoir that permits particle exchange. In this setting, FF denotes the constrained grand potential. For a prescribed instantaneous density profile 𝒏={ni}\bm{n}=\{n_{i}\}, the auxiliary MF-AVP Lagrangian is
ℒMF​[G,𝒇;𝒏]=EHF​[G]−T​SG​[G]−μ​Tr​G+∑ifi​(Gi​i−ni).\mathcal{L}_{\rm MF}[G,\bm{f};\bm{n}]=E_{\rm HF}[G]-TS_{\rm G}[G]-\mu\,\mathrm{Tr}\,G+\sum_{i}f_{i}(G_{ii}-n_{i}).
(47)
Here Gi​iG_{ii} is the on-site density of the trial mean-field state, whereas nin_{i} is the prescribed dynamical variable. The conjugate fields 𝒇={fi}\bm{f}=\{f_{i}\} enforce Gi​i=niG_{ii}=n_{i} at convergence and provide the thermodynamic forces fi=−∂F/∂nif_{i}=-\partial F/\partial n_{i}.
Applying the general MF-AVP construction to Eq. (47) gives the effective quadratic Hamiltonian
ℋ^HF\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm HF}
=−∑⟨i​j⟩[(tnn+VGi​j)c^i†c^j+h.c.]\displaystyle=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\left[(t_{\rm nn}+VG_{ij})\hat{c}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j}^{\,}+{\rm h.c.}\right]
(48)
+∑i(fi+V∑j∈𝒩⁡(i)Gj​j)n^i,\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\left(f_{i}+V\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}(i)}G_{jj}\right)\hat{n}_{i},
where Gi​j=⟨c^j†​c^i⟩G_{ij}=\langle\hat{c}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i}^{\,}\rangle. The interaction generates a site-dependent Hartree potential and a Fock correction to the hopping, while fif_{i} enforces the prescribed local density. The chemical potential enters the occupations of this Hamiltonian. For each instantaneous 𝒏\bm{n}, the constraint fields and unconstrained components of GG are determined self-consistently until Gi​i=niG_{ii}=n_{i}. Details of this constrained HF calculation are given in Appendix C.
Because the density operators commute, [n^i,n^j]=0[\hat{n}_{i},\hat{n}_{j}]=0, the organized reversible contribution vanishes for this choice of retained variables. We adopt local dissipative dynamics,
d​nid​t=−Γ​∂F∂ni+ηi​(t)=Γ​fi+ηi​(t),\frac{dn_{i}}{dt}=-\Gamma\frac{\partial F}{\partial n_{i}}+\eta_{i}(t)=\Gamma f_{i}+\eta_{i}(t),
(49)
where Γ\Gamma is a phenomenological kinetic coefficient and ηi​(t)\eta_{i}(t) is Gaussian thermal noise satisfying the fluctuation–dissipation relation. This local relaxation law allows the total density to fluctuate, consistently with the fixed-chemical-potential setting above. The forces are obtained directly from the constrained electronic state, without constructing or numerically differentiating a free-energy landscape. We retain Γ\Gamma phenomenologically to represent the dissipative response of the eliminated degrees of freedom and the reservoir; its microscopic determination would require specifying their dynamics and couplings. Together, the constrained HF calculation and Eq. (49) implement the inner and outer loops of Fig. 3, providing the dynamics examined below.
We now turn to the CDW coarsening dynamics summarized in Fig. 4. The simulations are performed on a 64×6464\times 64 square lattice with V/tnn=0.5V/t_{\rm nn}=0.5, electronic temperature T/tnn=0.1T/t_{\rm nn}=0.1, and fixed chemical potential μ/tnn=1\mu/t_{\rm nn}=1. As discussed before, the nearest-neighbor hopping tnnt_{\rm nn} is used as the electronic energy unit, while time is measured in units of (Γ​tnn)−1(\Gamma t_{\rm nn})^{-1}. The initial density profile is obtained from an electronic calculation with random on-site potentials. During the subsequent MF-AVP evolution, the constrained mean-field problem is solved using GPU-accelerated exact diagonalization, providing the thermodynamic forces and the instantaneous electronic structure at each step.
Figure 4(a) shows the local checkerboard order parameter ϕi\phi_{i} defined in Eq. (46), whose sign distinguishes the two symmetry-related ordered states. The initially disordered configuration develops domains of both states, which subsequently grow and coalesce. The corresponding fields fif_{i}, shown in Fig. 4(b), are conjugate to the dynamical densities nin_{i}; their staggered pattern reflects the alternating density adjustments associated with checkerboard ordering. As coarsening proceeds, appreciable forces become increasingly concentrated near domain walls, while the interiors of well-formed domains are nearly relaxed. The microscopic force distribution thus identifies the interfaces as the regions responsible for the continuing collective evolution.
The constrained electronic calculation also reveals how the evolving domain pattern shapes the electronic spectrum. Figure 4(c) shows the local density of states near the Fermi level, obtained from the instantaneous mean-field eigenvalues ϵn\epsilon_{n} and eigenvectors ψn,i\psi_{n,i} as ρi​(ω)=∑n|ψn,i|2​δ​(ω−ϵn)\rho_{i}(\omega)=\sum_{n}|\psi_{n,i}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{n}). The eigenvalues and eigenvectors are those of the constrained mean-field Hamiltonian in Eq. (48), including the fields fif_{i} that enforce the instantaneous density configuration. As CDW order develops, low-energy spectral weight is strongly suppressed within the ordered domains but remains concentrated along their boundaries. These interfacial states lie within the bulk CDW gap, providing an electronic signature of the defects. MF-AVP thus follows both the coarsening of the domain pattern and the accompanying evolution of the electronic states supported by its interfaces.

The growth of this characteristic length is approximately algebraic,
L⁡(t)∼tα,L(t)\sim t^{\alpha},
(50)
with an exponent consistent with the Allen–Cahn value α=1/2\alpha=1/2 for a nonconserved scalar order parameter. This growth accompanies the smoothing of domain walls and the disappearance of smaller domains visible in Fig. 4(a). Together, the correlation collapse and the growth law support conventional curvature-driven coarsening over the accessible regime, before the domain size becomes comparable to the system dimensions.
This behavior can be understood in terms of interfaces whose local profiles relax more rapidly than the domain pattern evolves. The nearest-neighbor repulsion favors alternating charge densities, so disruptions of this pattern across a domain wall carry an interaction-energy cost. Electronic hopping, exchange, and thermal occupations also contribute to the wall profile and its free energy. Once the interfaces acquire approximately size-independent tension and mobility, their subsequent motion is governed primarily by curvature: the interfacial tension produces a driving force proportional to the local curvature, and a finite mobility converts this force into a wall velocity. For a domain pattern characterized by the scale LL, the typical curvature is of order 1/L1/L, giving d​L/d​t∝1/LdL/dt\propto 1/L and hence L⁡(t)∼t1/2L(t)\sim t^{1/2} [35]. The observed scaling is consistent with this picture, in which the microscopic electronic structure sets the interfacial properties and the coarsening rate while leaving the large-scale growth exponent unchanged.
A complementary comparison is provided by the classical lattice-gas simulations in Appendix E. The model ℋcl=V​∑⟨i​j⟩ni​nj\mathcal{H}_{\rm cl}=V\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}n_{i}n_{j} isolates the repulsion-driven preference for checkerboard order and also exhibits L⁡(t)∼t1/2L(t)\sim t^{1/2} growth under the nearest-neighbor particle-hopping kMC dynamics used there. Those updates conserve particle number, whereas the present MF-AVP simulations allow local density relaxation at fixed chemical potential; the two calculations therefore employ distinct microscopic dynamics. Their common growth law illustrates that different microscopic evolution rules can reach the same collective coarsening regime. For the MF-AVP, the recovery of Allen–Cahn scaling provides a successful benchmark of the constrained microscopic forces, while the simultaneous resolution of interfacial electronic states demonstrates the additional information retained beyond a phenomenological order-parameter description.
V.2 CDW Coarsening on the Triangular Lattice

(a) Local two-component CDW order parameter ϕi\bm{\phi}_{i}, represented by the triangular color map whose vertices correspond to the three symmetry-related ordered states. The evolving configuration develops well-defined Z3Z_{3} domains separated by a network of domain walls and junctions.

We next consider the spinless tt–VV model of Eq. (44) on the triangular lattice at one-third filling, where three-sublattice charge ordering has been studied in this model and related interacting-fermion settings [90, 91]. Unlike the half-filled square lattice, the noninteracting Fermi surface is not perfectly nested, and charge order develops only beyond a finite interaction strength [91]. The structure of the ordered phase is especially transparent in the strong-coupling limit. For V/tnn→∞V/t_{\rm nn}\rightarrow\infty, the nearest-neighbor repulsion is minimized by occupying one of three interpenetrating sublattices while leaving the other two empty, so that no occupied nearest-neighbor pairs occur. This 3×3\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3} charge modulation triples the unit cell and has three symmetry-related realizations, corresponding to the choice of occupied sublattice. The ordered state therefore breaks a discrete Z3Z_{3} translation symmetry, with a symmetry-breaking structure analogous to that of the three-state Potts or clock model.
A convenient representation of this order is provided by a two-component field ϕ=(ϕ1,ϕ2)\bm{\phi}=(\phi^{1},\phi^{2}). Denoting the densities on the three sublattices by nAn_{A}, nBn_{B}, and nCn_{C}, we define
ϕ1=nA+nB−2​nC6,ϕ2=nA−nB2.\phi^{1}=\frac{n_{A}+n_{B}-2n_{C}}{\sqrt{6}},\qquad\phi^{2}=\frac{n_{A}-n_{B}}{\sqrt{2}}.
(51)
These combinations separate the charge modulation from the uniform density component. The three symmetry-related ordered states correspond to values 𝚽A\bm{\Phi}_{A}, 𝚽B\bm{\Phi}_{B}, and 𝚽C\bm{\Phi}_{C} at the vertices of an equilateral triangle in the (ϕ1,ϕ2)(\phi^{1},\phi^{2}) plane, with their distance from the origin set by the ordering amplitude. This representation connects the CDW to continuum descriptions of three-state ordering. For example, a phenomenological Ginzburg–Landau functional with the required threefold structure can be written as [92]
FGL​[ϕ]=∫d2​r​[|∇ϕ|2+∏X=A,B,C|ϕ−𝚽X|2],F_{\rm GL}[\bm{\phi}]=\int d^{2}r\,\left[|\nabla\bm{\phi}|^{2}+\prod_{X=A,B,C}|\bm{\phi}-\bm{\Phi}_{X}|^{2}\right],
(52)
with coefficients set to unity for illustration. Nonconserved relaxational dynamics generated by such a functional describes the growth of domains associated with the three degenerate minima [93, 92].
The defect structure is richer than in the two-state Ising case because three domain walls can meet at Y-shaped junctions. For equal, isotropic interfacial tensions, local mechanical balance gives 120∘120^{\circ} angles between the walls [92]. Although the discrete manifold of the three ordered states does not support the usual π1\pi_{1} classification of vortices [94], the junctions can be distinguished by the cyclic ordering of the surrounding domains: A→B→CA\to B\to C and A→C→BA\to C\to B, traversed counterclockwise, define opposite chiralities. These junctions remain attached to domain walls and evolve as part of the wall network, introducing geometrical constraints and rearrangement processes absent in the Ising problem.
Studies of nonconserved three-state ordering provide a useful reference for the resulting coarsening dynamics [95, 96, 97, 93, 98, 99]. When domain walls and junctions remain mobile and their kinetic coefficients approach size-independent values, growth is expected to be governed primarily by curvature-driven domain-wall motion, which reduces the total interfacial energy and yields the conventional law L⁡(t)∼t1/2L(t)\sim t^{1/2}. The approach to this regime can nevertheless be complicated by junction rearrangements, lattice pinning, and long-lived multidomain configurations; at zero temperature, trapping can even prevent continued coarsening [95, 96, 92]. The three-state domain network can therefore exhibit substantial intermediate-time structure even when its eventual growth is consistent with Allen–Cahn scaling.
In the MF-AVP, the microscopic functional FAVP​[ϕ]F_{\rm AVP}[\bm{\phi}] is obtained from the constrained electronic problem, rather than prescribed through the empirical form in Eq. (52). The threefold ordering landscape and the energetics of domain walls and junctions thus follow from the underlying lattice Hamiltonian within the mean-field approximation. We implement this construction using the same constrained-density dynamics as in Sec. V.1, taking the local densities nin_{i} as the slow variables. To characterize spatially inhomogeneous order, we construct a local two-component field on each upward-pointing elementary triangle by substituting its three vertex densities into Eq. (51). The vertices are labeled according to a fixed global three-sublattice assignment, and the resulting vector is associated with the triangle center. This field resolves the local amplitude and orientation of the charge order, distinguishing the three symmetry-related domains and their intervening defects. Both components are combinations of commuting density observables, so their two-component representation does not introduce a reversible contribution. The constrained HF implementation is described in Appendix C.

Resolving the long-time behavior requires larger systems and longer trajectories, which remain computationally demanding because each MF-AVP time step involves repeated solutions of the constrained electronic problem. This motivates the development of machine-learning surrogates for the microscopic thermodynamic forces, as discussed in the outlook.
VI Adiabatic Spin-Density-Wave Dynamics
The CDW examples considered above involve commuting local density operators, for which the reversible tensor ℛ\mathcal{R} vanishes. A qualitatively different situation arises for SDW order: the three components of the local magnetic moment inherit the noncommuting algebra of the electronic spin operators and generate reversible precession alongside dissipative relaxation. We demonstrate this aspect of the AVP using the repulsive Hubbard model on the two-dimensional square lattice. Both the orientations and magnitudes of the local moments are allowed to evolve, describing the development of magnetic correlations and the accompanying inhomogeneous spin textures.
The constrained electronic construction discussed in Sec. IV has particularly close precedents in magnetism. Following the general formulation of constrained density-functional theory [77], constraints on local magnetic moments have been used to characterize noncollinear configurations that are not stationary states of the unconstrained electronic problem [100, 101, 102, 103]. These constraints can be enforced through Lagrange multipliers or through penalty functionals that penalize deviations from the prescribed configuration [104, 105]. In both approaches, the constraining fields provide access to the electronic response associated with departures from magnetic equilibrium.
The use of electronic calculations to generate magnetization dynamics was developed in the pioneering ab initio spin-dynamics work of Antropov and collaborators [106, 107], together with related formulations of adiabatic local-moment dynamics [108, 109]. The electronic state adjusts to a slowly evolving magnetic configuration, and the resulting effective fields drive its motion. Subsequent implementations have employed constrained electronic states, using Lagrange-multiplier or penalty-based approaches, to follow magnetic relaxation and precession without relying on a fixed pairwise spin Hamiltonian [110, 105, 111, 112]. The SDW application of the AVP shares this central construction, and the connection between constraining fields and effective magnetic fields has been examined explicitly within this literature [113, 114].
A separate issue concerns the dynamical variables retained in the magnetic description. Conventional fixed-length Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert dynamics follows the orientations of local moments, while the formation and relaxation of itinerant SDW order can also involve substantial changes in their magnitudes. Established treatments of such longitudinal relaxation include Landau–Lifshitz–Bloch theory, which describes a thermally averaged magnetization of variable magnitude [115, 116, 117], and generalized Langevin dynamics, which treats individual local-moment magnitudes as dynamical variables [118]. Stochastic formulations incorporate the associated thermal fluctuations [119], and longitudinal relaxation has also been included in semiclassical SDW dynamics [120]. These developments concern the structure of the magnetic equation of motion and are distinct from the electronic method used to determine its driving forces.
Here these two aspects meet in a microscopic description of magnetic ordering in itinerant electron systems. The AVP constraints identify the evolving local moments with the spin expectation values of the instantaneous electronic state, while the collective dynamics allows their orientations and amplitudes to evolve. Our focus is the formation and evolution of spatially extended magnetic textures, encompassing both the development of local moments and the growth of orientational correlations. Following magnetic ordering on these scales requires repeated constrained electronic calculations over the full lattice as the texture evolves. The MF-AVP simulations below address this computational challenge, extending the microscopic description developed for charge ordering to itinerant magnetism.

As amplitude fluctuations become increasingly costly in the local-moment regime, MiM_{i} becomes approximately fixed and the remaining dynamics approaches the familiar Landau–Lifshitz evolution of moment orientations. Retaining both channels allows the MF-AVP to follow itinerant SDW textures with evolving magnetic amplitudes, using the constrained electronic calculation to supply the driving fields throughout the trajectory.

A more systematic study of SDW ordering would track correlations of the staggered magnetization to extract a growing domain scale, alongside the staggered structure factor. These measures could distinguish continued domain growth from saturation caused by thermal fluctuations or the finite lattice size. In the isotropic two-dimensional system studied here, the equilibrium correlation length remains finite at nonzero temperature. Extending the calculations to three dimensions would allow the same framework to examine the growth of true Néel long-range order and the approach to its ordering transition.
The stochastic AVP dynamics also offers a route to the equilibrium statistical physics of itinerant magnetism. When the noise and dissipative response are chosen to yield the appropriate stationary distribution, long Langevin trajectories can sample spatially varying SDW configurations according to their constrained free energy. Such sampling incorporates fluctuations of the order-parameter field absent from a spatially uniform mean-field treatment and may be especially useful at intermediate UU, where both the formation and orientation of local moments matter. Reaching the system sizes and sampling times needed for this program would require many constrained electronic calculations. In the Outlook, we discuss how machine-learning surrogates for the free energy and its forces could reduce this cost while maintaining the thermodynamic consistency of the dynamics.
VII Discussion and Outlook
In this work, we have developed the Adiabatic Variational Principle (AVP) as a framework for connecting microscopic many-body calculations to the nonequilibrium dynamics of emergent electronic order. Under a separation of time scales, each instantaneous order-parameter configuration defines a constrained quasi-stationary electronic state, whose Lagrange multipliers provide the thermodynamic forces driving the collective evolution. Nonlinear Mori–Zwanzig projection theory places this construction within a statistical-mechanical framework: the intrinsic organized motion determines the reversible sector, while the eliminated degrees of freedom generate memory, dissipation, and fluctuations. In the Markovian adiabatic limit, the resulting dynamics provides a microscopic route to phase-field-like simulations without prescribing an empirical free-energy functional.
The mean-field applications demonstrate this construction for charge- and spin-density-wave dynamics in interacting lattice models. The square-lattice tt–VV model recovers Allen–Cahn coarsening, while the triangular lattice exhibits slower intermediate-time growth within a network of threefold domains and persistent junctions. Both calculations resolve the evolving textures together with their conjugate forces and defect-associated electronic states. The Hubbard-model application extends this description to magnetic ordering, with Landau–Lifshitz precession emerging from the spin commutation algebra. The simulations capture rapid local-moment formation followed by slower orientational ordering, through which antiferromagnetic correlations spread across the finite two-dimensional lattice.
These demonstrations use a mean-field electronic solver, but the AVP construction is independent of this choice. Extending the constrained calculation to more correlated many-body states is therefore a natural next step. The present simulations inherit the limitations of fermionic Gaussian states, particularly in their treatment of strong correlations and the electronic structure of defects. Variational Monte Carlo, matrix-product and other tensor-network states, and neural-network quantum states offer routes to retaining additional correlations within the same AVP construction. Density-functional implementations provide a complementary route toward material-specific applications. Constrained DFT and adiabatic spin dynamics already realize closely related constructions for magnetic degrees of freedom [77, 107, 104]. The broader AVP framework motivates extending this strategy to other collective electronic variables, including charge and orbital order, and to their coupled evolution.
Alongside improvements in the constrained electronic state, a more complete microscopic description requires a corresponding treatment of the kinetics. Although the thermodynamic forces are obtained from the constrained electronic problem, the dissipative coefficients in the present simulations remain phenomenological. Evaluating the transport kernels of the projected dynamics would connect these coefficients and their associated fluctuations to microscopic electronic processes and system–bath coupling [48, 70]. Retaining their temporal structure would also allow finite-memory corrections beyond the Markovian approximation. When additional degrees of freedom evolve on comparable time scales, they must instead be incorporated explicitly into the retained slow sector [39]. These extensions would clarify how microscopic relaxation controls collective kinetics and broaden the range of nonequilibrium regimes accessible to the framework.
Machine learning (ML) offers a practical route to extending AVP simulations to the length and time scales needed to resolve collective textures. Even at the mean-field level, each dynamical time step requires an iterative electronic calculation, making repeated evaluations of the thermodynamic forces a major computational cost. ML surrogates have enabled substantial advances in ab initio molecular dynamics [121, 122, 123, 124, 125] and multiscale lattice-Hamiltonian simulations [126, 127, 128, 129, 130, 131], where electronic calculations typically supply energies or forces for independently specified atomic or classical variables within an adiabatic description. The AVP provides the link needed to extend this ML strategy to emergent electronic order by defining the constrained microscopic states and conjugate thermodynamic forces associated with evolving order-parameter configurations. These calculations supply training data for ML models of the constrained free energy or its forces. Once trained, the ML surrogates could replace repeated constrained electronic calculations during the dynamics, enabling simulations of larger defect networks and longer collective relaxation times.
A further opportunity is to explore how preparation protocols control the formation and evolution of electronic textures. Extending AVP simulations beyond sudden thermal quenches to controlled cooling and annealing would allow studies of cooling-rate-dependent defect production and subsequent relaxation in the context of Kibble–Zurek physics [132, 133, 134, 135]. Such protocols must remain compatible with rapid microscopic relaxation, even as the collective variables fall out of equilibrium near a transition. This separation between microscopic adjustment and collective relaxation is the regime targeted by the AVP. By resolving both the resulting textures and their electronic structure, these simulations could connect defect densities and spatial correlations measured by real-space imaging, including magnetic imaging [136], with local spectroscopic signatures of defect-associated electronic states [31, 32].
The same ability to follow collective textures together with their electronic structure motivates applications to more complex forms of emergent order. Multicomponent and intertwined phases, including superconductivity coupled to charge or spin order [9, 137, 138], provide settings in which several collective fields compete, coexist, and evolve through coupled reversible and dissipative channels. Their dynamics could reveal how microscopic electronic structure influences the formation and transformation of heterogeneous phases. Extensions beyond conventional symmetry breaking, including Mott transitions and quantum spin liquids, present further intriguing possibilities, although they require suitable collective variables and constrained state manifolds. These directions would broaden the scope of the AVP from ordering dynamics to the nonequilibrium evolution of correlated quantum phases more generally.

Appendix A Nonlinear Projection Formalism
This Appendix summarizes the nonlinear projection formalism underlying Sec. III and clarifies the role of bath centering in the organized motion. We first specify the relevant ensemble, projection operator, and equations for the macroscopic means and their fluctuations, then apply the organized-drift construction to the system–bath setting of the AVP.
A.1 Relevant Ensemble and Projected Dynamics
We follow the nonlinear projection construction of Ref. [48], with notation adapted to the present application; see also Ref. [70]. For the AVP, the relevant observables include the system energy, bath energy, and order parameters. The electronic reduced state of Eq. (65) then has the constrained Gibbs form in Eq. (23). A.2 Bath Centering and Organized Motion
For the system–bath construction used in the main text, the relevant ensemble factorizes as ρ^reltot=ρ^rel⊗ρ^B,rel\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}^{\rm tot}=\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}\otimes\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}, where ρ^rel\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel} denotes the electronic relevant state. Define the bath-averaged interaction by ℋ¯SB=TrB​{ρ^B,rel​ℋ^SB}\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}={\rm Tr}_{\rm B}\{\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}\}, an operator on the electronic Hilbert space. For any electronic observable A^\hat{A}, Eq. (68) gives
vA\displaystyle v_{A}
=i​TrSB​{ρ^reltot​[ℋ^tot,A^]}\displaystyle=i\,{\rm Tr}_{\rm SB}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}^{\rm tot}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm tot},\hat{A}]\}
(76)
=i​TrS​{ρ^rel​[ℋ^+ℋ¯SB,A^]},\displaystyle=i\,{\rm Tr}_{\rm S}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB},\hat{A}]\},
since [ℋ^B,A^]=0[\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B},\hat{A}]=0. The direct bath contribution to organized motion is therefore determined by the average interaction.
In the main text, we retain the prescribed lattice Hamiltonian ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} and assume that its coupling to the reference thermal bath satisfies ℋ¯SB=0\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}=0. This is a standard assumption in open-system treatments [139, 140, 72], satisfied naturally by linear coupling to harmonic-bath displacements about equilibrium, as illustrated below. It excludes an additional static bath-average field, but does not set the coupling operator or its fluctuations to zero. Under this assumption, the organized drift becomes
vA=i​TrS​{ρ^rel​[ℋ^,A^]}.v_{A}=i\,{\rm Tr}_{\rm S}\{\hat{\rho}_{\rm rel}[\hat{\mathcal{H}},\hat{A}]\}.
(77)
Taking A^=ℋ^\hat{A}=\hat{\mathcal{H}} gives vE=0v_{E}=0, while taking A^=A^iα\hat{A}=\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha} yields the intrinsic order-parameter drift derived in the main text.
A thermal free-boson bath illustrates why the zero-average condition need not require fine-tuning. For ℋ^B=∑qωq​b^q†​b^q\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm B}=\sum_{q}\omega_{q}\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{q}, the averages satisfy ⟨b^q⟩B=⟨b^q†⟩B=0\langle\hat{b}_{q}\rangle_{\rm B}=\langle\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}\rangle_{\rm B}=0. A linear coupling ℋ^SB=∑qS^q⊗(gq​b^q+gq∗​b^q†)\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}=\sum_{q}\hat{S}_{q}\otimes(g_{q}\hat{b}_{q}+g_{q}^{*}\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}), with Hermitian electronic operators S^q\hat{S}_{q}, is therefore already centered, independently of the coefficients gqg_{q} and the choice of S^q\hat{S}_{q}. The condition follows from the vanishing mean displacement of the thermal bath, rather than from a cancellation imposed on the electronic state. Rapid decay of bath correlations is a separate dynamical requirement.
For more general couplings, the bath average need not vanish. At a fixed reference bath state, a nonzero average can be absorbed into the system Hamiltonian by the standard centering procedure [139, 140, 72],
ℋ^′=ℋ^+ℋ¯SB,ℋ^SB′=ℋ^SB−ℋ¯SB⊗I^B.\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}+\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB},\qquad\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{\rm SB}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}-\overline{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}\otimes\hat{I}_{\rm B}.
(78)
The total Hamiltonian is unchanged, while TrB​{ρ^B,rel​ℋ^SB′}=0{\rm Tr}_{\rm B}\{\hat{\rho}_{{\rm B},{\rm rel}}\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}_{\rm SB}\}=0. This rearrangement redefines the electronic Hamiltonian and hence the energy and constrained variational problem associated with it. For example, a coupling ℋ^SB=∑qgq​S^q⊗b^q†​b^q\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm SB}=\sum_{q}g_{q}\hat{S}_{q}\otimes\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{q}, with real gqg_{q}, has average ∑qgq​nq​S^q\sum_{q}g_{q}n_{q}\hat{S}_{q}, where nq=(eβB​ωq−1)−1n_{q}=(e^{\beta_{\rm B}\omega_{q}}-1)^{-1}. Centering replaces b^q†​b^q\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{q} by b^q†​b^q−nq\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{q}-n_{q} and includes the average contribution in ℋ^′\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{\prime}. Thus, zero bath average is an assumption about the coupling to the prescribed ℋ^\hat{\mathcal{H}} in the present application, not a requirement of the AVP itself.
Centering does not eliminate fluctuations: for example, ⟨(b^q+b^q†)2⟩B=2​nq+1\langle(\hat{b}_{q}+\hat{b}_{q}^{\dagger})^{2}\rangle_{\rm B}=2n_{q}+1 remains nonzero. Nor does factorization of the relevant ensemble imply factorization of the actual evolving state. Bath correlations and system–bath correlations generated by the full dynamics continue to contribute through memory and fluctuating forces. Thus, vE=0v_{E}=0 removes only the organized energy drift, not energy exchange or relaxation.
Appendix B Wave-Function Interpretation of the Reversible Dynamics
The reversible tensor derived from nonlinear projection theory in Sec. III also follows directly from the constrained wave-function formulation of Sec. II. This complementary derivation makes transparent how the microscopic commutation algebra converts the conjugate thermodynamic fields into reversible order-parameter motion.
Consider an instantaneous constrained state |Ψ⁡[ϕ]⟩|\Psi[\bm{\phi}]\rangle. Under unitary evolution generated by the electronic Hamiltonian, i​d​|Ψ⟩/d​t=ℋ^​|Ψ⟩i\,d|\Psi\rangle/dt=\hat{\mathcal{H}}|\Psi\rangle, the Hamiltonian contribution to the order-parameter velocity is
(d​ϕiαd​t)R=i⁡⟨Ψ|[ℋ^,A^iα]|Ψ⟩.\left(\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}{dt}\right)_{\!R}=i\langle\Psi|[\hat{\mathcal{H}},\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}]|\Psi\rangle.
(79)
This expression evaluates the instantaneous Hamiltonian contribution at the constrained state; it does not assume that unitary evolution preserves the constrained manifold. The full AVP dynamics also includes relaxation and fluctuations.
The variational stationarity condition provides a simple way to evaluate Eq. (79). From Eqs. (7) and (8), the constrained state is an eigenstate of the deformed Hamiltonian ℋ^∗​[𝒇]\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}[\bm{f}], so that ⟨Ψ|[ℋ^∗,A^iα]|Ψ⟩=0\langle\Psi|[\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*},\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha}]|\Psi\rangle=0. Substituting ℋ^=ℋ^∗−∑j,βfjβ​A^jβ\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}^{*}-\sum_{j,\beta}f_{j}^{\beta}\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta} therefore gives
(d​ϕiαd​t)R=i​∑j,βfjβ​⟨Ψ|[A^iα,A^jβ]|Ψ⟩.\left(\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}{dt}\right)_{\!R}=i\sum_{j,\beta}f_{j}^{\beta}\langle\Psi|[\hat{A}_{i}^{\alpha},\hat{A}_{j}^{\beta}]|\Psi\rangle.
Once the microscopic state has been optimized at fixed ϕ\bm{\phi}, the instantaneous reactive motion of the order parameters is thus determined by the conjugate fields acting through their operator commutators.
Using the closed algebra in Eq. (2) and the relation fiα=−∂F/∂ϕiαf_{i}^{\alpha}=-\partial F/\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha} established in Eq. (10), we obtain
(d​ϕiαd​t)R=∑β,γCα​β​γ​ϕiγ​∂F∂ϕiβ.\left(\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}{dt}\right)_{\!R}=\sum_{\beta,\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\phi_{i}^{\gamma}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{i}^{\beta}}.
Comparison with Eq. (3) reproduces the reversible tensor in Eq. (34), showing that its Poisson-bracket-like structure follows directly from the microscopic commutation algebra.
The nondissipative character of this motion is explicit in the corresponding rate of change of the constrained functional:
(d​Fd​t)R\displaystyle\left(\frac{dF}{dt}\right)_{\!R}
=\displaystyle=
∑i,α∂F∂ϕiα​(d​ϕiαd​t)R\displaystyle\sum_{i,\alpha}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}\left(\frac{d\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}{dt}\right)_{\!R}
(80)
=\displaystyle=
∑i,α,β,γCα​β​γ​ϕiγ​∂F∂ϕiα​∂F∂ϕiβ=0.\displaystyle\sum_{i,\alpha,\beta,\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\phi_{i}^{\gamma}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{i}^{\alpha}}\frac{\partial F}{\partial\phi_{i}^{\beta}}=0.
The final equality follows from the antisymmetry of Cα​β​γC_{\alpha\beta\gamma} under α↔β\alpha\leftrightarrow\beta, whereas the product of the two thermodynamic forces is symmetric. The reversible flow therefore follows surfaces of constant FF.
For spin order, this result has an especially simple interpretation. The SU(2) commutation algebra converts the thermodynamic field conjugate to the local magnetization into transverse precessional motion, giving the Landau–Lifshitz structure encountered in Sec. VI. The projection-theory formulation extends this interpretation to mixed relevant states and incorporates the memory and fluctuating contributions generated by the complementary microscopic sector.

This calculation implements the inner loop of the MF-AVP workflow illustrated in Fig. 3. The constrained HF Hamiltonian in Eq. (48) contains both the Hartree contribution to the local potential and the Fock correction to the nearest-neighbor hopping. The density constraints must be satisfied together with HF self-consistency. In particular, the nearest-neighbor off-diagonal elements Gi​jG_{ij} remain variational and enter the Hamiltonian through the Fock term. The iteration is therefore continued until both the residuals Gi​i−niG_{ii}-n_{i} and the changes in the unconstrained mean fields are sufficiently small. They enter the density dynamics in Eq. (49) directly, without separate evaluation or numerical differentiation of FF. Advancing 𝒏\bm{n} by one time step defines the next constrained electronic problem.

Appendix D Constrained Hartree–Fock and Generalized Landau–Lifshitz Dynamics for the SDW
This appendix provides details of the constrained electronic calculation and the stochastic magnetic dynamics used in Sec. VI. We first describe the spin-dependent Hartree–Fock iteration, distinguishing the prescribed magnetic texture from the moments calculated during the electronic minimization. We then establish the relation between the vector and amplitude–orientation descriptions and specify the associated noise correlations and numerical integration methods.
D.1 Constrained Hartree–Fock calculation
The electronic state is characterized by the single-particle density matrix Gi​jα​β=⟨c^j,β†​c^i,α⟩G_{ij}^{\alpha\beta}=\langle\hat{c}_{j,\beta}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{i,\alpha}^{\,}\rangle. To distinguish the prescribed magnetic moments 𝐦i\mathbf{m}_{i} from those obtained during the constrained iteration, we denote the latter by 𝐬i​[G]=(1/2)​trs⁡(𝝈​Gi​i)\mathbf{s}_{i}[G]=(1/2)\operatorname{tr}_{s}(\bm{\sigma}G_{ii}), where trs\operatorname{tr}_{s} is the trace over spin indices. The local density matrix therefore has the decomposition
Gi​iα​β=ni​[G]2​δα​β+𝐬i​[G]⋅𝝈α​β,G_{ii}^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{n_{i}[G]}{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\mathbf{s}_{i}[G]\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta},
(87)
with ni​[G]=trs⁡Gi​in_{i}[G]=\operatorname{tr}_{s}G_{ii}. The constrained calculation requires 𝐬i​[G]=𝐦i\mathbf{s}_{i}[G]=\mathbf{m}_{i} upon convergence, while the charge densities remain variational.
As in the CDW implementation of Appendix C, it is convenient to iterate the combined local field entering the single-particle Hamiltonian. For the Hubbard model, the exchange and constraint contributions appear through
𝐡i=𝐛i−2​U​𝐬i​[G].\mathbf{h}_{i}=\mathbf{b}_{i}-2U\mathbf{s}_{i}[G].
(88)
The effective field 𝐡i\mathbf{h}_{i} determines the spin-dependent electronic potential, whereas 𝐛i\mathbf{b}_{i} is the thermodynamic field conjugate to the prescribed moment. Writing the quadratic Hamiltonian as ℋ^HF=∑i​j,α​βHi​α,j​β​c^i,α†​c^j,β\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\rm HF}=\sum_{ij,\alpha\beta}H_{i\alpha,j\beta}\hat{c}_{i,\alpha}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{j,\beta}^{\,}, its single-particle matrix becomes
Hi​α,j​β=−tnn​δ⟨i​j⟩​δα​β+δi​j​[U​ni2​δα​β+12​𝐡i⋅𝝈α​β],H_{i\alpha,j\beta}=-t_{\rm nn}\,\delta_{\langle ij\rangle}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\delta_{ij}\left[\frac{Un_{i}}{2}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{h}_{i}\cdot\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha\beta}\right],
(89)
where δ⟨i​j⟩=1\delta_{\langle ij\rangle}=1 for nearest neighbors and vanishes otherwise. The factor of 1/21/2 in the spin-dependent term follows from the definition of the electronic spin operator in Eq. (54).
For a prescribed magnetic texture {𝐦i}\{\mathbf{m}_{i}\}, the effective fields 𝐡i\mathbf{h}_{i} and unconstrained charge densities nin_{i} are determined iteratively. At iteration kk, their current values define H(k)H^{(k)}, whose eigenstates satisfy
∑j,βHi​α,j​β(k)​ψν,j​β(k)=ϵν(k)​ψν,i​α(k).\sum_{j,\beta}H_{i\alpha,j\beta}^{(k)}\psi_{\nu,j\beta}^{(k)}=\epsilon_{\nu}^{(k)}\psi_{\nu,i\alpha}^{(k)}.
(90)
The updated density matrix is then
Gi​j(k+1),α​β=∑νnF(ϵν(k)−μ)ψν,i​α(k)ψν,j​β(k)∗,G_{ij}^{(k+1),\alpha\beta}=\sum_{\nu}n_{\rm F}\!\left(\epsilon_{\nu}^{(k)}-\mu\right)\psi_{\nu,i\alpha}^{(k)}\psi_{\nu,j\beta}^{(k)*},
(91)
where ν\nu labels the single-particle eigenstates, nF​(x)=(eβ​x+1)−1n_{\rm F}(x)=(e^{\beta x}+1)^{-1}, and the chemical potential is adjusted to maintain half filling. The local blocks of G(k+1)G^{(k+1)} determine the updated charge densities and calculated spin moments.
The discrepancy between the calculated and prescribed moments is fed back into the effective fields through
hia,(k+1)=hia,(k)+κh(sia[G(k+1)]−mia),a=x,y,z,h_{i}^{a,(k+1)}=h_{i}^{a,(k)}+\kappa_{h}\left(s_{i}^{a}[G^{(k+1)}]-m_{i}^{a}\right),\qquad a=x,y,z,
(92)
with κh>0\kappa_{h}>0. Because the spin-dependent term is +𝐡i⋅𝐬^i+\mathbf{h}_{i}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{i}, increasing hiah_{i}^{a} tends to suppress the corresponding spin expectation value, giving the required negative feedback. The three field components are coupled through the electronic response and must be converged together. During the same iteration, the charge densities entering the Hartree potential are updated from ni​[G]n_{i}[G]. Mixing successive charge densities or effective fields can be used to improve convergence.
The iteration continues until the charge densities are self-consistent and the prescribed magnetic moments are reproduced. Denoting the converged quantities by 𝐡¯i\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i} and G¯\bar{G}, the thermodynamic fields returned by the constrained calculation are
𝐛i=𝐡¯i+2​U​𝐬i​[G¯]=𝐡¯i+2​U​𝐦i.\mathbf{b}_{i}=\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}+2U\mathbf{s}_{i}[\bar{G}]=\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{i}+2U\mathbf{m}_{i}.
(93)
The second equality follows from the converged spin constraints. Thus, the effective fields used to solve the electronic problem are converted into the conjugate fields 𝐛i=−∂F/∂𝐦i\mathbf{b}_{i}=-\partial F/\partial\mathbf{m}_{i}, which drive both the precessional and dissipative magnetic dynamics.
D.2 Relation between vector and amplitude–orientation dynamics
The decomposition 𝐦i=Mi​𝐞^i\mathbf{m}_{i}=M_{i}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i} introduced in Eq. (59) separates changes in moment magnitude from rotations of its direction. For Mi>0M_{i}>0, a variation satisfies δ​𝐦i=𝐞^i​δ​Mi+Mi​δ​𝐞^i\delta\mathbf{m}_{i}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\,\delta M_{i}+M_{i}\,\delta\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}, with 𝐞^i⋅δ​𝐞^i=0\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\delta\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}=0. Consequently, the force conjugate to the amplitude is
∂F∂Mi=𝐞^i⋅∂F∂𝐦i=−𝐞^i⋅𝐛i,\frac{\partial F}{\partial M_{i}}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{m}_{i}}=-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{i},
(94)
where the derivative is taken at fixed orientations and other amplitudes. The corresponding tangential gradient on the unit sphere is
∇S2,iF=Mi​(∂F∂𝐦i)⟂=−Mi​𝐛i,⟂.\nabla_{S^{2},i}F=M_{i}\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{m}_{i}}\right)_{\perp}=-M_{i}\mathbf{b}_{i,\perp}.
(95)
Both driving forces therefore follow from the same conjugate field 𝐛i\mathbf{b}_{i} supplied by the constrained electronic calculation.
The deterministic amplitude and orientation equations follow by projecting the vector dynamics along and perpendicular to the instantaneous moment:
d​Mid​t=𝐞^i⋅d​𝐦id​t,d​𝐞^id​t=1Mi​Pi,⟂​d​𝐦id​t,\frac{dM_{i}}{dt}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\frac{d\mathbf{m}_{i}}{dt},\qquad\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{dt}=\frac{1}{M_{i}}P_{i,\perp}\frac{d\mathbf{m}_{i}}{dt},
(96)
where Pi,∥a​b=eiaeibP_{i,\parallel}^{ab}=e_{i}^{a}e_{i}^{b} and Pi,⟂a​b=δa​b−eia​eibP_{i,\perp}^{ab}=\delta_{ab}-e_{i}^{a}e_{i}^{b}. The longitudinal projection eliminates both precession and transverse damping, yielding the Model-A relaxation term Γ∥𝐞^i⋅𝐛i=−Γ∥∂F/∂Mi\Gamma_{\parallel}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{i}=-\Gamma_{\parallel}\partial F/\partial M_{i}. The transverse projection gives precession together with Γ⟂​𝐛i,⟂/Mi\Gamma_{\perp}\mathbf{b}_{i,\perp}/M_{i}. Using 𝐛i,⟂=−𝐞^i×(𝐞^i×𝐛i)\mathbf{b}_{i,\perp}=-\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times\mathbf{b}_{i}) recovers the Landau–Lifshitz damping term in Eq. (63). The factor 1/Mi1/M_{i} converts a transverse change of the magnetic moment into a change of its orientation.
With the convention 𝐛i=−∂F/∂𝐦i\mathbf{b}_{i}=-\partial F/\partial\mathbf{m}_{i} and ℏ=1\hbar=1, the reversible orientational term is +𝐞^i×𝐛i+\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times\mathbf{b}_{i}, consistently with the microscopic spin commutation algebra. Precession and transverse damping preserve the moment magnitude, while longitudinal relaxation changes it. Although these contributions separate under projection, their evolution remains coupled through the dependence of 𝐛i\mathbf{b}_{i} on the full magnetic texture.
D.3 Thermal fluctuations and numerical integration
We implement thermal fluctuations directly in the amplitude and orientation equations, using independent longitudinal and transverse noise sources. The amplitude obeys
d​Mid​t=Γ∥​𝐞^i⋅𝐛i+ξi​(t),\frac{dM_{i}}{dt}=\Gamma_{\parallel}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{b}_{i}+\xi_{i}(t),
(97)
where ξi​(t)\xi_{i}(t) is a zero-mean Gaussian white noise with
⟨ξi​(t)​ξj​(t′)⟩=2​T​Γ∥​δi​j​δ​(t−t′).\left\langle\xi_{i}(t)\xi_{j}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle=2T\Gamma_{\parallel}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).
(98)
Thus, the constrained electronic field determines the longitudinal relaxation, while the noise strength is set by the temperature and the same kinetic coefficient.
The orientational dynamics is
d​𝐞^id​t=𝐞^i×𝐛i−Γ⟂Mi​𝐞^i×(𝐞^i×𝐛i)+𝜻i​(t).\frac{d\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}}{dt}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times\mathbf{b}_{i}-\frac{\Gamma_{\perp}}{M_{i}}\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times\left(\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\times\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)+\bm{\zeta}_{i}(t).
(99)
The noise is tangent to the unit sphere and is interpreted in the Stratonovich sense, as appropriate for the stochastic Landau–Lifshitz formulation [141, 142]. An explicit representation is 𝜻i​d​t=2​T​Γ⟂​Pi,⟂∘d​𝐖i/Mi\bm{\zeta}_{i}\,dt=\sqrt{2T\Gamma_{\perp}}\,P_{i,\perp}\circ d\mathbf{W}_{i}/M_{i}, where 𝐖i\mathbf{W}_{i} are independent three-component Wiener processes and ∘\circ denotes a Stratonovich differential. At a given instantaneous configuration, the corresponding covariance is
⟨ζia​(t)​ζjb​(t′)⟩𝐦=2​T​Γ⟂Mi2​Pi,⟂a​b​δi​j​δ​(t−t′).\left\langle\zeta_{i}^{a}(t)\zeta_{j}^{b}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\mathbf{m}}=\frac{2T\Gamma_{\perp}}{M_{i}^{2}}P_{i,\perp}^{ab}\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).
(100)
The subscript 𝐦\mathbf{m} indicates that the configuration-dependent noise amplitude is evaluated at the instantaneous magnetic configuration. The tangential projection and Stratonovich interpretation preserve |𝐞^i|=1|\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}|=1, and the transverse driving Wiener processes are independent of those generating ξi\xi_{i}.
At the level of the local noise covariance, the longitudinal and transverse contributions combine as 𝜼i=𝐞^i​ξi+Mi​𝜻i\bm{\eta}_{i}=\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i}\xi_{i}+M_{i}\bm{\zeta}_{i}. Their covariance therefore has the fluctuation–dissipation form
⟨ηia(t)ηjb(t′)⟩𝐦=2T(Γ∥Pi,∥a​b+Γ⟂Pi,⟂a​b)δi​jδ(t−t′).\left\langle\eta_{i}^{a}(t)\eta_{j}^{b}(t^{\prime})\right\rangle_{\mathbf{m}}=2T\left(\Gamma_{\parallel}P_{i,\parallel}^{ab}+\Gamma_{\perp}P_{i,\perp}^{ab}\right)\delta_{ij}\delta(t-t^{\prime}).
(101)
The factors of MiM_{i} in the orientational equation and its noise covariance ensure that Γ⟂\Gamma_{\perp} retains its meaning as the transverse mobility of the magnetic moment. The stochastic evolution used here is specified by the amplitude–orientation equations above.
The amplitude equation is integrated using a Heun predictor–corrector scheme, while the orientational equation is advanced using the semi-implicit B (SIB) method of Mentink et al. [142]. SIB preserves the length of each orientation vector by construction. The same noise realization is used in the predictor and corrector stages of a given stochastic step. Both methods have second-order accuracy in the deterministic limit; this should be distinguished from their convergence order for stochastic trajectories or averaged observables [142].
In the simulations presented here, the moment amplitudes remain sharply distributed around their equilibrium magnitude once local moments have developed. We use a small longitudinal relaxation coefficient, with the corresponding noise strength fixed by Eq. (98). The physical upper bound Mi≤1/2M_{i}\leq 1/2 is enforced numerically by setting Mi=1/2M_{i}=1/2 whenever a proposed amplitude update exceeds this value. This clipping serves as a safeguard against excursions outside the allowed range; the trajectories studied here remain below the upper boundary.

The nearest-neighbor repulsion responsible for CDW ordering in Sec. V also defines a useful classical reference problem. Suppressing the electronic hopping leaves the lattice-gas Hamiltonian
ℋcl=V∑⟨i​j⟩ninj,ni=0,1.\mathcal{H}_{\rm cl}=V\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}n_{i}n_{j},\qquad n_{i}=0,1.
(102)
On the square lattice at half filling and the triangular lattice at one-third filling, this interaction favors the same checkerboard and three-sublattice ordering patterns discussed in Secs. V.1 and V.2, respectively. The classical simulations use particle-conserving local hopping updates, whereas the MF-AVP calculations employ density dynamics at fixed chemical potential. The comparison therefore examines ordering morphology and growth laws under different microscopic kinetics.
The connection to classical spin models follows by introducing Ising variables σi=2​ni−1=±1\sigma_{i}=2n_{i}-1=\pm 1. At fixed particle number, Eq. (102) is equivalent, up to a configuration-independent constant, to the antiferromagnetic Ising Hamiltonian
ℋIsing=V4​∑⟨i​j⟩σi​σj.\mathcal{H}_{\rm Ising}=\frac{V}{4}\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}.
(103)
The particle-number constraint becomes a fixed-magnetization condition: ∑iσi=0\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=0 for the half-filled square lattice and N−1∑iσi=−1/3N^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=-1/3 for the one-third-filled triangular lattice, where NN is the number of sites. The latter constraint is essential to the comparison. At this filling, the classical ground states are the three 3×3\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3} configurations, rather than the extensively degenerate ground-state manifold of the unrestricted triangular-lattice antiferromagnetic Ising model.
We simulate the classical model using nearest-neighbor Kawasaki exchange dynamics with heat-bath acceptance. A proposed particle hop exchanges the occupations of a neighboring particle–hole pair, 10↔0110\leftrightarrow 01, and is accepted with probability
Pacc​(Δ​E)=11+exp⁡(β​Δ​E),P_{\rm acc}(\Delta E)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(\beta\Delta E)},
(104)
where Δ​E\Delta E is the change in the classical interaction energy and β=1/T\beta=1/T, with kB=1k_{\rm B}=1. The square and triangular lattices contain 64×6464\times 64 and 63×6363\times 63 sites, respectively, and both simulations use V=1V=1 and T=0.1T=0.1. One unit of simulation time corresponds to one sweep, during which every electron is considered once for a hopping update. These updates conserve total particle number but allow occupation to be transferred between the ordering sublattices, so neither the checkerboard nor the three-sublattice CDW order parameter is conserved.
For both lattices, we characterize domain growth using the corresponding order-parameter correlations defined in the main text. The equal-time correlation function is normalized such that C⁡(0,t)=1C(0,t)=1, and the characteristic domain size is extracted from C⁡(L⁡(t),t)=1/2C(L(t),t)=1/2. Plotting the correlations against r/L⁡(t)r/L(t) then tests whether the evolving texture is described approximately by a single growing length scale.
E.1 Square lattice at half filling
At half filling on the square lattice, the interaction energy is minimized by occupying one sublattice and leaving the other empty. The two resulting checkerboard states correspond to the symmetry-related Néel states of the Ising representation. Although the exchange dynamics conserves the total density, each particle hop changes the occupation of both sublattices and can therefore relax the checkerboard order. This permits curvature-driven coarsening of the nonconserved Z2Z_{2} order parameter despite conservation of the underlying particle number.
Figure 10 shows representative configurations of the local checkerboard order parameter ϕi\phi_{i}, obtained from the occupation variables using Eq. (46). The sublattice factor (−1)xi+yi(-1)^{x_{i}+y_{i}} included in this definition makes the two ordered states distinguishable by the sign of ϕi\phi_{i}. The initially disordered state develops checkerboard domains that subsequently grow and merge. Their overall morphology resembles the MF-AVP configurations in Fig. 4, although the classical interfaces are microscopically rougher and isolated local defects persist within otherwise ordered regions over the displayed time interval. These defects are local occupation excitations whose removal requires particle rearrangements through the allowed nearest-neighbor exchanges. The differences in interface structure and defect relaxation reflect the distinct microscopic descriptions and kinetic rules of the two calculations.
The normalized correlation functions of ϕi\phi_{i} in Fig. 11 exhibit an approximate collapse when plotted against r/L⁡(t)r/L(t). The characteristic length shown in the inset grows as L⁡(t)∼t1/2L(t)\sim t^{1/2} over the observed coarsening regime, consistent with Allen–Cahn behavior. Thus, the classical exchange dynamics and the microscopic MF-AVP evolution recover the same growth law despite their different update rules and interface structures. This comparison supports the interpretation of the square-lattice MF-AVP results as conventional coarsening of the checkerboard order generated by the repulsive interaction.

The normalized equal-time correlation functions, C⁡(0,t)=1C(0,t)=1, are plotted against r/L⁡(t)r/L(t), with L⁡(t)L(t) defined by C⁡(L⁡(t),t)=1/2C(L(t),t)=1/2. E.2 Triangular lattice at one-third filling
At one-third filling on the triangular lattice, occupying any one of the three ordering sublattices eliminates all occupied nearest-neighbor pairs. The three possible choices give the symmetry-related 3×3\sqrt{3}\times\sqrt{3} ground states and the associated Z3Z_{3} order. As on the square lattice, particle-conserving hopping updates can change the sublattice populations and therefore relax the CDW order. The additional ordered state allows three distinct domains to meet, producing the junction-rich textures characteristic of this problem.
Representative configurations are shown in Fig. 12. Local charge order develops from the initially disordered state, followed by the growth of domains separated by an extended network of walls and Y-shaped junctions. The local two-component field is reconstructed from the occupation variables using the same triangle-based definition as in Sec. V.2. The resulting domain network closely resembles that of the electronic MF-AVP calculation, while the classical interfaces remain rougher and residual occupation defects persist within some ordered regions. These snapshots show that the repulsive lattice-gas interaction alone produces the principal geometrical structures of the three-state CDW texture.
Figure 13 quantifies this evolution through the normalized correlation functions. Their approximate collapse against r/L⁡(t)r/L(t) indicates that a single characteristic length describes the domain structure over the accessible coarsening regime. The inset gives an effective growth law L⁡(t)∼t0.436L(t)\sim t^{0.436}, slower than the square-lattice result. This behavior parallels the reduced effective exponent α≃0.415\alpha\simeq 0.415 found in the electronic triangular-lattice calculation, showing that slower intermediate-time growth can also occur in the absence of electronic hopping.
The similar trends suggest that the three-state domain geometry and the relaxation of its junction network are relevant to both calculations, although they do not establish a common microscopic slowing mechanism. Neither fitted exponent should be identified with a distinct asymptotic growth law on the basis of the present simulations. Junction rearrangements and long-lived configurations can contribute to an extended crossover regime, while finite system size limits the late-time analysis. The classical benchmark therefore provides a reference for interpreting the electronic results while leaving open whether their slower growth eventually crosses over to conventional Allen–Cahn behavior.
