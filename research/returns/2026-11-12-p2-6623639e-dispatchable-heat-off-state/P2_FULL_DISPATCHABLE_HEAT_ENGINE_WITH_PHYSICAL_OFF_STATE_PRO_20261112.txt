Invent a low-cost heat-to-electricity converter with a real low-loss OFF state.

Can an attainable physical architecture provide efficient, economical electricity on demand from stored heat, including the periods when electricity is NOT wanted? The complete invention is the hot-to-useful-electricity operation together with its physical disconnection and restart. A better cell, repaired wafer, regenerator figure of merit or a cold shutter alone is not that capability.

The full ON target remains a converter delivering 100 W regulated net DC continuously for 100 hours, rejecting heat to ambient air at 35 degrees Celsius, with every hot emitting/contact surface at most 1400 degrees Celsius. Choose and justify the hot operating temperature, output voltage and a useful load envelope including 50--100 W. Net heat-to-electric efficiency at rated output must be at least 35 percent after all converter auxiliaries and losses. The production-cost target remains at most US$500 per kW of rated NET output, initially evaluate 10 MW/year manufacture. These are unachieved invention targets, not properties supplied by the question.

Add an actual OFF mode while the thermal reservoir remains hot. Target at most 1 W thermal withdrawal through the complete converter and its introduced closure/interfaces per 100 W rated capacity, and at most 0.1 W electrical standby demand. Construct the mechanism that produces the contrast between ON and OFF. A disconnected electrical load, a parked engine or a reflective cold shutter is not automatically a thermal disconnect. No closure, actuator, support or aperture can disappear from the energy and cost boundaries when it changes state. The source may be a specified practical hot reservoir; the converter need not be a universal clamp-on device for every material and geometry.

Define Q_hot as total net reservoir heat removed by the ENTIRE converter and all interfaces/enclosures introduced for it. Define P_net as useful regulated output after fans, pumps, controls, vacuum maintenance, rectification and all other electrical auxiliaries irrespective of how they are powered. Include source temperature nonuniformity and the actual cold-side conditions. Meter startup, shutdown, OFF operation, actuation and restart, and keep stored thermal inventories consistent. If an existing heat store has separate baseline losses, identify them explicitly and close the COMPLETE plant balance as well; do not relabel introduced converter losses as storage losses.

Use a 10-hour delivery / 14-hour OFF daily duty and a 100-hour delivery / 144-hour OFF long-duration duty to assess the complete useful operation. Specify charging time and an attainable store/interface for each, or explain why one hardware design cannot serve both. Include electricity-to-heat efficiency, actual temperature evolution or phase change, storage losses, thermal transfer, startup/restart, auxiliaries and inverter. The OFF-state target must actually improve the resulting delivered-electricity economics; a mode that saves heat but consumes expensive drive work or destroys cycling life may not help.

All methods are open. You may change the conversion principle, geometry, active material, manufacturing route, thermal coupling, module organization or representation. You may choose TPV, Stirling or another heat engine, or a new mechanism. Do not preserve the last wafer architecture simply because it has a model. A different material or mechanism must have attainable preparation and explicit untested properties. No ideal emitter/mirror, free cold-water supply, supplied mechanical precision, uncharged high-temperature valve, free vacuum or nominally zero-leak switch is gifted.

Actual prior design work gives useful constraints. A coupled electrical/optical repair architecture bypasses defective TPV sites AND covers them with reflective caps. Under its disclosed surrogate optics and manufacturing assumptions, a 100 W candidate at 1375 C draws about 321.68 W hot, gives about 31.09 percent net efficiency and costs about US$246 per unit rather than the target US$50. Optical repair saves roughly 16 W compared with electrical bypass alone, but does not beat defect-free devices or a known-good-die comparator. The model's cold-shutter standby draws about 77 W thermal plus 3.7 W electricity. None of this is a measured device or an all-architecture lower bound. The underlying geometry, energy accounting, matched wafer allocation, transport calculations and failure modes remain useful, with their actual limitations.

Some interfaces that cannot be bypassed by bookkeeping:
- At 100 W net, 35 percent requires Q_hot<=285.714 W. The present 321.68 W design needs about 35.97 W less hot draw at unchanged net output. A 2 W numerical discrepancy cannot close that gap.
- In the current cost scenario, even free cells leave roughly US$71 per complete unit. Cell-area price reduction alone therefore cannot meet US$50; packaging, containment, cooling, manufacture or the architecture must change as well.
- For a cold surface viewing the source, absorption is approximately A times the actual spectral/angular integral of absorptivity and incident radiance. At 1375 C, 96.1 cm2 sees a roughly 4 kW blackbody scale at unit view factor. Reducing direct cold absorption below 1 W would require an effective absorptivity-times-view-factor around 2.5e-4 in that simple geometry, before other losses. This is a diagnostic, not a requirement to achieve an ideal mirror. Moving a closure to a hot boundary can change the physics, but its back-face radiation, insulation, conduction, seals, actuation and finite temperature gradients must then be paid.
- Storage efficiency requires both ON and OFF energy. Preserving the last candidate's standby can defeat even an assumed improved converter. No freely supplied isothermal store, cold mirror or instantaneous thermal valve is allowed.

Strong existing heat-engine alternatives matter. NASA/Sunpower reports an 88 W AC free-piston Stirling converter at 38-percent heat-to-AC efficiency with 850 C hot and 90 C rejection conditions. That is actual relevant prior performance, not a US$50 manufacturable device or a net air-cooled module with the present OFF mode. Its gas bearings, alternator, heater-head material, regenerator, centering and small clearances were real engineered subsystems. A cheaper replacement has to preserve the whole conversion operation. Similarly, a microfabricated regenerator's better heat-transfer/pressure-drop metric did not automatically improve the tested complete engine because axial conduction consumed the benefit.

Construct a complete design with materials/components, dimensions, fabrication and assembly, ON/OFF/restart sequence, active and idle thermal paths, electrical/control architecture and numerical operating points. Use actual temperature-dependent optical, thermal, electrical and mechanical data or clearly bounded extrapolations. Include finite heat transfer, pressure drop, leakage, radiation, resistance, contact and conversion losses, creep/expansion, contamination, material compatibility, tolerances and cycling degradation. Show an attainable parameter margin rather than resize an ideal component whenever a sensitivity case fails.

Use an executable coupled model for the energy/material balances and the decisive physical subproblem, with a consistent thermal inventory through the whole cycle. Simulations with assigned perfect boundaries remain conditional. If a useful physical mechanism has been constructed only as a component design, state the operation it still lacks instead of awarding it the full converter performance.

The US$500/NETkW cost includes active elements or engine, converter heat exchangers, hot enclosure and interface, real disconnect, cooling, atmosphere/vacuum, supports, alternator/electronics, actuation, assembly, yield, inspection and required replacement. Pay exact wafer/panel or precision-part utilization, not fractional stock without a usable remainder. Give first-prototype and production costs separately. State capital utilization, material/service life, maintenance, equipment throughput, waste and waste-processing assumptions. A lifetime or factory rate is not proved by a 100-hour model.

Compare the same useful ON/OFF operation against the strongest relevant existing converters and thermal-storage architectures. Credit their actual capability and distinguish demonstrated numbers from targets. For the two storage duties, calculate all-input electrical round-trip efficiency and full delivered-energy cost at charging electricity US$20 and US$40 per MWh, with storage, charger, inverter, installation, replacements, annual cycles and financing paid. A design that wins only on one isolated component is not the invention. If an incumbent already meets the full operation, find a consequential new advantage rather than replicate it.

Develop a concrete first prototype and decisive experiment for later physical execution. It should test the proposed central new operation, including actual OFF-state heat leakage and restart, complete ON efficiency and the manufacturing bottleneck. Specify attainable build/access/components, controls, calibrated heater/electrical/calorimetric measurements, uncertainties, numerical pass/fail criteria and total cost. Distinguish proposed tests from actual performance; do not execute a physical experiment here or present an unbuilt prototype as a demonstration.
