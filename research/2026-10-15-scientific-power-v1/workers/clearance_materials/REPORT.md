# Materials and fabrication feasibility: temporary routing in a closed network

**Status:** bounded screen of primary papers and patent documents; no device was fabricated or tested in this investigation. All numerical pass criteria below are proposed screening gates, not reported performance.

## Decision

Do not build the original 500 × 500 µm Wheatstone device with 25 µm calcium-alginate longitudinal septa first. The literature supports neither that print scale for this alginate route nor its pressure survival; low-ion cold flushing may also weaken the calcium crosslinks before the intended EDTA step.

The updated millimetre-scale lollipop geometry is a substantially better first candidate: an 80 mm, 1 × 1 mm loop connected to a 3 × 3 × 1 mm root chamber, with one 0.5 mm-thick Ca-alginate diagonal gate temporarily pairing inlet-to-loop-start and loop-return-to-outlet. Primary work directly demonstrates room-temperature epoxy curing around F127 and 4 °C removal at 800 µm channel scale. Separate alginate work demonstrates approximately 100 µm lateral patterning and 350 µm-high embedded sacrificial channels. Those are useful scale precedents, but no retrieved primary source demonstrates the exact wet-alginate/epoxy root seal, its survival through cold F127 clearance, or the two-port sequence. The lollipop is therefore **plausible enough for a two-port coupon experiment, not established as manufacturable or functional**.

Keep the Al-foil/NaOH proposal as a contingency only. Aluminum is stiff and sacrificial-metal templates have been used inside PDMS and epoxy, but the closest process dissolves Al slowly at room temperature, uses 6 M NaOH and 60–80 °C, warns against stopping flow during dissolution, and requires a more involved rinse. Its theoretical hydrogen production for the earlier thin-wall geometry is very large relative to the channel volume.

## Current design and physical interpretation

The current topology is a side-loop (“lollipop”) whose final graph is attached to the A–D mainline at one articulation vertex. Once the root partition is erased, steady pressure-driven flow between A and D has no source–sink path through the side loop: both loop ends return to the same pressure node. During Stage 1, the temporary gate must instead seal the 3 × 3 × 1 mm root chamber so flow is forced from the inlet into one loop arm, around the loop, and back to the outlet. This design removes the long internal septa that made EDTA access and leakage accumulation especially difficult.

The 1 × 1 mm, 80 mm loop contains about 80 µL; the 3 × 3 × 1 mm root chamber adds about 9 µL before the mainline and port features. These are geometric estimates, not measured fill volumes. The Stage-1 fluid must reach and clear the distal loop; a dye at the outlet alone cannot show that F127 was removed from every part of the opaque final device.

## Material screen

### F127 core and epoxy host: strongest positive evidence

Zhang et al. report 40% (w/v) Pluronic F127 embedded in a fumed-silica/epoxy support, with epoxy initially crosslinking for 12 h and further curing for 60 h at room temperature. Their printed channels were 800 µm in diameter; after the composite was cooled to 4 °C for 1 h, liquefied F127 was removed, and dyed water was injected to demonstrate continuity. This directly supports the combination of room-temperature epoxy processing, F127 and cold removal at a scale close to the proposed 1 mm loop. It does not establish the exact epoxy chemistry/acoustic formulation, an opaque 80 mm loop, or compatibility with a wet alginate gate. The proposed process must use its exact host formulation and curing schedule in the coupon; “epoxy” alone is not a transferable material specification. [Zhang et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym16233320)

F127 is salt-sensitive. Jiang et al. measured a decrease in F127 sol–gel transition temperature as NaCl or CaCl2 was added. A calcium-balanced carrier may be needed to preserve the alginate gate during the cold wash, but it changes F127 rheology and transition behavior. The usable Stage-1 recipe is therefore a coupled choice: measure F127 removal and gate retention with the same low-temperature carrier, rather than selecting the F127 wash and alginate storage conditions independently. [Jiang et al., 2008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.polymer.2008.05.038)

### Calcium-alginate root gate: dimension plausible, interface unproven

Valentin et al. report approximately 100 µm lateral spatial resolution for their photopatterned alginate method; measured minimum features depended strongly on formulation, from 231 µm for one Ba/Mg blend to 434 µm for 2X Ca and 1.04 mm for 4X Ca. Thus the proposed 0.5 mm gate is only just above the demonstrated minimum for the tested 2X Ca formulation; their 4X Ca formulation, which might be considered for a stronger gate, did not print below about 1.04 mm. They also formed a 350 µm-high, 37 mm-long serpentine sacrificial template, embedded it in agarose, and removed it using 100 mM EDTA overnight. Their gels were soft: representative shear moduli were around 1.5–2.7 kPa, and the paper describes the material as relatively soft. A 0.5 mm gate in a 3 mm chamber is thus a plausible pattern size, but these results do not show a pressure-tight 0.5 mm Ca-only partition in cured epoxy. [Valentin et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1039/C7LC00694B)

A key conflict is that Stage 1 is proposed to use cold water to liquefy and remove F127, while Valentin et al. explicitly kept their deionized-water rinse brief to prevent premature alginate dissolution from loss of crosslinking cations. A calcium-alginate gate may therefore erode or soften during Stage 1 unless the carrier preserves its crosslinks. Adding calcium to the carrier can shift F127 behavior. Measure gate leakage and mechanical stability through the *full* cold wash; do not infer orthogonality simply because EDTA is the intended Stage-2 trigger.

The resin–hydrogel seal is another independent gate. The cited alginate channel work encapsulated templates in agarose, not cured epoxy. No retrieved primary source establishes that a hydrated alginate partition can bond or remain gap-free where it contacts this epoxy formulation after cure, swelling and two-sided pressure loading. A printed or molded gate may look closed but leak around its perimeter. A transparent witness helps visualize that seam but does not substitute for the exact acoustic host.

EDTA chelates the divalent crosslinks; it does not make the alginate polymer disappear by itself. The released alginate, calcium–EDTA and excess EDTA must be exported through A/D. Saez et al. demonstrate in-situ calcium-alginate microvalves erased with EDTA in a microfluidic device, a close precedent for the local gate chemistry. Their device uses a T-shaped main/cross-channel arrangement and controlled reagent feeds, not this no-extra-port root seal or an 80 mm return loop. [Saez et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.snb.2016.04.140)

### Why the old 25 µm alginate septum is a no-go for the first build

The thin-wall design requires a continuous 25 µm alginate septum over a 500 µm channel span and tens of millimetres of path, plus sealed junction partitions. The relevant alginate literature reports minimum features several hundred micrometres for the tested formulations and low-kPa shear moduli. As a screening model only, treating a 25 µm wall spanning 500 µm as an ideally clamped elastic strip gives maximum deflection approximately ΔP·h^4/(32·E·t^3). With E estimated as 3G under the paper’s short-time, incompressible-gel assumption and G = 1.5–2.7 kPa from the closest calcium-containing formulations, a 100 Pa cross-wall pressure would predict millimetre-scale deflection, beyond the model’s small-deflection range and much larger than the available lane. The useful inference is not the extrapolated millimetre value; it is that even a few-pascal allowable differential would need direct confirmation. Full allocated branch pressure can act across remote lanes, so pressure testing must measure pressure across the wall and use the actual support condition.

A 0.5 mm local gate is materially different: it is twenty times thicker, shorter and localized in a much larger chamber. It may avoid the distributed deflection and defect risks, but the full pressure differential still acts across the gate and its perimeter seal. Its pressure rating must be measured in the exact wet, cured assembly.

### Al foil and NaOH: stiff but poor-fit removal chemistry

Yamashita et al. embedded aluminum tubes and balls in PDMS and epoxy to create channels after template removal. Their Al templates were dissolved using aqueous NaOH. The paper reports that room-temperature dissolution is very slow, heats the process to 60–80 °C to accelerate it, and warns that stopping the NaOH injection can lead to apparent precipitate clogging. Its Al-tube/ball templates and post-rinsing sequence are not a demonstrated 25 µm foil membrane sealed to four channel walls, and the reported accelerated process misses the below-40 °C target. [Yamashita et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.2116/analsci.31.1261)

The foil has a passive surface oxide whose condition and alloy/temper affect neutral-water corrosion; a neutral cold wash cannot be assumed to preserve an unspecified foil indefinitely. Drazic and Popic found that hydrogen evolution on Al in neutral-pH chloride solutions depended on the spontaneously formed surface film. That study used 0.5 M NaCl, so it is not a corrosion-rate prediction for the proposed chloride-free rinse; it reinforces that the actual foil surface and carrier chemistry require measurement. [Drazic and Popic, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-0728(93)80377-T)

For the earlier Wheatstone geometry, two 20 mm × 0.5 mm × 25 µm septa contain 0.5 µL of aluminum, or about 1.35 mg assuming pure-Al density. The reaction 2 Al + 2 NaOH + 6 H2O → 2 Na[Al(OH)4] + 3 H2 gives a complete-conversion upper bound of about 75 µmol H2, approximately 1.8 mL at 25 °C and 1 atm. The five 20 mm × 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm channels hold about 25 µL, so this gas upper bound is roughly 74 times the network liquid volume. If all of it were confined to a gas volume equal to 25 µL, the ideal-gas upper-bound pressure would be about 7.4 MPa absolute; a 1 µL foil volume doubles both bounds. These are stoichiometric sealed-volume limits, not predicted operating pressures: an open, vented flow path may export gas, but a trapped bubble or blocked outlet can instead cause gas lock or pressure excursions. A foiled partition also leaves edge-sealing, folds, alloy residue, aluminate export and caustic-host compatibility unresolved.

Hoek et al. show that flushing PDMS/PDMS and glass/PDMS channels with 1 M NaOH for 24 h changes PDMS surface chemistry and doubles electroosmotic flow. They did not report the proposed Al etch or the unspecified epoxy formulation. This is evidence that caustic exposure can change microchannel surface behavior, not evidence that the target host is damaged or safe. [Hoek et al., 2010](https://doi.org/10.1039/C004769D)

## Closest incumbents and what they establish

- **F127 in epoxy:** Zhang et al. directly establish room-temperature epoxy cure, 4 °C F127 removal and submillimetre channels; this is the closest material/process incumbent.
- **Removable alginate microvalves:** Saez et al. establish Ca-alginate gating and EDTA erasure in a fluidic chip, but with reagent access through a cross-channel.
- **Sacrificial Al templates:** Yamashita et al. establish Al-template removal in PDMS/epoxy, but with heated NaOH, larger tubular/ball forms and a more extensive rinse.
- **Injection-based removal and a strong competitor:** Chu et al. experimentally compare injection-driven removal with centrifugation. In their geometries, parallel-path resistance differences above 2000× led to shunting; injection failed to clear high-resistance channels and some devices cracked. Centrifugation cleared their tested wax/resin geometries, but required monotone channel geometry relative to the centrifugal-force direction. This supports testing a series-routing concept against a well-optimized two-port wash; it does not establish an advantage for this F127/epoxy lollipop. The lollipop is a closed return loop and may not meet the monotonicity requirement without changing its geometry. [Chu et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1089/3dp.2021.0133)
- **Older U-turn divider concepts:** US4800953A and US20080003325A1 describe baffles that partition blind passages into supply/return legs to force a U-turn flow. They are not sacrificial microfluidic gates, but show the series return-routing concept is established outside the proposed material-removal operation. This is a prior-art flag, not a patentability or freedom-to-operate opinion. [US4800953A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4800953A/en), [US20080003325A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080003325A1/en)
- **cPPA sacrificial templates:** Garg et al. demonstrate embedded vascular templates in polymer matrices, but their reported removal uses 110 °C for 1 h (or about 100 °C over a longer period), outside the target temperature. [Garg et al., 2022](https://doi.org/10.1021/acsapm.1c01372)

The bounded search did not identify the exact combination “two-port forced series passage around a final blind side-loop, followed by localized removal of one root partition” in a primary paper. That does not establish novelty; the relevant mechanical routing concept is old, and the alginate valve, F127/epoxy sacrificial-channel and Al-template elements all have direct precedents.

## Proposed feasibility gates

All numerical limits below are proposed engineering screens to be preregistered with the device owner; they are not literature-derived standards.

1. **Lock the build.** Specify exact epoxy formulation and cure, alginate composition/crosslink treatment, gate placement and perimeter contact, F127 concentration, cold-wash carrier composition, EDTA concentration, maximum process temperature, pressure limit, total fluid/time budget, and residue assays. Measure temperature in the device; the entire process must remain below 40 °C.

2. **Root-gate fabrication and seal.** Make a 3 × 3 × 1 mm root-chamber coupon with the proposed 0.5 mm diagonal gate in the exact host. Test both orientations with tracer before and during the longest Stage-1 wash. Require no visible bypass and less than 1% cross-gate tracer transfer at the maximum intended Stage-1 pressure; record gate movement and leakage quantitatively. A defect-free gate should force flow into the loop without extra ports.

3. **Gate survival while F127 is cleared.** Test the real low-temperature F127 recipe through a representative 1 × 1 mm loop of the full 80 mm centerline length. Map tracer arrival at proximal, midpoint and distal loop locations and quantify F127 remaining in matched destructive replicates with a validated polymer-specific assay and spike-recovery. Proposed pass: at least 95% of the loaded F127 is accounted for in effluent or validated extracts, residual is below 1% of initial load or the assay’s lower quantification limit, and gate leakage remains below the Stage-1 limit through that endpoint. Require the measured time to clear the distal loop to be at least twofold shorter than the time for first unacceptable gate leakage. If the Ca-alginate gate cannot survive the cold F127 wash without a carrier that breaks F127 clearance, stop this design.

4. **Root-gate erasure and export.** Only after Stage-1 endpoint is met, deliver EDTA through A and D. In time-stopped witness replicates, map residual alginate through the gate thickness and perimeter; quantify EDTA and calcium-containing effluent until prespecified wash endpoints are met. Require complete gate opening, no residual polymer/particles that obstruct the root, no leak/delamination or crack, and device temperature below 40 °C. Assay the alginate and EDTA products rather than treating visual gate disappearance as clean removal.

5. **Final dimensional and functional check.** Verify actual channel cross-sections and gate clearance with a method demonstrated to resolve the 1 mm loop and submillimetre gate features. For an opaque host, a transparent witness alone is insufficient: use validated-resolution micro-CT or serial destructive sections on the actual material. Confirm A–D mainline flow, and separately confirm the blind lobe’s open geometry since final A–D conductance cannot establish that an articulation-attached loop is unobstructed. If the intended use is an acoustic side-lobe, measure the post-process acoustic response against the design prediction and an appropriate no-loop control; hydraulic continuity alone is not that function.

6. **Matched baseline and stop rule.** Run a matched device with the best ordinary two-port F127 wash (including justified reverse-flow/pressure-pulse settings) at the same maximum pressure, total fluid, time and host. Include the centrifugation option only if the closed-loop geometry can be made monotone with the force direction and the host/device can tolerate it. If direct washing already reaches the same F127 residual and final function at equal or lower burden, the routing method has no demonstrated manufacturing advantage. Advance from coupons to the full device only after gates 2–4 pass.

## Decisive experiment

The decisive test is a replicated, exact-material two-port lollipop coupon pair: 1 × 1 mm, 80 mm loop; 3 × 3 × 1 mm root; 0.5 mm Ca-alginate diagonal gate; the actual epoxy and F127 recipes; identical A/D ports; one routed device and one optimized direct-wash control. Run cold F127 clearance, then EDTA removal, measuring pressure, temperature, distal tracer arrival, F127 mass balance, gate leakage/remaining alginate by staged destructive specimens, effluent Ca/EDTA, final cross-sections, and the target acoustic function if applicable. This one experiment discriminates the material-seal question, the central washout claim and its strongest practical comparator. No result from it exists yet.
