\[
r_i=\operatorname{rank}\bar d_i,

h_i=n_i-r_i-r_{i+1},

\[

The crucial field-linear decomposition is

\[
\ker(Y\mapsto AY+YA)

\operatorname{im}(X\mapsto AX-XA)

\bigoplus_i\operatorname{Hom}(H_i,H_{i-1}).

I used this decomposition **one arithmetic digit at a time**.

## 3. Exact counting and uniform generation

Let \(C_e(\mathbf n;q)\) denote the number of actual complexes of graded dimensions \(\mathbf n\), over a chain ring of length \(e\) and residue cardinality \(q\). Set \(C_0=1\).

For a feasible residue-rank profile \(\mathbf r\), let \(F_q(\mathbf n;\mathbf r)\) count its field complexes. This is explicit:

\[
F_q(\mathbf n;\mathbf r)
=
\prod_{i=1}^{L}
\left[
{n_{i-1}-r_{i-1}\brack r_i}_q
\prod_{j=0}^{r_i-1}(q^{n_i}-q^j)
\right].
\]

The Gaussian binomial chooses the image inside the preceding kernel; the second factor chooses an onto map to that image.

Define

\[
E_1(\mathbf h)=\sum_i h_i h_{i+1}.
\]

The bijection proves the exact recurrence

\[
\boxed{
C_e(\mathbf n;q)
=
\sum_{\mathbf r}
F_q(\mathbf n;\mathbf r)\,
q^{(e-1)b(\mathbf n,\mathbf h)+E_1(\mathbf h)}
C_{e-2}(\mathbf h;q).
}
\]

Every factor has an accounted-for origin:

- \(F_q\) counts the residue differentials.
- \(q^{(e-1)b}\) counts the reversible gauge coordinates.
- \(q^{E_1(\mathbf h)}\) counts the free highest digit.
- \(C_{e-2}(\mathbf h;q)\) counts the remaining compatible complex.

This also proves an arithmetic universality statement:

\[
\boxed{
|\mathcal C_R(\mathbf n)|
\text{ depends only on }q,e,\mathbf n.
}
\]

In particular,

\[
|\mathcal C_{\mathbb Z/p^e\mathbb Z}(\mathbf n)|
=
|\mathcal C_{\mathbb F_p[t]/(t^e)}(\mathbf n)|.
\]

The residue-rank distributions agree as well.

### The sampler constructs the actual matrices

The exact sampler:

1. Chooses a residue-rank profile with its full integer weight from the recurrence.
2. Recursively samples the smaller complex on homology.
3. Samples the free digit and gauge coordinates uniformly.
4. Reverses the changes of basis and randomizes the residue complex within its field orbit.

Uniformity follows from the bijection and equal orbit-fiber multiplicities. It does not depend on empirical frequency tests.

There is no rejection of complete tuples because they violate \(D^2=0\). The implementation does use bounded-expected-cost rejection to obtain invertible field matrices, an explicitly paid local operation.

## 6. The growth law has a geometric and probabilistic meaning

The transfer matrix’s diagonal entries are

\[
\lambda_{\mathbf v}
=
q^{\,\|\mathbf n\|_2^2-\|\mathbf v\|_2^2+E_1(\mathbf v)}.
\]

Let \(\lambda_*\) be the largest diagonal value. Let \(J\) be the maximum number of states attaining \(\lambda_*\) along a strict directed reduction path.

The exact leading law is

\[
\boxed{
C_e(\mathbf n;q)
=
q^{\gamma e}e^{J-1}
\bigl(c_{e\bmod2}+o(1)\bigr),
}
\]

with explicitly computable positive constants \(c_0,c_1\).

The proof uses positivity at the dominant pole. Paths containing \(J\) dominant states contribute the highest polynomial power, with positive coefficients, so the leading contribution cannot cancel.

### The exponential rate equals the largest field-stratum dimension

A further constructive argument gives

\[
\boxed{
\gamma
=
\max_{\mathbf r}
\frac{\|\mathbf n\|_2^2-\|\mathbf h\|_2^2}{2}.
}
\]

This is the dimension of the largest change-of-basis stratum in the field variety of complexes. In particular, \(\gamma\) is an integer.

The key inequality is elementary but useful. Define

\[
Q(\mathbf h)=\|\mathbf h\|_2^2-E_1(\mathbf h).
\]

Split the adjacent edge products into the two alternating matchings of the chain. One matching contains at least half their total weight. Place full-rank maps on those disjoint edges. Cancelling a pair of dimensions \((a,b)\) lowers squared norm by exactly \(2ab\). Further cancellations lead to a terminal homology vector \(\mathbf k\), with no adjacent positive dimensions, satisfying

\[
\|\mathbf k\|_2^2\le Q(\mathbf h).
\]

This proves the equality needed to identify the transfer growth exponent with the largest field-stratum dimension.

The exponent also has a smaller evaluator: a path dynamic program minimizes

\[
\sum_i(n_i-r_i-r_{i+1})^2

under the rank constraints. It uses \(O((L+1)(N+1)^2)\) scalar transitions, where \(N=\max_i n_i\), and returns a minimizing rank profile as a witness.

### The polynomial factor measures how depth is shared between homology states

A genuinely different constructive perspective supplied a stronger interpretation of \(J-1\).

Apply the specified recursive reduction to a uniformly chosen deep complex. Remove self steps from its homology history, leaving a strict path. Conditional on a dominant path containing \(J\) dominant states:

\[
\boxed{
\left(
\frac{T_1}{m},\ldots,\frac{T_J}{m}
\right)
\Longrightarrow
\operatorname{Dirichlet}(1,\ldots,1),
\qquad m=\lfloor e/2\rfloor.
}
\]

Here \(T_i\) counts the reduction levels spent at the \(i\)-th dominant state. The limiting distribution is uniform on the \((J-1)\)-dimensional simplex.

Residence counts at lower-growth states converge to independent geometric distributions. The limiting weights of the different dominant strict paths are explicit positive rational expressions.

The mechanism is exact: after fixing the lower-state residence counts, all weak compositions of the remaining levels among dominant states have equal weight. Their normalized lattice points converge to uniform simplex measure.

For the simplest case \(\mathbf n=(1,1,1)\), \(q=2\), and even depth \(2m\), the probability of no strict change is exactly \(1/(m+1)\). Conditional on a change to either terminal state, its location is exactly uniform among the \(m\) possible steps.

Thus the factor \(e^{J-1}\) has a concrete meaning: it counts the macroscopic ways arithmetic depth can be distributed across dominant homology states. This describes the specified reduction algorithm, with no physical-time interpretation assumed.
