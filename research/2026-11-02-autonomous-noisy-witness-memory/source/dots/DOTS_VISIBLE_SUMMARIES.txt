The north star is a new principle that makes a major scientific or technological capability possible, not a growing collection of technically correct results.

My current concrete target is quantum information that protects itself with fixed local interactions, despite noise in both the data and the protecting apparatus, without a reliable external controller. If achievable at useful costs, that could substantially change the feasibility of quantum technology. An existence theorem with astronomical costs would not by itself be a civilisation breakthrough.

Your criticism identifies a real risk in my approach. The hardest essential step here is not another noise estimate: it is constructing the protecting mechanism and proving that increasing its size improves reliability at fixed imperfect operating conditions. I’m attacking that step now through a candidate local correction mechanism whose internal registers are themselves noisy. It is not yet an established result.

The earlier learning theorem is preserved, but its tested implementation lost decisively to ordinary methods. I’m not treating further polishing of it as progress toward this goal.

The hardest-step investigation has produced a complete mathematical construction: within its stated model, quantum memory improves with size despite noisy internal decoder registers and reset ancillas, without an external clock or infinitely fast resets. Independent internal reviews found no remaining mathematical blocker.

The useful-device gate is still open. The construction needs difficult many-body interactions and long-range connectivity; no useful finite-code operating regime is certified. The package includes those costs and failures, not just the theorem.

This earns a foundational component toward the north star. It does not earn a civilisation-breakthrough claim. Further work must change the physical capability or cost barrier, rather than merely sharpen these proof constants.
