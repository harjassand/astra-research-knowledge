\]

the transfer time is \(\pi/\Omega\), and

\[
\frac12\|\mathcal T-\mathrm{id}\|_\diamond
\le
1-e^{-\pi n\lambda/\Omega}
\le

A smooth \(\sin^2\) envelope doubles the time to \(2\pi/\Omega\) and has finite slew. It uses \(n\) receiving qubits and \(n\) parallel exchange interactions; aggregate peak interaction norm is \(n\Omega/2\).

## 5. Why the converse works

The load-bearing operation is to compare **an early hidden error** with **an error already present in the unknown input**.

### Early errors remain nearly indistinguishable from input changes

Move the hidden \(Z\) jumps in an initial interval \([0,\delta]\) to the input side of the evolution. The remaining dynamics differ only through the part of the Hamiltonian that fails to commute with \(Z\).

The resulting dilation-amplitude discrepancy is at most

\[
A(\delta).
\]

Crucially, this bound is independent of how many early jumps occurred: their parity toggles the sign of the noncommuting Hamiltonian.

Let \(s_0\) denote successful Bell-input probability when the selected hidden noise is suppressed only during that initial interval. Comparing amplitudes gives

\[
\sqrt{s_0}\ge\sqrt{s}-A(\delta).
\]

This is stronger than subtracting the entire early-noise probability from success. It prevents a decoder from apparently escaping the bound by rejecting an unspecified “bad component.”

### Rejecting early errors also suppresses acquisition

For a single hidden jump at time \(t\), the comparison input has acquired a \(Z\). On the reference half of a Bell state, this produces an orthogonal Bell state.

If the no-early-error process successfully transfers the original input, this nearby error process necessarily contributes a wrong-Bell component unless the available control action has already made the two processes distinguishable.

Positivity of the trajectory decomposition then yields, for a suitable initial interval,

\[
e\ge\frac{567}{2048}\lambda\delta
\ge\frac{\lambda\delta}{4}.
\]

### The protocol cannot escape by stopping immediately

Remove every noncommuting source interaction. Once the source is discarded, the remaining output can depend only on its initial \(Z\) value. That comparison channel is entanglement breaking.

A bounded-amplitude comparison with this channel shows that successful high-fidelity acquisition requires

\[
A(T)\ge\frac{\sqrt{s}}8.
\]

Choose the first interval with \(A(\delta)=\sqrt{s}/8\), capped at \(1/(8\lambda)\). For \(e<1/32\), the cap cannot be active, and \(\delta\le4e/\lambda\). This gives the integrated-action theorem.

All these comparisons occur **before normalizing the successful state**. No invalid contraction argument is applied after postselection.

### Why the register result allows collective receivers

For \(n\) inputs, moving all early hidden jumps to the input produces the comparison

\[
\left\|
\mathcal A_T-
\mathcal A_0\circ\mathcal D_\delta^{\otimes n}
\right\|_\diamond
\le n\lambda\Omega\delta^2,
\]

where

\[
\mathcal D_\delta(\rho)
=
\frac{1+e^{-2\lambda\delta}}2\,\rho
+
\frac{1-e^{-2\lambda\delta}}2\,Z\rho Z.
\]

The product dephasing appears in the **derived comparison**, not as an assumption about the actual receiver output.

Its different input \(Z\)-strings create mutually orthogonal reference Bell states. No subsequent positive successful operation can give Bell fidelity exceeding the largest string probability, apart from the explicitly bounded comparison error. A separate short-time argument covers protocols ending before \(\delta\). Choosing

\[
\delta=\frac{\ln n}{n\lambda}
\]

gives the strong converse.
