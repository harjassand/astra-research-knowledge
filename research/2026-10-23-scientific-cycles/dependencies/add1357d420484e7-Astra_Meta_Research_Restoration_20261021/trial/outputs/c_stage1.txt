TASK 1 — Exact identification with calibration

Theta is uniquely identified for every attainable observation (y,z) if and only if rank(CT)=q, equivalently ker(CT)={0}. In geometric terms, this says ker(C) intersects im(T) only at zero.

Because A has full column rank, its left inverse A^+=(A^T A)^{-1}A^T exists, so the observations determine

    w=A^+y=B+T theta.

Consequently they determine

    d=Cw-z=CT theta.

Sufficiency: If M=CT has full column rank, theta is reconstructed directly as

    theta=(M^T M)^{-1}M^T(CA^+y-z).

This formula recovers the same theta for every parameter pair producing the observations.

Necessity: If rank(CT)<q, choose a nonzero v with CTv=0. For any pair (B,theta), the distinct pair

    theta'=theta+v,    B'=B-Tv

has B'+T theta'=B+T theta and CB'=CB. Thus both observations are identical although theta changes. Nonuniqueness therefore occurs at every attainable observation.

The geometric equivalence uses the injectivity of T: CTv=0 means Tv lies in ker(C), and Tv is nonzero whenever v is nonzero.

Full reconstruction of B is not required first. The reconstruction uses only w and the calibrated contrast Cw-z; C itself need not have full column rank. Once theta is known, B=w-T theta is also uniquely reconstructed.

Specified designs: With A=I_3, w=y and

    T=[[1,0],[1,0],[0,1]].

For C_bad=[1,-1,0], C_bad T=[0,0]. The calibration merely repeats z=y_1-y_2, giving no information about theta. Both theta coordinates remain arbitrary, with B=y-T theta.

For C_good=[[1,0,0],[0,0,1]], C_good T=I_2. Hence

    theta=(y_1-z_1, y_3-z_2)^T,
    B=(z_1, y_2-y_1+z_1, z_2)^T.

Minimum additional calibration rows: Let r=rank(CT). Exactly q-r arbitrary new rows are necessary and sufficient. Necessity follows because each appended row raises rank by at most one. For sufficiency, extend a basis of the row space of CT to a basis of R^q, using q-r row vectors v_j. Every such vector can be realized as c_j T: take

    c_j=v_j(T^T T)^{-1}T^T.

Appending these c_j to C makes the augmented product with T have rank q. Thus C_bad needs two extra rows, while C_good needs none.

TASK 2 — A negative frozen gain does not rule out positive transfer

The fixed ordinary prediction b cancels from the error. Write d=x^T y and s=||x||^2. Expanding gives

    L(alpha)=||y||^2-2 alpha d+alpha^2 s,
    G(alpha)=L(0)-L(alpha)=2 alpha d-alpha^2 s.

In particular,

    G(1/2)=d-s/4.

A strictly negative frozen gain therefore means d<s/4, which does not imply d<=0. It also implies x is nonzero, since x=0 gives zero gain at every alpha.

For x nonzero and alpha>0, positive gain occurs exactly when

    d>0 and 0<alpha<2d/s.

Thus some positive strength improves this point loss if and only if x^T y>0. If d>0, the optimal strength is alpha*=d/s and its gain is d^2/s. If d<=0, no positive strength improves the loss. If x=0, every strength gives identical loss.

Counterexample: In one dimension, take x=1 and y=1/8. Their inner product is 1/8>0, but

    L(0)=1/64,
    L(1/2)=9/64,
    G(1/2)=-1/8<0.

At alpha=1/8, however, L(alpha)=0 and G(alpha)=1/64>0. The frozen strength overshoots despite positive alignment.

This algebra supports the possibility of a beneficial smaller strength and permits exploratory analysis of why the frozen operation lost. It does not change the negative result at alpha=1/2. Choosing alpha after seeing the evaluation target y makes the resulting score a target-fitted, exploratory or oracle result; it cannot establish success under the original frozen criterion or an independently validated predictive benefit. A new fixed strength or adaptive selection rule needs evaluation on fresh held-out outcomes, with any strength selection confined to the permitted training or validation information. The point-loss calculation alone establishes neither generalization nor an underlying scientific mechanism.
