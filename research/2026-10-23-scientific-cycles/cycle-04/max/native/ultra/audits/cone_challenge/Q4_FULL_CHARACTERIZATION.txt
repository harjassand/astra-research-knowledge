Q4 MATCHING DOMINATION: FULL DIMENSION-FOUR CHARACTERIZATION
2026-10-22. Exact analytical result; independent internal proof audit passed.
No floating-point search or SDP-solver output is used.

THEOREM A.
Let B be a real symmetric, entrywise nonnegative 4-by-4 matrix with every
row sum equal to one. Let P1=P_(12)(34), P2=P_(13)(24), P3=P_(14)(23).
The following are equivalent:

(i) There are m_i>=0, sum_i m_i=1, with B-sum_i m_i P_i >=0.
(ii) The four nonnegative numbers sqrt(B_ii) obey the polygon inequality:

       max_i sqrt(B_ii) <= sum_{j!=i_max} sqrt(B_jj).

THEOREM B.
Every complex symmetric unitary 4-by-4 matrix S satisfies the polygon
inequality for its diagonal moduli |S_ii|. Therefore B_ij=|S_ij|^2 always
satisfies (i).

Scope: these statements concern only the specified dimension-four matching
cone. They do not assert any extension to larger dimensions or a full
entropy theorem.

PROOF OF THEOREM A.
Write d_i=B_ii.

Necessity. Put D=B-sum m_i P_i. Then D>=0, D 1=0, and D_ii=d_i.
Represent D as a real Gram matrix D_ij=<v_i,v_j>. The row-sum condition
implies ||sum_i v_i||^2=1^T D 1=0, so sum_i v_i=0. Taking norms gives
||v_i||<=sum_{j!=i}||v_j||, and ||v_i||=sqrt(d_i).

Signed feasibility from the polygon condition. The elementary polygon
lemma says that nonnegative lengths l_i admit real planar vectors v_i
of those lengths summing to zero exactly when max l_i<=sum others.
This includes zero lengths and degenerate straight polygons. Apply it
with l_i=sqrt(d_i), and let D be the resulting Gram matrix. It is PSD,
has diagonal d, and has zero row sums. Thus E=B-D is symmetric, has zero
diagonal, and every row sum is one. Such a matrix has equal complementary
edge entries:

    E12=E34, E13=E24, E14=E23.

Indeed subtraction of the sums of rows 1,2 and 3,4 gives E12=E34,
and relabeling gives the rest. Consequently E=sum_i n_i P_i for REAL
weights n_i summing to one. These weights may have either sign.

Walsh compression. Let Q have columns

    (1,1,-1,-1)^T/2, (1,-1,1,-1)^T/2, (1,-1,-1,1)^T/2,

and put A=(Q^T B Q+I_3)/2. The columns of Q are an orthonormal basis
of 1-perp. The three P_i restrict to diag(1,-1,-1), diag(-1,1,-1),
diag(-1,-1,1), respectively. Therefore, for weights of total one,

    B-sum_i n_i P_i>=0 iff A-diag(n)>=0.                 (1)

In particular the signed construction supplies a feasible n with sum1.

Key property: A is diagonally dominant with nonnegative diagonal.
For its first row, write D0=sum_i d_i and s1=B12+B34. Direct expansion
using the row sums gives

    A11=(D0+2s1)/4,
    A12=(d1-d2-d3+d4)/4,
    A13=(d1-d2+d3-d4)/4.

The row sums also give

    B12-B34=(d3+d4-d1-d2)/2.

Since B12,B34>=0,

    A11 >= max(d1+d2,d3+d4)/2
          >= max(|d1-d2|,|d3-d4|)/2
          = |A12|+|A13|.

The other two rows follow by relabeling. Thus A is PSD as well: it is
a sum of nonnegative diagonal matrices and signed edge squares.

Removing the sign restriction by dual Gram normalization.
All SDP variables and Gram matrices in this proof are real symmetric.
Consider the two finite-dimensional SDPs

    mu_free = max sum_i t_i,  A-diag(t)>=0, t in R^3;
    mu_pos  = max sum_i t_i,  A-diag(t)>=0, t_i>=0.

Their respective duals are

    nu_eq = min tr(A X), X>=0, X_ii=1;
    nu_ge = min tr(A X), X>=0, X_ii>=1.

Both primals are feasible and bounded: for free variables take sufficiently
negative t; for positive variables t=0 works because A>=0; in either case
sum t<=tr A. The duals have strict feasible points I and 2I respectively
(relative to their equality constraints). Finite-dimensional SDP strong
duality therefore gives mu_free=nu_eq and mu_pos=nu_ge. The positive
primal attains its optimum because 0<=t_i<=A_ii gives a compact feasible
set.

Now let X>=0 have X_ii>=1. Take Gram vectors x_i with lengths alpha_i>=1,
and normalize to y_i=x_i/alpha_i. Their Gram matrix Y satisfies Y>=0,
Y_ii=1. For any i,j, write c=<y_i,y_j> in [-1,1] and sigma in {+1,-1}.
Then

    ||x_i+sigma x_j||^2 - ||y_i+sigma y_j||^2
      = (alpha_i-alpha_j)^2
        +2(alpha_i alpha_j-1)(1+sigma c) >=0.            (2)

Diagonal dominance gives the exact decomposition

    A=diag(r_i)+sum_{i<j}|A_ij|
       (e_i+sign(A_ij)e_j)(e_i+sign(A_ij)e_j)^T,
    r_i=A_ii-sum_{j!=i}|A_ij|>=0,

omitting zero edges. Equation (2) and ||x_i||>=||y_i|| show
tr(A Y)<=tr(A X). Hence every feasible point of nu_ge normalizes to a
point of nu_eq with no greater objective. As the nu_eq feasible set is
also contained in that of nu_ge, their optimal values are equal.
Therefore mu_pos=mu_free.

The signed construction and (1) show mu_free>=1. Thus an optimal
nonnegative t has total T>=1 and A-diag(t)>=0. Set m=t/T. Then m>=0,
sum m=1, and

    A-diag(m)=(A-diag(t))+diag(t-m)>=0.

Equation (1) proves the required convex matching domination. This also
handles d=0 and every singular or zero-coordinate case. QED Theorem A.

PROOF OF THEOREM B.
Fix a diagonal position and permute it to the first position. Partition

    S=[ a  v^T ]
      [ v   T  ],

where T is a complex symmetric 3-by-3 matrix. Unitarity implies

    T T^*=I_3-v v^*,    ||v||^2=1-|a|^2.

Thus the singular values of T, in decreasing order, are (1,1,|a|).
Let Z=T-diag(T), a hollow complex symmetric 3-by-3 matrix.

There is a diagonal unitary U such that U Z U is real symmetric and
hollow. If all three edges are nonzero, choose phases phi_i solving
phi_i+phi_j=-arg Z_ij for the three edges; this three-equation system
is invertible. If some edges vanish, solve the remaining subset, which
is always consistent. Unitary congruence preserves singular values.
A real symmetric hollow 3-by-3 matrix has trace zero, so its real
eigenvalues sum to zero. Their absolute values therefore satisfy

    s1(Z)=s2(Z)+s3(Z).                                   (3)

This includes the zero and rank-deficient cases.

Use the standard trace-norm singular-value perturbation inequality
(Mirsky inequality), stated here in the needed form:

    sum_{k=1}^3 |s_k(T)-s_k(Z)| <= ||T-Z||_*.

Because (3) holds and s(T)=(1,1,|a|), the scalar triangle inequality gives

    |a| = |(1-s1(Z))-(1-s2(Z))-(|a|-s3(Z))|
        <= sum_k |s_k(T)-s_k(Z)|
        <= ||diag(T)||_*
         = sum_{j!=first}|S_jj|.

The first displayed equality is understood in absolute value: its
inside is -|a| by (3). Repeating for each diagonal position proves the
polygon inequalities. B=|S|^2 is real symmetric nonnegative doubly
stochastic, so Theorem A applies. QED Theorem B.

Accessible primary research source containing the exact needed inequality:
Koenraad M.R. Audenaert, "A generalisation of Mirsky's singular value
inequalities", arXiv:1410.4941v1 (2014), page 1, Theorem 1, equation (1).
PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.4941
Abstract/versions: https://arxiv.org/abs/1410.4941
For complex n-by-n X,Y, nonnegative concave f with f(0)=0, m<=n,
and increasing indices i_1,...,i_m, that theorem states

  sum_{k=1}^m |f(sigma_{i_k}(X))-f(sigma_{i_k}(Y))|
    <= sum_{k=1}^m f(sigma_k(X-Y)).

Set f(t)=t, m=n=3, and i_k=k. This is exactly the trace-norm
perturbation inequality used in Theorem B above. Audenaert identifies
the f-free version as Mirsky's inequalities immediately after (1), and
his reference [5] is the original 1960 Mirsky paper.
