## The advance I would pursue

**I developed a concrete mathematical candidate: a guarantee that ordinary local measurements can control rare failure probabilities at every deadline, with relative uncertainty that does not grow with the number of times the system cycles before failing.**

The mechanism is a comparison through **complete excursions**: stretches of dynamics that leave a state and eventually return to it, or reach the consequential outcome. This makes it possible to account for uncertainty once per relevant state, even when a typical lifetime contains an enormous number of repeated attempts.

I completed a proof, a finite-data learning construction, a design guarantee, an information lower bound, and a numerical example with explicit arithmetic error bounds. These underwent separate internal mathematical and code audits. **Historical priority and a practical advantage in a physical system remain unestablished.** The promising result is a conditional mathematical foundation; the principal scientific challenge is earning its assumptions in a consequential system.

## 1. The precise scientific question

I would focus on **rare failure produced by a network of individually accessible transitions**.

Examples within the mathematical class include repeated repair, protective sequences, competing pathways, and metastable traps. Each elementary move can be reasonably common when the system is prepared in the appropriate state, while the complete failure requires an exceptionally unlikely combination of those moves.

The question is:

> **Can local experiments support simultaneous, relative confidence bounds on mean lifetime and failure before any mission deadline—and support design choices using those bounds—at a cost governed by local complexity rather than the natural waiting time for failure?**

Let \(B\) denote failure, and let \(\tau_B\) be its first occurrence. The two outputs are

\[
m_Q(x)=\mathbb E_x[\tau_B],
\qquad
F_Q(t,x)=\Pr_x(\tau_B\le t).
\]

The distinction matters. A design can increase mean lifetime while making early failure more likely. I constructed a simple two-path example in which the mean improves by approximately **a factor of 1,000**, while failure before an early deadline becomes approximately **10 times more likely**. A deep trap creates the longer mean; an increased direct leak creates the worse early risk.

For a very small \(F_Q(t)\), useful accuracy must be relative. An absolute error of \(10^{-6}\) says little about a risk of \(10^{-12}\). The desired statement has the form

\[
e^{-\varepsilon}F_Q(t)
\le \widehat F(t)
\le e^\varepsilon F_Q(t).
\]

There is an essential boundary: an unknown elementary catastrophe that is itself extremely rare generally remains expensive to learn. The favorable regime is **global rarity assembled from learnable local behavior**.

Here, “deadline risk” specifically means failure before a deadline. Rare late completion or extreme survival tails require a separate analysis.

## 2. The central result: a relative bound valid at every deadline

Consider two finite, time-homogeneous continuous-time Markov models, \(Q\) and \(\widetilde Q\). They must have:

- The same full state description.
- The same possible transitions.
- The same initial state or initial distribution.
- Positive rates on the transitions being compared.

The rates may differ, and the network may contain arbitrary cycles, branching, and competing absorbing outcomes.

For each transition out of state \(i\), define its log-rate change

\[
\ell_{ij}=\log\frac{\widetilde q_{ij}}{q_{ij}}.
\]

For each non-target state, define

\[
d_i=
\max\!\left(0,\max_j\ell_{ij}\right)
-
\min\!\left(0,\min_j\ell_{ij}\right),
\qquad
D=\sum_{i\notin B}d_i.
\]

### Deadline comparison

The construction proves

\[
\boxed{
e^{-D}F_Q(t)
\le F_{\widetilde Q}(t)
\le e^D F_Q(t)
\quad\text{for every }t\ge0.
}
\]

The bound contains **no factor proportional to the deadline, mean lifetime, number of returns, or inverse failure probability**.

If there are \(n\) affected states and every positive rate is known to log-accuracy \(\eta\), then

\[
D\le 2n\eta.
\]

Thus sufficient local precision gives a relative certificate for an arbitrarily small failure probability, even at an extremely long deadline.

This is stronger than claiming that a fitted model predicts a long lifetime. It specifies how errors in measured mechanisms can affect the prediction.

A particularly sharp consequence concerns one transition. Multiplying a single rate by \(c\ge1\) gives

\[
c^{-1}F_Q(t)
\le F_{\widetilde Q}(t)
\le cF_Q(t).
\]

Where the derivative exists and the risk is positive,

\[
\left|
\frac{\partial\log F_Q(t)}
{\partial\log q_{ij}}
\right|\le1.
\]

Increasing a rate can increase or decrease risk: the transition might lead toward failure, toward repair, or into a trap. The result controls the magnitude of its influence.

### Mean lifetime and resource budgets

A separate regenerative argument gives the same multiplicative comparison for **finite mean lifetimes**. It is a separate proof because a relative CDF comparison cannot simply be integrated to obtain a relative mean bound.

The excursion construction also extends to failure subject to joint upper limits on unchanged, nonnegative accumulated costs:

\[
\Pr\{\tau_B\le t,\;
\text{energy used}\le E,\;
\text{exposure}\le A\}.
\]

Deleting a return excursion reduces all such costs. Signed rewards, required minimum activity, and exact timing windows do not share that property.

## 3. Why repeated attempts do not multiply the uncertainty indefinitely

The proof’s useful idea is to organize paths around **returns to a full state**.

Suppose only the outgoing rates of one state \(v\) change. Write

\[
r_j=\frac{\widetilde q_{vj}}{q_{vj}},
\qquad
r_-=\min_jr_j,
\qquad
r_+=\max_jr_j,
\qquad
b=\max(1,r_+).
\]

Measure time only while the system is actually in \(v\). In this accumulated local time, departures of each type \(v\to j\) form Poisson processes.

Attach a complete excursion to every departure: the subsequent path until it first returns to \(v\), reaches the target, or reaches a competing absorbing outcome.

**The law of this excursion does not use the outgoing rates of \(v\).** It stops as soon as those rates would become relevant again.

Now construct the perturbed process:

1. Retain a departure of type \(j\) with probability \(r_j/b\).
2. Compress the source-state local-time coordinate by the factor \(b\).
3. Keep the complete excursions attached to retained departures.

These retained and rescaled departures have exactly the new rates.

Consider an original path that reaches failure by time \(t\). Every excursion before its final successful excursion returned to \(v\). If the final excursion is retained, the new path reaches the same failure after deleting some complete returns and spending no more time at \(v\).

The final excursion is retained with probability at least \(r_-/b\). Therefore,

\[
F_{\widetilde Q}(t)
\ge
\frac{r_-}{\max(1,r_+)}F_Q(t).
\]

Reverse the comparison to obtain the other direction:

\[
\frac{r_-}{\max(1,r_+)}F_Q(t)
\le F_{\widetilde Q}(t)
\le
\frac{r_+}{\min(1,r_-)}F_Q(t).
\]

Changing rows one at a time proves the general theorem.

This explains the absence of a lifetime factor. The proof preserves the final productive excursion while deleting complete returns. A billion repeated returns therefore need not incur a billion separate uncertainty penalties.

The comparison controls this particular hitting event. Two full trajectories can still become statistically distinguishable after many transitions.

## 4. Turning the result into learning and design

### Acquiring the local information

A concrete experimental service is:

- Prepare a specified full state.
- Observe its departure.
- Restart.
- Accumulate a fixed amount of actual residence time in that state.

If the accumulated residence exposure at state \(i\) is \(L_i\), the number of departures along edge \(i\to j\) satisfies

\[
N_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(q_{ij}L_i).
\]

Ordinary Poisson confidence intervals then bound the local rates.

For an explicit sufficient budget, suppose every admitted positive rate out of state \(i\) is at least a known \(q_{*,i}>0\). Let \(E\) be the number of admitted edges. Log-rate accuracy \(\eta_i\) for all edges, with overall confidence \(1-\delta\), follows from

\[
L_i\ge
\frac{3}
{q_{*,i}(1-e^{-\eta_i})^2}
\log\frac{2E}{\delta}.
\]

On that single confidence event,

\[
|\log \widehat F(t)-\log F(t)|
\le 2\sum_i\eta_i
\]

for every deadline.

There is no need to pay a separate statistical confidence penalty for every time point on the risk curve.

With a common lower local-rate bound \(q_*\), allocating \(\eta_i=\varepsilon/(2n)\) gives total residence exposure of order

\[
\boxed{
\frac{n^3}{q_*\varepsilon^2}
\log\frac{E}{\delta}.
}
\]

This is a conditional polynomial acquisition cost. Its units are time; it is not automatically a bound on the number of jumps.

Preparation must also be charged. If resetting state \(i\) costs mean time \(c_i\) and its exit rate is \(\lambda_i\), expected experimental time includes approximately

\[
\sum_i L_i(1+c_i\lambda_i),
\]

plus initial preparation and measurement costs. Expensive state access or a tiny elementary rate can erase the advantage.

### Choosing designs after seeing the data

Suppose a stationary design \(u\) has a qualified response law such as

\[
q_{ij}(u)=a_{ij}(u)\theta_{ij},
\]

where the multipliers \(a_{ij}(u)\) are known and the parameters \(\theta_{ij}\) are learned.

The same parameter confidence event controls every design in this family. The design may therefore be selected after inspecting the data.

If

\[
e^{-D}\widehat F_u
\le F_u
\le e^D\widehat F_u
\]

holds for every admissible design, and \(\widehat u\) minimizes the learned risk, then

\[
\boxed{
F_{\widehat u}\le e^{2D}\inf_u F_u.
}
\]

This turns prediction accuracy into a performance guarantee for design selection.

The response law and the optimization still have to be earned. An unknown physical response cannot be treated as known merely because it appears in a simulator, and a confidence theorem does not make a difficult global optimization easy.

### Why the cubic acquisition cost can be real

I also constructed a lower bound for a rich design family.

There are \(n\) stages, with all local transition probabilities between \(3/8\) and \(5/8\). Every elementary observation is ordinary, yet the global outcome is exponentially rare. Each stage contains an unknown small bias, and the design chooses which direction of that bias is favorable.

To select a design within a factor \(e^\varepsilon\) of optimal, an algorithm must recover enough of those small biases. A packing and Fano argument gives a requirement of order

\[
N=\Omega\!\left(\frac{n^3}{\varepsilon^2}\right)
\]

local observations at fixed high confidence, even with adaptive sampling and free preparation.

This lower bound applies to the specified observation service and design family. A single fixed prediction, or a system with shared parameters, can be easier. The numerical example below uses such shared structure.

## 5. A completed numerical demonstration

I used a 60-stage model:

- Elementary attempts occur at known Poisson rate \(2\).
- Each attempt succeeds with an unknown common probability \(p\).
- An unsuccessful attempt resets progress to zero.
- Sixty consecutive successful steps produce the consequential outcome.

At the generating value \(p=1/2\),

\[
m(p)
=
\frac{p^{-60}-1}{2(1-p)}
=
2^{60}-1
\approx 1.1529\times10^{18}
\]

model time units.

The synthetic experiment generated the sufficient count for **16 million ordinary local probes**:

\[
8{,}001{,}865\text{ successes},
\qquad
\widehat p=0.5001165625.
\]

A 99% Hoeffding interval gives approximately

\[
0.4997096566
\le p\le
0.5005234684.
\]

Under the shared-parameter model, that interval produces

\[
D\approx0.09684376,
\qquad
e^D\approx1.101688.
\]

The statistical event controls all deadlines simultaneously. For three selected deadlines, I also computed outward numerical enclosures:

| Deadline in model time units | Lower failure-risk bound | Upper failure-risk bound |
|---:|---:|---:|
| \(100\) | \(5.62606\times10^{-17}\) | \(6.92922\times10^{-17}\) |
| \(10^6\) | \(7.92377\times10^{-13}\) | \(9.75915\times10^{-13}\) |
| \(10^{18}\) | \(0.5271032\) | \(0.6491952\) |

Thus ordinary local observations support a useful relative interval for a deadline risk near **one in a trillion**, under the stated model assumptions.

The estimated mean lifetime is \(1.1372\times10^{18}\). A specialized conventional calculation using the same data gives the mean interval

\[
[1.08389745,\;1.19311791]\times10^{18}.

The expected exposure for the ordinary probes is \(8\times10^6\) model time units, before preparation overhead. Actual waiting times were not sampled; the experiment draws the aggregate outcome count.

## 6. Computing the tiny probabilities reliably

A prediction framework is incomplete if it leaves the requested probability behind an unspecified numerical solver.

For an absorbed generator \(G\), define the positive resolvent

\[
R_s=s(sI-G)^{-1}.
\]

This is a nonnegative stochastic matrix. The target coordinate of \(R_s^k\) computes the probability of reaching the target before a random deadline that is a sum of \(k\) exponential waits.

Set \(s=k/t\). The random deadline becomes \(tZ\), where \(Z\) has mean one and becomes concentrated as \(k\) increases.

The comparison theorem also yields

\[
F(ct)\le c^nF(t),\qquad c\ge1.
\]

That growth bound converts random-clock error into **relative deterministic-deadline error**. If

\[
H_k(t)=\mathbb E[F(tZ)],
\]

the construction gives an explicitly computable \(d_{n,k}<1\) such that

\[
\frac{H_k(t)}{1+d_{n,k}}
\le F(t)\le
\frac{H_k(t)}{1-d_{n,k}}.
\]

A choice \(k=O(n^2/\varepsilon_{\rm num}^2)\) suffices for a prescribed numerical relative tolerance.

The implementation uses:

- A residual certificate for the resolvent.
- Nonnegative matrix products with outward rounding.
- Exact rational gamma moments.
- An outward bound for the deterministic-clock conversion.

The demonstration used 75-digit arithmetic and \(k=2^{26}\), implemented through 26 matrix squarings. It never obtains a tiny failure risk by subtracting a nearly-one survival probability from one.

Erlangization itself is established numerical machinery. The role here is to combine a positive calculation with an explicit relative clock-conversion bound. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

The calculation still depends on the explicit state count. A finite system with an astronomical number of states remains a computational challenge.

## 8. The boundaries that determine whether this becomes useful

### An unseen failure pathway can dominate everything

Suppose the model predicts \(F_0(t)\), but there is an omitted independent catastrophe clock of rate \(\gamma\). Then

\[
F_\gamma(t)
=
1-e^{-\gamma t}\bigl(1-F_0(t)\bigr).
\]

Every measured rate in the original model can be correct while the real failure probability is much larger.

Observing zero catastrophes during total exposure \(L\) only supports the one-sided bound

\[
\gamma\le\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{L}.
\]

To make the extra risk small relative to \(F_0(t)\), one needs approximately

\[
\gamma t\ll F_0(t).
\]

Consequently, qualification by observation alone can require exposure of order

\[
L\gtrsim \frac{t}{F_0(t)},
\]

with additional factors for the desired relative margin and confidence.

**The framework cannot establish the absence of a rare pathway from silence.** Independent physical constraints or mechanism-specific evidence must carry that part of the argument.

### The state must retain the relevant memory

Consider two stage durations, each marginally uniform on \(\{1,3\}\).

In one system, the second duration equals the first. In another, the second is \(4\) minus the first. Both systems have the same local marginal waits and the same mean total duration, \(4\).

Their probabilities of finishing by time \(3\) are nevertheless

\[
\frac12
\quad\text{and}\quad
0.
\]

A hidden carried variable changes the deadline risk.

This is closely related to a known
