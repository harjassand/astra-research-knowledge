## 2. The sharp result: acquiring one local rate has a local cost

Consider an unknown stochastic network of binary coordinates. Coordinate \(i\) flips at a rate \(c_i\) that depends on a known neighbourhood \(B_i\):

\[
(Qf)(x)
=
\sum_i c_i(x_{B_i})
\bigl[f(x^i)-f(x)\bigr],
\qquad
0\le c_i\le\Lambda.
\]

Here \(x^i\) is the configuration obtained by flipping coordinate \(i\), and \(Q\) is the unknown generator.

The assumptions permit directed interactions, irreversible currents, zero rates, absorbing states and arbitrarily slow mixing. There is no equilibrium assumption.

The available interface is:

- An acquired **complete Markov-state checkpoint**, which may be opaque.
- Restoration and independent continuation of that checkpoint.
- Native evolution for a requested duration.
- Reading and comparing the relevant local coordinates.
- A warranted rate ceiling \(\Lambda\) and dependency neighbourhood.

The method does not inspect rates or event histories. For this point-query theorem, it also does not need to edit the state.

### The central cancellation

Suppose the desired quantity is the instantaneous flip rate of coordinate \(i\) at the current checkpoint. Set

\[
f(x)=\mathbf 1_{\{x_i=1\}},
\qquad
g(x)=Qf(x)=(1-2x_i)c_i(x_{B_i}).
\]

The important structural fact is that **\(g\) is local**.

The finite-time expectation of \(f\) generally depends on the exterior. A local observed process can retain substantial hidden memory. None of that prevents its instantaneous generator action \(g\) from depending on only a small neighbourhood.

Let \(B\) contain that neighbourhood, let \(b=|B|\), and run the unchanged simulator for an independent exponential duration with rate

\[
\mu=5b\Lambda.
\]

The resulting endpoint kernel is

\[
P=\mu(\mu I-Q)^{-1}.
\]

Resolvent-based generator reconstruction is established machinery; the new step here is the local stopping operation used to implement its inverse. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="4"}

The resolvent identity gives

\[
g=\mu(P-I)f+(I-P)g.
\]

Define, on functions of the **complete** checkpoint,

\[
(K_Bh)(x)
=
\mathbb E_x\!\left[
\mathbf 1_{\{Y_B\ne x_B\}}
\bigl(h(x)-h(Y)\bigr)
\right].
\]

Because the true \(g\) is \(B\)-local,

\[
K_Bg=(I-P)g.
\]

Meanwhile,

\[
\Pr(Y_B\ne x_B)
\le
\frac{b\Lambda}{\mu+b\Lambda}
=
\frac16,
\]

so

\[
\|K_B\|_{\infty\to\infty}\le\frac13.
\]

This makes the inverse a contracting fixed-point problem.

**Exterior changes are retained in checkpoints and continuations.** The stopping rule is justified by the locality of the particular function \(g\), without asserting that the observed neighbourhood has autonomous Markov dynamics.

### An executable estimator

At a signed checkpoint \((z,a)\), with \(a\in\{+1,-1\}\):

1. Obtain a fresh exponential-time native endpoint \(Y\).
2. Stop if \(Y_B=z_B\).
3. Otherwise add
   \[
   \mu a\bigl(f(Y)-f(z)\bigr)
   \]
   and continue with children \((z,a)\) and \((Y,-a)\).

The resulting signed sum \(G\) satisfies

\[
\mathbb EG=g(x).
\]

Therefore \((1-2x_i)G\) is an unbiased estimate of the desired rate.

The branching process terminates almost surely, with

\[
\mathbb E[\text{endpoint calls}]\le\frac32,
\qquad
\mathbb E[\text{native duration}]
\le\frac{3}{10b\Lambda}.
\]

A refined moment calculation gives

\[
\mathbb EG^2
\le
\left(
\frac{15b}{2}
+\frac92
-\frac{3}{2(5b+1)}
\right)\Lambda^2.
\]

The total number of coordinates and the global exit rate do not appear.

### Matching lower bounds

Repeating these queries and using a median of means estimates the local rate to absolute error \(\eta\), with failure probability at most \(\delta\), using

\[
O\!\left(
b\Lambda^2\eta^{-2}\log\frac1\delta
\right)
\]

expected endpoint calls and

\[
O\!\left(
\Lambda\eta^{-2}\log\frac1\delta
\right)
\]

total native evolution duration.

I also constructed a family showing that both orders are necessary.

A target transition has unknown rate \(\theta\) only while all \(b-1\) neighbouring bits remain alive. Each neighbour dies at rate \(\Lambda\). The first change disables the unknown rate permanently, so another informative experiment requires restoration of the original checkpoint.

Even observing the entire first-event race gives only

\[

\theta\log(\theta/\theta')-\theta+\theta'
}{
\theta+(b-1)\Lambda
}
\]

KL divergence between two parameter choices. An endpoint at any duration cannot contain more information. Applying the chain rule to adaptive, randomly stopped experiments yields matching lower bounds.

For \(0<\eta\le\Lambda/16\) and \(\delta\le1/4\), the worst-case optimal orders are therefore

\[
\boxed{

=
\Theta\!\left(

and

\[
\boxed{

The upper construction attains both simultaneously.

This is a concrete resource law: a larger dependency neighbourhood requires more short experiments, while the necessary total native evolution duration has the same order. **Checkpoint copying, processing an endpoint and simulating the exterior remain additional costs.**

## 3. Closing the intervention task with positive samples

A rate estimator alone would leave the scientific task unfinished. Moreover, exact signed estimators can acquire exponential variance for strong or long interventions.

I therefore constructed a positive method whose guarantee matches the intended task: the terminal distribution, or the states recorded at a chosen finite observation grid, within a specified error.

Let the unknown native generator have single-coordinate updates,

\[
Q=\sum_iQ_i.
\]

Each update \(Q_i\) reads a known neighbourhood \(N_i\), writes coordinate \(i\), and has total rate at most \(\Lambda_i\).

For a controlled set \(C\), the target changes rates by known bounded factors

\[
r_i^*(x,z)=a_i(x_{A_i},z)r_i(x,z),
\qquad
0\le a_i\le M,
\]

with \(M\ge1\). The factor may depend on local state. It can suppress a transition completely or enhance it.

The additional resource is a **valid local checkpoint edit**: overwrite selected coordinates while preserving every exterior coordinate.

### Two native calls construct one controlled step

For a desired time step \(h\):

**First, evolve the exterior.**

Run the unchanged native system for \(h\), obtaining \(Y\). Restore the controlled coordinates to their starting values. Retain the resulting complete checkpoint \(b\).

**Second, generate controlled proposals through a temporary copy.**

Starting from \(b\), run an independent native continuation for \(Mh\), obtaining \(Z\). For each controlled coordinate whose endpoint changed, accept that net change with probability

\[
\frac{a_i(b_{A_i},Z_i)}{M}.
\]

Patch only the accepted controlled coordinates into the retained checkpoint \(b\). Discard the temporary copy’s exterior.

Every probability is nonnegative. The procedure does not evaluate or estimate a native rate.

### Why hidden-state contamination is bounded

The proof uses bounded-rate Poisson proposal clocks as a mathematical coupling. The algorithm never needs to see those clocks.

An error requires an interacting pair of proposals within the same short interval. For example:

- A temporary controlled change occurs and then influences an exterior update.
- A relevant input changes before a controlled proposal, making an initial-state acceptance rule stale.
- Separating controlled and uncontrolled evolution reverses the order of two updates that do not commute.

The expected number of each ordered pair is proportional to \(h^2\). Unrelated bulk updates do not enter that count.

With explicit constants collected into \(D\), the one-step full-state error is

\[
\operatorname{TV}
\left(
\text{constructed endpoint law},
\text{target endpoint law}
\right)
\le Dh^2.
\]

The report gives every term in \(D\), including incoming dependencies and outgoing influence.

Across a partition of \([0,T]\),

\[
\operatorname{TV}
\left(
\text{joint recorded-state laws}
\right)
\le
D\sum_kh_k^2.
\]

Consequently,

\[
\boxed{
K=\max\!\left\{1,\left\lceil\frac{DT^2}{\varepsilon}\right\rceil\right\}
}
\]

equal steps suffice for error at most \(\varepsilon\), using

\[
\boxed{
2K\text{ native endpoint calls},
\qquad
(1+M)T\text{ total native evolution duration}.
}
\]

This completes a positive finite-error intervention simulator for the stated class, including finite enhancements and complete suppression.

### Two useful cheaper cases

For **suppression only**, one native call per interval suffices. Accept a net controlled change with its prescribed probability; otherwise restore that coordinate. A temporary copy is unnecessary.

For **enhancement after calibration**, a learned local rate table supports ordinary positive Poisson superposition. If the table errors are bounded by \(\rho_i\) and the enhancement factors by \(V_i\), the induced discrepancy is at most

\[
T\sum_iV_i\rho_i.
\]

Deployment then uses native evolution duration \(T\) and expected

\[
1+T\sum_iV_i\Lambda_i
\]

endpoint calls, excluding extra requested observations. The rate-table acquisition cost is paid separately.

These methods have different useful regimes. A few intervention predictions can use the temporary-copy construction without calibration. Repeated predictions may justify acquiring a reusable local table.

### The guarantee’s physical meaning

The result controls terminal states and the complete recorded grid of states. It does not automatically control every unobserved continuous-time event.

For example, a fully suppressed target site never jumps. A native site may jump temporarily and then be restored before the next observation. Those two paths can agree at every recorded time while differing in first-passage events or reaction counts.

The method also needs valid checkpoint and editing operations. It does not supply physical cloning, noninvasive measurements, perfect resets or their thermodynamic cost.

## 5. A foundational boundary: complete passive knowledge can still miss response

The independent starting perspective produced a stronger explanatory result than a generic statement that “hidden variables matter.”

I constructed two reversible switching diffusions with:

- One continuous observed coordinate \(X\) on a circle.
- One unobserved two-state mode.
- Exactly the same complete equilibrium law of the observed trajectory.
- The same observed diffusion coefficient and mobility.
- The same observed equilibrium distribution after every static observed potential.
- The same linear endpoint responses to observed conservative forcing.

Nevertheless, applying the same potential \(\lambda\cos X\) produces different nonlinear transient responses.

### How exact passive equivalence is achieved

Write the first reversible generator in a symmetric representation using

\[
r(x)=
\begin{pmatrix}
1+\tfrac14\cos x\\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

A constant orthogonal rotation of its hidden components,

\[
O=
\begin{pmatrix}
12/13&-5/13\\
5/13&12/13
\end{pmatrix},
\]

produces a second, explicitly verified valid switching diffusion.

Every observed multiplication operator \(f(X)I\) commutes with this rotation. Therefore the rotation cancels from every equilibrium multi-time expectation. This proves equality of **all finite-dimensional observed distributions**, and hence equality of the full continuous observed path law.
