\title{Acquiring exact phase representations\\from dense quadratic data\\
\large Unknown symmetric diagonal completions, sparse errors, and exact counting}
\author{Astra P3 research}\date{27 October 2026}
\begin{document}\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
We give a deterministic representation compiler for a raw alternating
binary matrix. Suppose its off-diagonal entries agree, except for at
most $M$ unordered pairs, with an unknown symmetric matrix of rank at
most $r$. Neither that matrix, its diagonal, its column space, nor the
error locations are supplied. The compiler deletes
$O(\sqrt{rM}+r)$ original coordinates and acquires an exact symmetric
rank-at-most-$r$ completion of the remaining matrix, with at most $M$
off-diagonal errors. Its time is subexponential in the original dimension
whenever $r=o(n)$ and $rM=o(n^2)$; its space is polynomial. It acquires a
column code, deletes supports of short codewords until decoding is
unique, and independently validates the result.

A fourth-root quadratic refinement converts the acquired completion into
at most $2^r$ exactly weighted unary modifications. Combined with the
previous constructive local-phase theorem, conditional on the pinned
OpenAI counting manuscript, this computes a dense quadratic phase
perturbed by $m$ fixed-locality phases in
$\poly(L+n)2^{o(n)}$ time and polynomial space if, additionally,
$\log(2+(m+M)/n)=o(\log n)$. In particular, $M=O(n)$ admits
every $r=o(n)$. The acquisition theorem itself is unconditional.
All representation, arithmetic, reconstruction, and description-size
costs are charged. Executable finite validators check the new compiler
and exact identities; they do not implement the asymptotic
replacement-form solver. Prior exact and approximate matrix algorithms
are compared, including the already-solved error-free diagonal case.
\end{abstract}

\section{Input model and the two results}
All matrix operations until explicitly stated otherwise are over $\F_2$.
A symmetric matrix is \emph{alternating} if its diagonal is zero. Write
$\off(D)=D+\diag(D_{11},\ldots,D_{nn})$. For an alternating matrix $S$,
let $e(S)=|\{(i,j):i<j,\ S_{ij}=1\}|$, counting unordered edges once.
For an alternating $B$, put
\[
q_B(x)=\sum_{i<j}B_{ij}x_ix_j\pmod2.
\]
The matrix is explicitly given, with at most $n^2$ entries. In the
phase problem, $L$ includes this matrix and every index and entry of
every local phase table. Polynomial input-reading and matrix costs
are charged separately from the exponent in $n$.

\begin{theorem}[Acquisition from a raw matrix]\label{thm:acquire}
Let $B$ be an alternating $n$ by $n$ matrix, $n\ge1$. Let $0\le r\le n$,
$0\le M\le\binom n2$, and let $1\le\delta\le n$ be an integer. Define
\[
A_n(t)=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,\lfloor t\rfloor)}\binom nj,\qquad
s_0=\min\!\left(n,\left\lfloor\frac{2M}{\delta}\right\rfloor+r\delta\right),
\quad
s=\min\!\left(n,\left\lfloor\frac{2M}{\delta}\right\rfloor+3r\delta\right).
\]
There is a deterministic algorithm with time
\begin{equation}\label{eq:acqtime}
\poly(n) A_n(r)A_n(s_0)2^r
\end{equation}
and polynomial space which returns either failure or a verified
representation
\[
R=[n]\setminus E,\quad
B[R,R]=\off(C)+S',\quad
C=C^\top,\quad \rank C\le r,\quad e(S')\le M,\quad |E|\le s.
\]
If there exists a symmetric matrix $D$ such that
\[
\rank D\le r,\qquad B=\off(D)+S,\qquad e(S)\le M,
\]
the algorithm necessarily returns a representation. The matrices
$D,S$ and their existence witness are not inputs.
\end{theorem}

This is an exact representation search, not a decision procedure
for the original full-matrix rank-editing promise: an accepted
retained representation can exist even when that promise is false.
Its identity is always checked. No incoherence, bound on the original
maximum error degree, or graph decomposition is assumed.

For $r>0$, choose
\begin{equation}\label{eq:radius}
\delta=\max\left(1,\left\lceil\sqrt{2M/(3r)}\right\rceil\right).
\end{equation}
Then $s\le 2\sqrt{6rM}+3r$. For $r=0$, directly test $e(B)\le M$
and take $C=0,E=\varnothing$. The usual binomial-sum bound gives
\[
\log_2 A_n(t)=O\!\left(t\log_2(en/t)\right)\quad(1\le t\le n/2).
\]
Thus \eqref{eq:acqtime} is $2^{o(n)}\poly(n)$ if
$r=o(n)$ and $rM=o(n^2)$. Enumerating ordered tuples and
using $n^r$ instead would lose this whole parameter regime.

\begin{theorem}[Dense quadratic backgrounds with acquired structure]
\label{thm:count}
Fix integers $k,b\ge1$. Let $\zeta$ be a primitive $2^b$-th root of
unity. The input consists of $B$ as above and $m$ explicit
tables $f_j:\bits^{I_j}\to\Z/2^b\Z$, with $|I_j|\le k$. Let
\[
Z=\sum_{x\in\bits^n}(-1)^{q_B(x)}
       \zeta^{\sum_{j=1}^m f_j(x|_{I_j})}.
\]
Conditional on the local-phase theorem in Section~\ref{sec:phase},
and hence on the specified replacement-form results in
\cite{governing}, there is an exact deterministic algorithm of bit
time $\poly_{k,b}(L+n)2^{o(n)}$ and polynomial space on every family
admitting the promise of Theorem~\ref{thm:acquire} with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:favorable}
r=o(n),\qquad rM=o(n^2),\qquad
\log(2+(m+M)/n)=o(\log n).
\end{equation}
The rank and error budgets can be discovered by dovetailing; the
representation and diagonal are never supplied. In particular,
$M=O(n)$ permits every $r=o(n)$.
\end{theorem}

This does not give a subexponential algorithm for an arbitrary dense
quadratic background plus nonlinear phases. The favorable guarantee
depends on an intrinsic rank-and-sparsity property, but its useful
representation is constructed and checked.

\section{Finding clean information without knowing the errors}
\label{sec:clean}
\begin{lemma}[An unknown clean-column witness]\label{lem:clean}
Under the promise of Theorem~\ref{thm:acquire}, there are sets
$I,E_0\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le r$, $|E_0|\le s_0$, such that
for $R_0=[n]\setminus E_0$,
\[
B[R_0,I]=D[R_0,I],\qquad
\col(D[R_0,R_0])\subseteq\col(B[R_0,I]).
\]
Every column of $(B+D)[R_0,R_0]$ has weight at most $\delta$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
In the hidden error graph let $H=\{i:\deg_S(i)\ge\delta\}$.
Its degree sum is at most $2M$, so
$|H|\le\lfloor2M/\delta\rfloor$. Put $V=[n]\setminus H$.
Choose column indices $I\subseteq V$ forming a basis for
$\col(D[V,V])$; there are at most $r$.
Let $J$ be the union of the $S$-neighbors of $I$ within $V$ and
set $E_0=H\cup J\cup I$. Every vertex in $V$ has error degree
at most $\delta-1$, so $|J\cup I|\le r(\delta-1)+r=r\delta$.
There are no off-diagonal errors between $R_0$ and $I$, and
deleting $I$ removes the unknown diagonal discrepancy from these
columns. Restricting the basis relations on $V$ proves the
column-space inclusion. Remaining off-diagonal errors have
column weight at most $\delta-1$; the unknown diagonal adds
at most one discrepancy per column.
\end{proof}

The basis indices in $I$ may lie in the deleted set; this
construction deliberately deletes them. The algorithm must keep
reading the corresponding \emph{external columns of the original
matrix}. Requiring the basis indices to survive is invalid.
Also the acquired column code may be a proper supercode of the
true retained column space, because principal restriction can
reduce the latter rank. Equality is not required.

\section{Short codewords turn erasures into unique decoding}
\label{sec:prune}
Enumerate all unordered $I\subseteq[n]$ with $|I|\le r$ and
$E_0\subseteq[n]$ with $|E_0|\le s_0$. For each pair, start with
$R=[n]\setminus E_0$ and compute independent generators for
$\mathcal C_R=\col(B[R,I])\subseteq\F_2^R$.
This step sees only $B,I,E_0$ and never accesses the hidden
matrices used in Lemma~\ref{lem:clean}.

\begin{lemma}[Distance by deleting supports]\label{lem:prune}
By deleting at most $2r\delta$ further coordinates, one can make
every nonzero word of the restricted code have weight greater
than $2\delta$. This takes $\poly(n)2^r$ time and polynomial
space. Any sequence of qualifying deletions works.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Enumerate codewords using the current independent generators.
If a nonzero word has weight at most $2\delta$, delete its
support and restrict the generators to the surviving coordinates.
This restriction map has a nonzero kernel, namely the chosen
word, so its code dimension strictly decreases. Recompute a
basis and repeat. There are at most $r$ rounds, each deleting at
most $2\delta$ coordinates. At most $2^r$ words are scanned per
round. Generate them one at a time; storing all words would
violate the polynomial-space claim.
\end{proof}

For the witness in Lemma~\ref{lem:clean}, inclusion of the true
remaining column space in the code survives every restriction.
Each observed remaining column is still within distance $\delta$
of its true column. At termination the code has minimum nonzero
distance greater than $2\delta$, so that true column is its unique
codeword within distance $\delta$.

Decode each column of $B[R,R]$ by scanning the codewords. Reject
if any column has no nearby word. Let $C$ be the decoded matrix.
Independently verify symmetry, rank at most $r$, and that
$S'=B[R,R]+\off(C)$ has at most $M$ unordered edges. The empty
code is allowed; its only word is zero. A wrong seed can fail
these checks or give a different valid representation, but
cannot give a false accepted identity.

The correct witness decodes to $D[R,R]$ and passes. The deletion
bound is $|E|\le\min(n,s_0+2r\delta)=s$.
Each candidate takes $\poly(n)2^r$ time and
polynomial space, including decoding, Gaussian elimination,
and verification. Multiplying by the two unordered-subset counts
proves Theorem~\ref{thm:acquire}. No $2^{\Theta(r^2)}$ search
over inner symmetric coefficient matrices is present.

\section{The diagonal becomes unary fourth-root phases}
\label{sec:refinement}
For even $n$ the alternating matrix $J_n+I_n$ has full rank,
but the symmetric completion $J_n$ has rank one. Thus allowing
the unknown diagonal broadens the former alternating-rank
promise. Diagonal completion alone is established machinery;
Section~\ref{sec:comparison} states the relevant prior algorithm.

Let the acquired code have $d\le r$ independent columns, in a
full-column-rank matrix $U\in\F_2^{R\times d}$. Its space
contains $\col(C)$. Obtain a left inverse $L_U$ by Gaussian
elimination and set $K=L_U C L_U^\top$. Then $K=K^\top$ and
$C=UKU^\top$. These identities are directly checked,
including when $\rank C<d$.

For a symmetric binary matrix $T$, define the $\Z/4\Z$-valued
quadratic refinement
\begin{equation}\label{eq:q4}
Q_T(z)=\sum_jT_{jj}z_j+2\sum_{j<\ell}T_{j\ell}z_jz_\ell\pmod4.
\end{equation}
Its polar form under Boolean addition is
\[
Q_T(x+y)-Q_T(x)-Q_T(y)=2x^\top T y\pmod4.
\]
\begin{lemma}[Exact correction under substitution]\label{lem:refine}
Let $u_i$ be row $i$ of $U$. Define
$\ell_i=(C_{ii}-Q_K(u_i))/2\pmod2$. The numerator is even and
\[
Q_C(y)=Q_K(U^\top y)+2\ell^\top y\pmod4.
\]
Consequently
\begin{equation}\label{eq:signrefine}
(-1)^{q_{\off(C)}(y)}
=i^{Q_K(U^\top y)}
 (-1)^{\ell^\top y}\prod_{j\in R}i^{-C_{jj}y_j}.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The two refinements have the same polar form because $C=UKU^\top$.
Their difference is a homomorphism from the elementary abelian
$2$-group $\F_2^R$ to $\Z/4\Z$, so its values are in $\{0,2\}$.
Evaluation on coordinate vectors gives the correction.
Equivalently $Q_K(u_i)\bmod2=u_i^\top K u_i=C_{ii}$, which
verifies the division by two. Subtracting the diagonal term
from \eqref{eq:q4} proves \eqref{eq:signrefine}.
\end{proof}

Even for an alternating completion, Boolean substitution need
not preserve the quadratic polynomial without a unary correction.
For rows of $U$ equal to $(1,1),(1,0),(0,1)$ and
$K=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$,
the substituted binary quadratic contains an extra $y_1$.
Dropping this correction changes exact answers.

For $a\in\F_2^d$ put
$G_K(a)=\sum_{z\in\F_2^d}i^{Q_K(z)}(-1)^{a^\top z}\in\Z[i]$.
Boolean Fourier inversion gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier}
i^{Q_K(U^\top y)}
=2^{-d}\sum_{a\in\F_2^d}G_K(a)(-1)^{(Ua)^\top y}.
\end{equation}
This replaces the completion by at most $2^r$ exactly weighted
families of unary phases. Recompute and stream the coefficients;
do not retain an exponentially large Fourier table.

\begin{lemma}[Polynomial-cost coefficients]\label{lem:gauss}
Each $G_K(a)$ can be computed in polynomial time and space using
Gaussian integers with $O(d)$ bits.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Write the exponent modulo four as
$\sum_j\alpha_jz_j+2\sum_{j<\ell}\beta_{j\ell}z_jz_\ell$,
where $\alpha_j=K_{jj}+2a_j$. If $\alpha_j$ is odd, let
$L(z)=\bigoplus_{\ell\ne j:\beta_{j\ell}=1}z_\ell$. Then
\[
1+i^{\alpha_j}(-1)^{L(z)}
=(1+i^{\alpha_j})i^{-\alpha_j L(z)}.
\]
Modulo four, a Boolean parity is the sum of its variables minus
twice their pairwise products. The remaining exponent stays
quadratic: subtract $\alpha_j$ from each neighbor's unary
coefficient and toggle each pair of neighbors' quadratic
coefficients. Multiply an accumulated Gaussian integer by
$1+i^{\alpha_j}$ and eliminate $z_j$. After at most $d$ steps,
all unary coefficients are even. The remaining sum is an
ordinary binary quadratic Gauss sum, evaluated by eliminating
coupled variable pairs and checking the remaining linear form.
This takes polynomially many field and integer operations.
All partial factors and sums have $O(d)$ bits.
\end{proof}

\section{The constructive local-phase dependency}\label{sec:phase}
The only non-elementary counting dependency is the companion
local-phase construction \cite{phase}. It applies to $u$ tables
of fixed locality $\kappa$ and fixed $2$-power root order on
$n$ independent Boolean inputs, with unrestricted interaction
graph. Its exact phase sum takes
$\poly_{\kappa,b}(L+n)2^{F(n,u)}$ time and polynomial space,
where $F(n,u)=o(n)$ if $\log(2+u/n)=o(\log n)$. More explicitly,
set
\[
h=\log_2(2+\kappa u/n)+O_{\kappa,b}(\log\log n),
\qquad a=h/\log_2n.
\]
In the useful range $a\to0$, one may take
\begin{equation}\label{eq:phaseexp}
F(n,u)=O_{\kappa,b}\!\left(
n\sqrt a\, [\log_2(2/a)]^{3/2}\right).
\end{equation}
Outside its guarded useful range the solver enumerates original
inputs. The companion proof gives explicit guards and parameters.

This dependency does not assume small shared summaries.
Multilinearly interpolate each phase-valued table $\zeta^{f_j}$ to $G_j$ over cyclotomic
integers. For $\pi=\zeta-1$, each coefficient of $G_j-1$
belongs to $(\pi)$. At root order $2^b$, put $d_0=2^{b-1}$;
then $\pi^{d_0}\in2\Z[\zeta]$. The retained product
\[
\prod_j(1+V(G_j-1))
\quad\hbox{in}\quad
\Q(\zeta)[V]/(V^{d_0(n+2)})
\]
has incidence degree $O_{\kappa,b}(n)$ independently of $u$,
and omitted orders vanish modulo $2^{n+2}$. Each coordinate
of the exact original sum lies between $-2^n$ and $2^n$,
so those residues determine it.

High-incidence variables are branched on at cost $2^{o(n)}$;
remaining incidence is $n^{o(1)}$. Lemma 4.7 of
\cite{governing} manufactures occurrence-specific replacement
forms, variable groups, and shared integer measurement matrices.
Proposition 3.4 reconstructs the retained polynomial from its
$O(n)$ incidence-degree budget. Density-sensitive choices
give \eqref{eq:phaseexp}; rational bit costs are controlled
as required in Appendix B of the source. Apply the
coefficient-sum map in $V$ only \emph{after} exact
reconstruction: setting $V=1$ is not a homomorphism on this
truncated algebra.

The new compiler therefore does not invoke an unspecified
arbitrary-weight sum oracle or divide modular residues.
It obtains every exact inner cyclotomic integer before the
divisions below.

\section{Original-input reconstruction and complete costs}
\label{sec:cost}
Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:acquire}. For each assignment $e$ to
$E$, write $x=(e,y)$ with $y$ ranging over the remaining
original variables. Dense quadratic interactions incident
to $E$ become a known constant and unary phases computed
from $B$. Restrict every original table by the same assignment;
locality cannot increase. The sparse matrix $S'$ adds at most
$M$ quadratic tables.

Use \eqref{eq:signrefine} and \eqref{eq:fourier} for $C$.
For each $a\in\F_2^d$, the inner sum contains the restricted
original tables, the $M$ sparse quadratic tables, and at most
$|R|$ unary tables, combining all diagonal, substitution,
character, and branch corrections. Set $b'=\max(b,2)$ and
embed the original phases into the $2^{b'}$-th roots.
Locality is fixed at $\kappa=\max(k,2)$ and the root order
is fixed.

To use the original $n$ uniformly in the quantitative bound,
pad each inner problem with $|E|$ unused Boolean variables.
Let $T_{e,a}$ be its \emph{exact} sum, including the branch's
constant phase. It is $2^{|E|}$ times the unpadded sum, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:finalreconstruction}
Z=\sum_{e\in\F_2^E}
  2^{-(d+|E|)}\sum_{a\in\F_2^d}G_K(a)T_{e,a}.
\end{equation}
The unpadded finite validator instead divides by $2^d$.
Assignments to $E$ are disjoint original-input branches,
with no averaging over them.

Each inner coordinate is bounded by $2^n$ and reconstructed
with the original precision $n+2$ before forming a numerator.
The Gaussian coefficients have coordinates bounded by $2^d$.
A streamed numerator has $O(n+r)$ bits per coordinate.
The denominator in \eqref{eq:finalreconstruction} divides
every coordinate exactly in the fixed integral cyclotomic
basis. Only then is the quotient added to the outer
accumulator. Divisibility can be checked.

Writing $u=m+M+O(n)$, the full bound is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total}
\poly_{k,b}(L+n)
\left[
A_n(r)A_n(s_0)2^r+
2^{s+r+F(n,u)}
\right].
\end{equation}
Acquisition is paid once, so it is added to the counter work.
The coefficient computations, table transformations, and exact
arithmetic are included. Input storage, polynomially many
matrices, one branch, one codeword, one Gaussian coefficient,
one inner-solver state, and exact accumulators suffice.
This proves polynomial space and Theorem~\ref{thm:count}.

\paragraph{No supplied favorable budgets.}
Dovetail the full acquisition-and-counting pipelines over
$r\in[0,n]$, $M\in[0,\binom n2]$, $\delta\in[1,n]$,
and direct enumeration. There are polynomially many
polynomial-space states. All accepted outputs are exact,
and the first completed answer takes at most polynomial
overhead relative to the best successful budget. An
uncontrolled sequential search would not prove this claim.

\section{A program-level consequence with all states charged}
\label{sec:program}
Consider a Boolean straight-line program of description size $L$
with $n$ independent inputs, arbitrary affine operations over
$\F_2$, $t$ two-input AND operations, and one requested output
bit. Eliminate affine intermediate states by symbolic linear
propagation. Introduce a formal result bit $u_j$ for each AND,
so its operands become computed affine forms
$a_j(x,u_{<j}),b_j(x,u_{<j})$. The output is an affine form
$o(x,u)$. This costs polynomial time in $L+n+t$ using vectors
of length $n+t+1$, without enumerating input assignments.

Introduce operand bits $v_j,w_j$, character bits
$\alpha_j,\beta_j,\lambda_j$, and one output character $\mu$.
For a requested value $\tau$, character orthogonality gives
\begin{align}
\#\{x:o(x)=\tau\}
&=2^{-(3t+1)}
 \sum_{x,u,v,w,\alpha,\beta,\lambda,\mu}
 (-1)^{P_{\rm prog}},\label{eq:program}\\
P_{\rm prog}
&=\sum_{j=1}^t
 \bigl[\alpha_j(v_j+a_j)+\beta_j(w_j+b_j)
       +\lambda_j(u_j+v_jw_j)\bigr]
 +\mu(o+\tau).\nonumber
\end{align}
There are exactly $N=n+6t+1$ free phase variables and exactly
$t$ cubic monomials $\lambda_jv_jw_j$. Everything else is
quadratic or unary. Construct the raw quadratic matrix by
expanding the computed affine forms, in polynomial time.
Each character enforces one constraint with a factor two;
acyclicity makes all gate and operand bits unique for each
$x$. This proves the exact normalization.

If $t=O(n)$ and this \emph{computed} $N$-dimensional matrix
admits the conditions of Theorem~\ref{thm:count}, the requested
count takes $\poly(L+n)2^{o(n)}$ time and polynomial space,
even with many more affine intermediate states than inputs.
The final division by $2^{3t+1}$ occurs after the exact phase
sum. Those conditions are not guaranteed for every program.
The useful representation is acquired from the raw computed
matrix, and the cost is measured against the original inputs.

\section{Comparison, decisive failures, and finite execution}
\label{sec:comparison}
\paragraph{A genuine exact prior competitor.}
Fomin, Golovach, and Panolan \cite{fgp}, Theorem 6, solve full
binary GF(2) rank-$r$ approximation within $k_0$ changed entries
in time $2^{O(r\sqrt{k_0\log(rk_0)})}n^2$.
For an alternating completion $k_0=2M$; with an unknown
diagonal one can use $k_0=2M+n$. Their output need not be
symmetric, but a polynomial support-cover repair deletes
$O(\sqrt{rk_0}+r)$ coordinates and makes it symmetric.
The retained residual has at most $k_0/2$ unordered
off-diagonal errors, still $O(M+n)$. The accompanying
audit proves and executes this repair.

For $M=O(n)$ the displayed exact bound guarantees
subexponential acquisition, for example, when
$r=o(\sqrt{n/\log n})$. It does not imply the whole
$r=o(n)$ regime of \eqref{eq:total}. Our output task is
weaker than full-matrix editing because it allows an acquired
coordinate deletion. This is not an unqualified improvement
to their full rigidity algorithm or a claim of first priority.

The GF(2) bicriteria algorithm of Velingker et al.\
\cite{velingker} has time
$2^{\poly(r/\varepsilon)}\poly(n)$ and output rank
$O(r\log n/\varepsilon)$. Its approximation error can be
retained as exact sparse phases; the label approximate
does not disqualify it. Its stated dependencies do not
supply the full sublinear-rank guarantee here. Basis-column
and codeword enumeration also have relevant ancestry in
incidence-matrix recovery \cite{incidence}; that result uses
a different residual class and $(mn)^{O(r)}$ time.

\paragraph{The error-free diagonal case is already solved.}
Kogan \cite{kogan}, Theorem 2 and Remark 3, constructs a
diagonal completion of rank at most twice the minimum
in $O(n^4)$ time. It already suffices for subexponential
latent expansion when $M=0$ and the minimum rank is $o(n)$.
That algorithm belongs in an equally informed portfolio.
The complete-graph example above illustrates scope, not
newness. The substantive acquisition result here combines
unknown sparse \emph{off-diagonal} corruption with low rank
and an unknown diagonal, while preserving a sparse residual.

\paragraph{Why deleting to low rank is inadequate.}
Take a perfect-matching adjacency matrix $S$ and an
alternating $D$ of rank $r$. Then
$\rank(D+S)\ge n-r$. Deleting $s$ coordinates can reduce
rank by at most $2s$, so making the raw retained matrix
rank at most $r$ requires $s\ge(n-2r)/2$.
Even free diagonal completion does not cure the example:
each matching block with any diagonal has rank at least one,
so $\min_v\rank(D+S+\diag v)\ge n/2-r$.
The present compiler keeps the sparse residual and counts it
exactly, avoiding that linear deletion or completion rank.

\paragraph{What failed, and what repaired it.}
Searching for an inner coefficient matrix costs
$2^{\Theta(r^2)}$ and loses large sublinear ranks; distance
pruning removes that search. Requiring an alternating
low-rank component excludes useful diagonal completions;
the fourth-root refinement incorporates them. Requiring
pivot coordinates to survive, or dropping the unary
substitution correction, gives incorrect algorithms.
Executable audits include these counterexamples.

These repairs do not remove the general dense quadratic
obstruction. A separated-variable representation of
$(-1)^{x^\top By}$ has rational matrix rank
$2^{\rank_{\F_2} B}$, so generic full cross-rank requires
exponentially many terms in that architecture. Eliminating
a Gaussian block can also densify nonlinear interactions:
\[
\sum_{u,v\in\F_2^t}
(-1)^{u^\top Av+u^\top f(x)+v^\top g(x)}
=2^t(-1)^{g(x)^\top A^{-1}f(x)}
\]
for invertible $A$. Local quadratic $f,g$ can yield
$\Theta(t^2)$ quartic terms. These are limitations of the
examined mechanisms, not lower bounds on all algorithms.
Pure quadratic sums, without a nonlinear or non-Clifford
local foreground, are already handled by Gaussian elimination.

\paragraph{Executed finite evidence.}
The root program \texttt{diagonal\_completion\_compiler.py}
receives only the raw matrix and search budgets. Hidden
matrices generate fixtures but are not passed to search.
It passed 16,933 exact fourth-root Gaussian checks, 5,088

\section{A strict family with exponential balanced-cut rank}
\label{sec:strict}
The larger rank range is not merely a redundant way to
describe a smaller-rank or small-width family.

\begin{lemma}[Support cover with a free diagonal]\label{lem:cover}
If a symmetric binary matrix $T$ has rank $q$ and at most
$W$ off-diagonal edges, one can find $E$ with
$T[R,R]=0$, $R=[n]\setminus E$, and
\[
|E|\le\left\lfloor\frac{2W}{\tau}\right\rfloor+q\tau
\quad\hbox{for every integer }\tau\ge1.
\]
In particular $|E|\le 2\sqrt{2qW}+q$ for $q>0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Delete vertices of off-diagonal degree at least $\tau$.
Every remaining row has support size at most $\tau$,
including its possible diagonal entry. Choose actual
rows forming a basis, and delete the union of their
supports. It contains every nonzero column, since every
row is a linear combination of these basis rows.
There are at most $q$ basis rows. Optimize with
$\tau=\max(1,\lceil\sqrt{2W/q}\rceil)$; if $q=0$,
the matrix is zero.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[A family outside the simpler conditions]
\label{prop:strict}
Fix $0<\alpha<1$ and let
$r=2\lfloor n^\alpha/2\rfloor$. There are Boolean cubic
polynomials $p_n=P_n+q_{B_n}$ with $n/3$ disjoint cubic
monomials in $P_n$, and raw quadratic descriptions of
$\Theta(n^2)$ entries, such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $B_n=A_n+S_n$, with $A_n$ alternating of rank $r$
and $e(S_n)=O(n)$, so Theorem~\ref{thm:count} applies
without receiving this decomposition.
\item $B_n$ has $\Omega(n^2)$ edges and every balanced
original-coordinate cut has binary cut rank $\Omega(n)$.
The full phase $(-1)^{p_n}$ has matrix flattening rank
$2^{\Omega(n)}$ over $\Q$ or $\mathbb C$ across every
such cut.
\item No symmetric component of rank below $r$, even
with arbitrary diagonal, approximates its off-diagonal
matrix within $n^{1+o(1)}$ edge errors. In particular,
it cannot be reclassified into the smaller
$n^{o(1)}$-rank, near-linear-error regime by choosing
another decomposition.
\end{enumerate}
These are separations between representation conditions,
not general counting lower bounds.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Let $J_r$ be the standard invertible alternating matrix,
and choose $U\in\F_2^{n\times r}$ whose column code has
minimum distance at least $n/4$ and for which
$A=UJ_rU^\top$ has $\Omega(n^2)$ edges.
Such a choice exists: for independent uniformly random
rows of $U$, the distance fails with probability at most
$(2^r-1)e^{-n/8}=o(1)$. To check density without assuming
independent matrix entries, write $t=2^{-r}$,
$N=\binom n2$, and $W=e(A)$. A direct covariance
calculation gives
\[
\mathbb EW=N(1-t)/2,\qquad
\operatorname{Var}W=
N(1-t^2)/4+\tfrac32\binom n3 t(1-t).
\]
Chebyshev's inequality gives
$\Pr(W<N/4)=O(n^{-2}+t/n)=o(1)$.
Both desired events therefore hold simultaneously.
Positive code distance implies $\rank A=r$.

Take $S$ from a simple constant-degree expander family,
with maximum degree $D$ and positive constant edge
expansion $h$; such explicit families are available
from \cite{expander}. Loops can be discarded and
bounded parallel multiplicities collapsed without
losing constant expansion. Work at sizes divisible by
three. Choose a partition into triples with only
$O(1)$ edges of $S$ internal to triples: a uniform
random partition has expected internal count
$2e(S)/(n-1)=O(1)$, so such a partition exists.
Let $P$ be the sum of the triples' cubic monomials.

For any cut with both parts of size at least $n/3$,
there are at least $hn/3$ crossing expander edges.
Discard the $O(1)$ edges within triples. Greedily
choose an induced crossing matching, never using
two vertices from the same triple. Selecting an
edge invalidates at most $O(D^2+D)$ remaining edges,
so the matching has $\ell=\Omega(n)$ edges.
Its cross submatrix of $S$ is $I_\ell$; hence the
corresponding submatrix of $B$ has rank at least
$\ell-\rank A=\Omega(n)$.

In the full phase flattening, fix every variable
outside these matching endpoints to zero. All cubic
monomials vanish because each triple retains at most
one variable. Quadratic terms confined to a side
only multiply rows or columns by signs. The remaining
cross character matrix has rank
$2^{\rank(I_\ell+A_{\rm cross})}\ge2^{\ell-r}$.
For completeness, a character matrix
$((-1)^{x^\top Hy})_{x,y}$ has exactly
$2^{\rank H}$ distinct orthogonal row characters.
This proves the claimed flattening rank.
Adding $O(n)$ sparse edges to dense $A$ preserves
$\Omega(n^2)$ edge density.

Finally suppose a symmetric $D'$ has rank $r'<r$
and off-diagonal error $M'=n^{1+o(1)}$ from $B$.
Then $T=A+D'$ has rank at most $r+r'$ and at most
$e(S)+M'$ off-diagonal edges. Lemma~\ref{lem:cover}
deletes only
$O(\sqrt{r(e(S)+M')}+r)=o(n)$ coordinates to make
$T[R,R]=0$. Removing fewer than the code distance
leaves $U_R$ of full column rank, so
$\rank A[R,R]=r$. But $A[R,R]=D'[R,R]$ has rank
at most $r'<r$, a contradiction.
\end{proof}

The cubic third-derivative tensor in this family is a
direct sum of three-dimensional volume forms. Its
primitive centroid blocks are those triples: a
centroid map cannot mix two blocks by nondegeneracy,
and the centroid of one volume form is scalar.
There are $\Omega(n^2)$ nonzero quadratic couplings
between the blocks, since each block pair contains
at most nine edges. Within-block changes of basis
cannot turn a nonzero cross matrix into zero.
Thus the preceding acquired-cubic-block method's
near-linear coupling criterion also fails here.

The support-cover proof was checked on every symmetric
matrix through dimension five: 168,146 threshold checks.
Separate programs checked 2,799 finite balanced cuts,
2,211 restricted full-phase character-rank identities,
and all 8,192 rank-zero/rank-one symmetric competitors
for a finite rank-two fixture. A larger exact certificate
has $n=4,098$, core rank six, code distance 1,993,
16,377 sparse edges, and 4,146,143 observed edges.
Any rank-below-six symmetric competitor with at most
$4n$ edge errors would imply a support cover of at
most 1,698 vertices, contradicting that distance.
The larger sampled sparse graph is not claimed to
be an expander; its certificate concerns rigidity only.
