## 1. The operational quantity

A detector \(E=(E_i)\) acts on a finite-dimensional system \(A\) and outputs a classical label. The input description explicitly lists the matrices \(E_i\), whose real and imaginary entries are rational, with

\[
E_i>0,\qquad \sum_iE_i=I.
\]

An experiment prepares one of two faithful states \(\rho_{AR},\sigma_{AR}\), applies the detector to \(A\), and retains the finite quantum reference \(R\). Both candidate detectors are evaluated on the same hypotheses and reference.

For output states \(X,Y\), define the quantum Chernoff distance in bits,

\[
C(X,Y)
=
-\log_2\min_{0\le s\le1}\operatorname{Tr}X^sY^{1-s}.
\]

For independent repetitions, this is the optimal asymptotic exponent of the equal-prior decision error. The operational identification comes from the quantum Chernoff theorem. :chatgpt-content-reference{index="0"}

The full benchmark is

\[
V_C(P,Q)
=
\sup_{\substack{\dim R<\infty\\ \rho,\sigma>0}}
\left[
C(Q_R\rho,Q_R\sigma)
-
C(P_R\rho,P_R\sigma)
\right],
\]

where \(E_R=E\otimes\mathrm{id}_R\).

Thus \(V_C(P,Q)\) measures the greatest improvement in binary-decision exponent obtainable by replacing \(P\) with \(Q\), allowing any finite retained reference.

A particularly useful subclass is governed by the square-root affinity

\[
a_E(\rho,\sigma)
=
\operatorname{Tr}
\sqrt{E_R\rho}\sqrt{E_R\sigma},
\]

and the benchmark

\[
V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)
=
\sup_{\substack{\dim R<\infty\\ \rho,\sigma>0}}
\log_2\frac{a_P(\rho,\sigma)}{a_Q(\rho,\sigma)}.
\]

This subclass has a direct physical realization. Add one retained fair classical bit and prepare

\[
\widetilde\rho=\frac12(\rho\oplus\sigma),
\qquad
\widetilde\sigma=\frac12(\sigma\oplus\rho).
\]

The Chernoff moment of this pair is convex and symmetric about \(s=1/2\), so its minimum occurs there. Consequently,

\[
C(E_R\widetilde\rho,E_R\widetilde\sigma)
=
-\log_2 a_E(\rho,\sigma).
\]

The fair-bit construction therefore realizes \(V_{\mathrm{bal}}\) as an actual discrimination-exponent advantage. It gives

\[
V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)\le V_C(P,Q).
\]

I do not assume their numerical values are equal.

### The quantities remain finite

Allowing different reference marginals in the two hypotheses might initially suggest that the reference could make these quantities infinite. Strict positivity of the detector effects prevents that.

Choose computable rational floors \(E_i\ge\ell_i I\) and set \(L_E=\sum_i\ell_i>0\). The proof establishes

\[
L_E Q_s(\rho_R,\sigma_R)
\le
Q_s(E_R\rho,E_R\sigma)
\le
Q_s(\rho_R,\sigma_R),
\]

where \(Q_s(X,Y)=\operatorname{Tr}X^sY^{1-s}\). The common reference contribution cancels when the two detectors are compared, yielding

\[
\boxed{
0\le V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)
\le V_C(P,Q)
\le-\log_2 L_Q.
}
\]

The impossibility theorem concerns bounded, finite operational quantities.

## 2. The resolved computability boundary

The main theorem has four parts.

| Problem | Result |
|---|---|
| Approximate \(V_C(P,Q)\) within \(1/4\) bit for every valid finite rational detector pair | No total algorithm |
| Approximate \(V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)\) within \(1/4\) bit for every such pair | No total algorithm |
| Compute \(V_{\mathrm{bal},r}(P,Q)\) at a specified finite reference dimension \(r\) | Computable to arbitrary accuracy |
| Compute a reference-memory budget sufficient to approximate \(V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)\) within \(1/4\) bit | No total algorithm |

The finite-reference statement is stronger than a numerical optimization prescription:

\[
\boxed{
2^{V_{\mathrm{bal},r}(P,Q)}
\text{ is a uniformly computable real algebraic number.}
}
\]

Its logarithm is computable, although it need not itself be algebraic.

The memory theorem rules out any total computable function \(D(P,Q)\) satisfying

\[
V_{\mathrm{bal}}(P,Q)
-
\max_{1\le r\le D(P,Q)}
V_{\mathrm{bal},r}(P,Q)
\le\frac14
\]

for every valid detector pair. It also rules out a sufficient reference-qubit budget and a sufficient budget depending only on the detector-description length.

For each individual pair, some finite reference achieves any prescribed positive approximation tolerance: that follows from the definition of the supremum. At each finite description length, there are only finitely many encoded detector pairs, so their worst-case required memory is finite. **Those finite worst-case requirements have no total computable upper envelope.**

That is the foundational consequence: every specified finite-memory benchmark can be evaluated, while universally deciding how far the memory search must go is impossible—even with a fixed nonzero tolerance.

### A rational filter concentrates the affinity Hessian

The partial-fraction residues, auxiliary matrices, and normalization are all explicit rational expressions. The required filter size is fixed by

\[
m=

\frac{432\kappa^2h^4}{d_0^2}

The negative-affinity Hessian becomes a weighted average of kernels

The normalized filter density concentrates near \(t=1\), where the kernel gives the SLD quadratic form. Its concentration is controlled through an exact beta-prime moment calculation. The resulting error in the difference of the two normalized Hessians is at most \(d_0/2\).

Together with the resolvent derivative bounds for the square root, these factors cancel the dependence on \(\mu\). Both the smoothing loss and the third-derivative remainder are then bounded using only computable source and auxiliary parameters.

Here \(W,L,\sigma_\zeta,Z\) are explicitly constructed auxiliary objects. Input positivity is checked separately from output estimates.

## 5. A finite experiment with a predetermined repetition count

An asymptotic exponent advantage does not by itself specify when a finite experiment exhibits the advantage. I resolved that operation separately.

For a faithful swap-balanced output pair \(A,B\), let

\[
x=\operatorname{Tr}\sqrt A\sqrt B.
\]

The new finite-copy bound is

\[
\boxed{
\frac{x^n}{4}
\exp\!\left[-\sqrt n\,\operatorname{arcosh}(1/x)\right]
\le e_n(A,B)
\le\frac{x^n}{2}.
}
\]

Here \(e_n\) is the optimal equal-prior error after \(n\) independent copies.

The proof uses the Nussbaum–Szkoła classical distributions associated with the two quantum states. Swap balance makes the tilted log-likelihood have mean zero. Its exponential moment controls its variance through

\[
\mathbb E\cosh(L/2)=1/x,
\qquad
\mathbb E L^2
\le4\operatorname{arcosh}(1/x)^2.
\]

The finite quantum/classical comparison then gives the lower bound. The upper bound is the half-order quantum Chernoff inequality. b_\varepsilon

## 6. Robustness and actual implementations

The obstruction survives specified perturbations of valid detectors.

If corresponding effects satisfy

\[
(1-\delta)E_i
\le E_i'
\le(1+\delta)E_i,
\qquad 0\le\delta<1,
\]

then, for either benchmark,

\[
\boxed{
|V(P',Q')-V(P,Q)|
\le
\log_2\frac{1+\delta}{1-\delta}.
}
\]

At \(\delta=1/16\), this is less than \(1/5\) bit. The compiled zero-versus-one family therefore retains a less-than-\(1/5\) versus greater-than-\(4/5\) separation in computably specified neighborhoods of valid detectors. Additive-\(1/4\) outputs still separate those neighborhoods.

For \(k\) independent primitive implementations, a sufficient relative effect tolerance is

\[
\delta_{\mathrm{base}}=\frac1{16k}.
\]

The independence condition matters: a one-call calibration certificate does not control arbitrary correlated memory errors across repetitions.

Preparation tolerances are explicit as well. An affinity gap \(\varepsilon\) survives at least as \(\varepsilon/2\) if each underlying hypothesis has trace-norm preparation error at most

\[

and each detector has relative effect error at most \(\varepsilon/8\). Applying the exact fair-bit swap to the prepared underlying pair preserves the symmetry required by the finite-copy bound. The predetermined count becomes \(n_{\varepsilon/2}\).
