# A2 — A civilization-scale materials capability

**Status (12 October 2026): one live research lead, no verified breakthrough.** The broad “predict synthesis kinetics” idea is already substantially advanced: a 2026 *Nature Materials* paper couples atomistic transport estimates to a solid-state reaction simulator for Ba–Ti–O. The potentially important open boundary is whether that mechanism can be made predictive across redox-active, variable-valence materials and realistic precursor microstructures, with no per-target experimental fitting. Treat this as an open, likely incremental research direction until it passes a blind transfer test.

## North star and exact question

The North Star is a materials designer that accepts a desired measured function, a target phase or structure, available precursors, equipment limits, and a finite time/cost budget, then returns a synthesis protocol with a calibrated probability of producing enough of the target phase for the measured property to land in a specified interval. The transformative capability would be a reliable design-to-recipe-to-measured-material loop across unfamiliar chemical families. A predicted crystal or a successful powder synthesis alone does not establish the target function, device performance, or manufacturing readiness. This matters because practical access to better batteries, catalysts, separations, and electronic materials depends on reaching the needed phase and microstructure by a reproducible route; the physical property and device benefit still require independent measurement.

The narrow research question worth testing is:

> Can a thermodynamically consistent, charge-aware reactive-transport model predict the time-resolved phase sequence and final target yield for a redox-active inorganic synthesis in a held-out precursor/thermal protocol, using atomistic transport calculations plus measured initial powder geometry, without fitting reaction rates to that held-out experiment?

This is deliberately narrower than a universal “synthesizability” oracle. The operational outcome is a distribution over time-dependent phase fractions, oxidation states, and target yield under a specified temperature and oxygen-fugacity history. The longer-term interface can add a measured-property constraint after the phase-path predictor survives.

A simple selection counterexample also weakens property-conditioned generation as a stand-alone answer. Suppose \hat{p}_i = p_i + \epsilon_i with independent \hat{p} errors of standard deviation \(\sigma\), and choose the candidate with the largest predicted value among \(n\) samples. Even if all true values \(p_i\) are equal, the selected prediction has a positive error of order \(\sigma\sqrt{2\log n}\). Generating more candidates can therefore improve the displayed maximum while leaving the measured property unchanged. The missing evidence is calibrated uncertainty plus prospective synthesis and independent property measurement, not another high predicted score.

## Strongest lead: the exact missing representation

The current systems show why this deserves a narrow attack. ARROWS³ searches stoichiometrically valid precursor sets and temperatures, observes pairwise intermediates, and updates rankings by the remaining reaction free energy. It benchmarked 188 experiments for YBa₂Cu₃O₆.₅ and reported that it found all ten best procedures while sampling less than half the route space; it also guided synthesis of two metastable targets. Its authors explicitly leave quantitative diffusion and nucleation kinetics open. The A-Lab demonstrated fast closed-loop synthesis, but its inputs and recipe ranking also lean on thermodynamics and short endpoint experiments. The corrected Nature record confirms 36 of 40 reported successes, leaves four inconclusive, and clarifies that “new” meant new to the prediction platform, not necessarily new to science. These are process results, not proof of a novel-material discovery engine.

The latest major advance is Karan et al. (2026): an ACE machine-learned potential supplies ion-transport/Onsager coefficients, including cross-ion correlations, to a cellular automaton of Ba–Ti–O powder reactions. The model predicts phase evolution for different precursor ratios and temperature histories, and shows that adding transport changes predictions relative to thermodynamics alone. Its authors bound the formulation: Ba, Ti, and O have fixed oxidation states in their study; the approximation fails when oxidation states vary or substantial charge transfer occurs. They also assume well-mixed, uniformly sized particles, assigning every precursor a radius of 0.1 µm. This is a real advance over “kinetics are missing,” so that slogan is no longer novel. The open candidate is transfer beyond this fixed-valence, idealized geometry regime.

The state required for that extension is not just a list of phase energies. At minimum, it must track local phase/composition fields (c_i^\alpha(\mathbf r,t)), redox/charge state, the oxygen or other chemical reservoir, contact geometry and particle-size distribution, temperature, and the reaction interfaces. Multicomponent fluxes need electrochemical-potential gradients, schematically

\[
J_i=-\sum_j L_{ij}\nabla\widetilde\mu_j,\qquad
\widetilde\mu_j=\mu_j+z_j e\phi,
\]

with charge/mass balance and reservoir exchange imposed consistently. Reaction and nucleation rates at phase boundaries then couple these fluxes to the evolving microstructure. The 2026 work supplies part of this object for a fixed-valence system; the transfer and identifiability of the charge-aware, morphology-conditioned object are not established here.

### Toy-model counterexample: hull stability cannot certify finite-time synthesis

Take two phases, precursor A and target P, with reversible transitions in both directions. Let the target free energy be δ below the precursor, and write β = 1/(kBT). Detailed balance requires only

\[
\frac{k_{A\to P}}{k_{P\to A}}=e^{\beta\delta}=:r.
\]

For every \(\kappa>0\), the rates

\[
k_{A\to P}=\kappa\frac{r}{1+r},\qquad
k_{P\to A}=\kappa\frac{1}{1+r}
\]

have the same free-energy difference and the same equilibrium target fraction \(\pi_P=r/(1+r)\). Starting from (A), however,

\[
P_P(t)=\frac{r}{1+r}(1-e^{-\kappa t}).
\]

At 1000 K, choose δ = 0.10 eV, giving an equilibrium target fraction of about 0.76. After a four-hour hold, κ = 1 h⁻¹ gives target probability about 0.75, while κ = 10⁻⁶ h⁻¹ gives about 3.0 × 10⁻⁶. Both models have exactly the same endpoint thermodynamics and detailed balance. In transition-state theory, raising the forward and reverse barriers together preserves that ratio while changing κ exponentially. A barrier shift of about 1.2 eV at this temperature changes rates by roughly six orders of magnitude.

This is an exact counterexample to the inference “stable on the hull, therefore practically synthesizable by this finite protocol.” It is an internal derivation of standard reversible kinetics, **not** a new theorem or scientific result. It pinpoints the minimum missing information: rate/transport bounds under stated synthesis conditions. It does not imply that all materials synthesis is a two-state Markov process.

### Evidence status

- **Known:** property-conditioned crystal generation, composition-based synthesizability scoring, thermodynamic precursor ranking, active robotic synthesis, and a Ba–Ti–O transport-aware phase simulator all have published primary-source demonstrations.
- **Internally derived:** the reversible two-state calculation above proves that identical equilibrium energies can coexist with arbitrarily different finite-time yields; the top-score selection example explains why maximizing an uncalibrated property prediction can inflate apparent performance.
- **Falsified:** “predicting synthesis kinetics” as a novel umbrella claim is already stale after Karan et al. (2026). The stronger claim that hull stability alone predicts a finite-time recipe is directly refuted by the toy model and contradicted empirically by the published kinetic/selectivity work.
- **Open:** whether charge-aware reactive transport, with measured particle/contact geometry and no per-route rate fitting, transfers to a redox-active chemistry and predicts held-out phase trajectories better than equally resourced baselines. No result here establishes that it does.

## Actual first investigation

Use the published redox-active Na₀.₆₇CoO₂ synthesis as the first transfer challenge. Bianchini et al. used in-situ synchrotron XRD to report a multi-stage route through metastable O3, O3′, and P3 phases before equilibrium P2. The study varied precursor choice and heating rate; CoO and Co₃O₄ routes provide distinct starting oxidation states. This is a sharper test than replaying another fixed-valence Ba–Ti–O trajectory, and it has phase evolution measured over time.

1. **Freeze the interface and split before fitting.** Recover the published diffraction patterns, time/temperature records, precursor details, and thermodynamic inputs. Hold out one complete precursor-plus-heating history. Do not use its phase sequence or final fractions to set rate parameters. Record missing measurements and uncertainty instead of imputing them as facts.
2. **Run the cheapest falsifier first.** Implement three predictors: (i) convex-hull/reaction-ΔG ranking, (ii) a diffusion/transport model with self terms but no cross-ion correlations or redox transport, and (iii) the proposed charge-aware model. Predict the held-out phase order, onset temperature/time, and phase fractions. If the proposed state cannot even be instantiated from the available records, or only works after fitting that held-out trajectory, stop before paying for new beamtime.
3. **Then make a prospective, fixed-budget test.** Fix one target and atmosphere. Compare two precursor routes (Na₂O₂ + CoO; Na₂O₂ + Co₃O₄) and two heating schedules (fast and slow), with three independently prepared batches per cell: 12 samples total. Freeze predictions before synthesis. Measure time-resolved phase fractions by in-situ high-energy XRD; use Co-edge absorption at selected interruptions plus final composition/oxygen-stoichiometry checks to distinguish a correct phase sequence from a compensating diffraction fit. Record particle-size distributions, mixing/milling history, ramp, dwell, atmosphere, mass changes, and all failed runs.
4. **Compare at equal information and cost.** Give each baseline the same phase/thermodynamic data, experimental outcomes, and total compute/experiment budget; debit every new DFT reference, potential-training run, and MD trajectory to the full model. Score held-out log likelihood for phase identity/fraction, onset-temperature error, and calibration of the probability that target yield reaches y*. A mechanism claim requires better held-out forecasts than both thermodynamics-only and diffusion-only ablations, with uncertainty intervals that cover replicated observations. A phase forecast is not a battery-capability result; only after the phase test passes should an independently measured electrochemical or other target property be added.

The first decisive next lemma is not a vast materials theorem: specify conditions under which the charge-aware flux/reaction model is thermodynamically consistent (mass and charge conservation, correct equilibrium stationary states) while retaining identifiable finite-time rates from the available observables. If this cannot be made mathematically well-posed, the proposed representation is defective. The decisive empirical gate is prospective, held-out transfer: if the full model does not beat equal-data baselines, or needs a bespoke fit for every precursor route, classify this as a useful system-specific simulator rather than a general synthesis capability.

## Prior work and novelty boundary

- [GNoME, “Scaling deep learning for materials discovery” (*Nature*, 2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06735-9): millions of predicted stable structures. This expands candidates; it does not provide their synthesis rates.
- [MatterGen, Zeni et al. (*Nature*, 2025)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-08628-5): property-conditioned structure generation and one experimental property-target demonstration. This makes inverse crystal generation itself an incumbent.
- [SynthNN, Antoniuk et al. (*npj Computational Materials*, 2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41524-023-01114-4): composition-based synthesizability classification. The output is not a finite-time, protocol-conditioned yield forecast.
- [ARROWS³, Szymanski et al. (*Nature Communications*, 2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-023-42329-9): experimentally informed precursor selection and pathway learning; paper explicitly identifies diffusion/nucleation kinetics as future work.
- [A-Lab, Szymanski et al. (*Nature*, 2023)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06734-w) and its [2026 Author Correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09992-y): useful autonomous recipe and measurement work; the correction narrows the success/novelty claim and leaves four successes inconclusive.
- [Karan et al., “Ion correlations explain kinetic selectivity…” (*Nature Materials*, 2026)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-026-02596-5): the closest incumbent and the reason this candidate is only the redox/microstructure transfer boundary.
- [Bianchini et al., “The interplay between thermodynamics and kinetics…” (*Nature Materials*, 2020)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41563-020-0688-6): time-resolved metastable pathways in Na₀.₆₇CoO₂ provide the initial redox-active challenge case.
- [Szymanski et al., “Quantifying the regime of thermodynamic control…” (*Science Advances*, 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adp3309): 37 reactant pairs; a ≥60 meV/atom driving-force margin predicts initial products in the thermodynamically controlled regime, while the Materials Project analysis places only about 15% of possible reactions there. This quantifies why a kinetics-aware model must add something beyond hull ranking.

**Final assessment:** pursue only the blind transfer pilot. The near-term falsifiable claim is that a charge-aware, measured-microstructure transport model improves held-out phase-path/yield prediction in a redox-active system over thermodynamics-only and fixed-valence transport baselines. A new universal synthesis principle, commercial material, or civilization-scale effect has not been established.
